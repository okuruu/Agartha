# **Chapter 1**

The first thing he felt was *heat*.  
 It burned through his skin like punishment from a god he could no longer name.

When his eyes opened, the sun above him was merciless — an unending white fire that scoured the horizon. Sand stretched in every direction, a golden ocean without mercy, and he was its lone castaway.

He couldn’t remember his name.  
He couldn’t remember *anything*.

Blood — his own — had dried into a dark crust over his chest and arms. His clothes, if they could still be called that, hung in tatters. Each breath was a battle, each step a defiance of something greater than death. He didn’t know if he was running *from* something or *toward* it — only that standing still felt like surrender.

Hours, or perhaps days, passed beneath the burning sky. The world shimmered with heat and silence, until the silence broke. The faint whistle of wind carried something unnatural — a whisper, too deliberate to be the desert’s own voice. He turned, but nothing was there.

And then, in the distance, he saw it — a dark wound carved into the dunes.

A cave.

He staggered toward it, dragging his body through the sand. Inside, the temperature dropped sharply, a relief so sudden it almost made him collapse. The air was old, filled with the scent of rust and forgotten ages.

There were signs of life here once — the remnants of a world long buried.  
Ancient tools, their edges dulled by time.  
A strip of faded cloth, its colors ghostly and thin.  
Someone had lived, and died, here.

Deeper in, he found the impossible.

A pool of water — pristine, untouched, gleaming under the dim light that filtered through cracks in the ceiling. The surface was too still, too perfect, like glass that reflected a world not quite this one.

Thirst overcame reason.  
He drank, then washed the blood and sand from his body. The pain dulled, but his wounds did not heal. The water cooled his flesh, yet left a strange pulse beneath his skin — as if something had woken *inside* him.

When he finally turned to leave… the cave was gone.  
Behind him stretched nothing but unbroken desert.

He stared in disbelief.  
Had he walked in circles? Hallucinated? The air shimmered with heat again, and then — a sound. A low growl, distant but rising, vibrating through the sand.

From the horizon, a shape crawled closer — too large, too fluid to belong to any beast he knew. Its body was wrong, shifting between flesh and shadow, its eyes like molten glass.

He ran.  
It followed.

When it struck, it was silent — no roar, no warning. Claws tore through the air, and pain exploded through his shoulder. He swung back wildly, his hand finding a jagged bone half-buried in the sand, and drove it into the creature’s throat.

It howled — a sound that wasn’t sound at all, more like the world *remembering* pain.

Then, as suddenly as it came, it vanished — dissolving into heat and dust.

He looked down and saw his arm — gone, severed above the elbow. Blood poured freely, staining the sand crimson.

He fell to his knees.

The sky blurred above him, a molten halo. His vision dimmed. Somewhere in the haze, a voice — neither male nor female — echoed faintly in his mind.

“You are not the first to wake… and you will not be the last.”

He collapsed, the desert swallowing his blood and his consciousness alike.  
The last thing he saw before darkness took him was the faint glimmer of light on the horizon — like the reflection of water where none should exist.

And then, silence.

# **Chapter 2**

He woke to silence again.  
No wind. No sun. Only the weight of his own breath echoing in the hollow between life and death.

The desert was colder now — the kind of cold that gnawed through the bones. His body lay half-buried in the sand, caked with dried blood that wasn’t entirely his own. For a long moment, he didn’t move. He simply breathed, letting the pain remind him that he still existed.

Then the ache came — sharp, deliberate, alive.

He looked to his arm. The wound that should have killed him had closed, but not cleanly. Flesh had sealed over the torn stump, leaving behind a line of thumb-sized, darkened spots beneath the skin. They pulsed faintly, like something trying to *breathe* from beneath.

He touched them once.  
They shuddered in response.

A jolt of pain flared through his body — quick, electric, and gone — leaving a whisper in its wake. It wasn’t sound, yet it filled his head.

A voice without words.  
A memory that wasn’t his.

He recoiled. The desert was too empty to answer him, too silent to explain.

He tried to remember — anything, anyone. A name. A face. But the emptiness inside him was perfect, merciless. He was a hollow vessel, filled with echoes of a life erased.

So he walked.

The dunes shifted endlessly beneath his feet until the land changed — rising into broken stone and shadow. Half-buried pillars jutted from the sand like the ribs of a fallen god. Crumbling arches framed fragments of what once might have been a city.

He stepped among the ruins, the sound of his breathing swallowed by stillness. Ancient murals clung to the walls, their colors faded but their message uncomfortably clear.

One showed a man — tall, bare-chested, marked by strange symbols along his arm. His face was indistinct, but something about the shape of his jaw, the curve of his shoulders — it mirrored *him*.

Beside that man was the creature — the same nightmare that had torn through him in the desert. Its form was twisted and divine, half-beast, half-shadow.   
Yet in the painting, the man did not fight it.  
He stood *with* it.

His chest tightened.

Was that him?  
Had he once been master to the monster that hunted him now?

He stumbled back, dust rising in thin ghosts around his feet. His pulse quickened, echoing with that same low vibration beneath his skin — the whisper returning, more insistent now, like a language his body almost understood.

Night fell swiftly, swallowing the ruins in deep indigo. The wind returned — howling low through the broken stone. He found shelter in what remained of a temple’s archway, its roof half-collapsed, its altar worn to smoothness by centuries of sand.

He curled against the cold, every tremor of his body shaking loose another fragment of fear. His eyelids grew heavy. The world outside the ruin blurred into thunder and sand.

And then — light.

A flash split the horizon. Lightning carved the night open, and for a heartbeat, the world was bright again. In that brightness stood *her* — a woman in white, unmoving, her face shadowed by the storm.

She did not speak. She did not move. But the way the wind bent around her made her presence undeniable.

The light vanished.  
So did she.

He blinked into the dark, his breath unsteady.  
Had she been real? Or just another mirage, born from fever and pain?

The whisper under his skin stirred once more — gentler this time, almost a murmur of reassurance or warning. He couldn’t tell which.

“Find me…”

The voice was softer than the wind, but it burned itself into his mind all the same.

And beneath the storm’s distant rumble, the ruins seemed to exhale — as if the ancient stones themselves remembered his name, even if he did not.

# **Chapter 3**

The world drifted in and out of focus.  
Time had lost its shape.

He walked because stopping meant dying, and dying — though tempting — felt unfinished somehow, like closing a book before knowing the ending.

His feet moved without thought, his body an automaton driven by something older than will. The desert stretched around him in endless waves of colorless dust. Above, the sun bled faintly through a veil of sandstorm clouds, its light distorted, unnatural — a red that promised nothing but ruin.

Sometimes, he thought he saw *footprints*.

They appeared just ahead of him — small, delicate, pressed into the sand as though left moments ago. He followed them instinctively, his heart stuttering with a hope he didn’t understand. But each time he stepped forward, the prints vanished, swallowed by the wind as if mocking him.

Still, he chased them.

The air grew heavier. Sound began to change — the wind no longer whistled but *spoke*. Whispers curled through the dunes, forming half-words that dissolved when he tried to listen.

“...you were…”  
“...left behind…”  
“...find her…”

He turned sharply, expecting to see someone behind him, but there was only the horizon, rolling and cruel. The voices faded into laughter — his laughter? — echoing back at him from nowhere.

His mind began to fracture under the heat and the silence.

Images burst unbidden behind his eyes. Screams. The sound of metal clashing. A woman crying his name. A hand reaching out from the fire, her face blurred by light and ash.

He fell to his knees, gripping his head as pain split through his skull.

“Who am I?”  
The words tore out of him like a wound reopening. The desert gave no answer. Only the wind continued its cruel song.

Storms began to form again on the horizon — violent and red, flashing with lightning that carved the sky like a blade. He stumbled forward, vision dimming, each breath a struggle. His body trembled with exhaustion and fever.

The whisper beneath his skin throbbed painfully, spreading warmth through his veins — not healing, but *remembering*. It was as if his blood itself was calling something forgotten, something that shouldn’t wake.

The world around him blurred — sand melting into light, light bending into shadow. He could no longer tell what was real.

And then he saw *her*.

At first, just a silhouette.  
A figure of white standing at the edge of the horizon, unmoving, serene amid the storm.

Her hair drifted with the wind, glinting faintly under the flashes of lightning. She faced away from him, her back turned as if she hadn’t noticed — or didn’t care — that he was there.

He tried to call out, but his voice failed him. Only a rasp escaped his throat.

The sky cracked open above them, pouring red light over the dunes. The storm howled louder, tearing at his clothes, but the woman in white did not move.

He stumbled toward her, one step, two — then collapsed.

The sand rose to meet him.

He caught one last glimpse of her — the faint tilt of her head, the fall of her hair across her shoulder — and then the world folded inward.

Darkness rushed in, absolute and final.

Yet as his vision faded, he thought he heard her speak — a voice soft and distant, threading through the storm.

“You were never meant to forget.”

And then, silence.  
Only the wind remained, sweeping away his footprints as though he had never existed.

# **Chapter 4**

He awoke to the sound of *water*.

Not the howling of the wind, not the hiss of sand, but the murmur of a stream trickling somewhere nearby — gentle, constant, impossibly real. His eyelids fluttered. Light pressed against them, soft and golden. The air was cool, heavy with the scent of grass and earth.

When he breathed, it hurt — not with the agony of the desert’s heat, but the ache of a body remembering it had survived.

He did not know that, days earlier, a passing caravan had found him lying near the desert’s edge — a broken figure half-buried in crimson sand. They thought him dead at first. His skin was pale, his pulse faint, his body limp as though the life had long since left it. But one of the travelers, a woman with calloused hands and eyes sharp as glass, had felt the faintest tremor under his ribs.

“He breathes,” she had whispered. “Barely.”

So they had carried him with them — through nights of sandstorm and days of blistering light — until at last, the dunes gave way to green.

The village that welcomed them was a quiet miracle.

Nestled between low hills and silver streams, it shimmered like an oasis untouched by time. Wildflowers clung to the riverbanks. Tall trees arched above narrow paths, their leaves whispering secrets with every gust of wind. The air smelled of rain and herbs, and the distant chime of bells marked the passing of hours that no longer belonged to the desert’s cruel rhythm.

Here, people *laughed*.

Children chased each other along the stream. An old man carved small wooden charms under the shade of a willow. Women hung herbs from their porches, their laughter mingling with the hum of bees. Life moved slowly, peacefully — as if the world beyond these forests had long ceased to exist.

When the caravan arrived, the villagers gathered around the strange, unconscious man they carried. His clothes were scorched, his skin marred by wounds that defied reason. Yet beneath the dirt and blood, something about him felt *ancient*.

They laid him in the care of the local apothecary — a humble building draped in ivy, its walls lined with bottles that caught the sunlight like gems. The scent of crushed mint and dried lavender filled the air.

The apothecary herself worked quietly, her hands practiced and calm. She cleaned his wounds, changed his bandages, whispered small prayers under her breath. The villagers spoke of him in hushed tones — a wanderer, perhaps cursed, perhaps chosen — but none dared disturb the stillness that seemed to surround him.

Days passed.  
His body remained still. His chest rose and fell like the faint pulse of dying embers.

Outside, the village thrived. The river sang. The forest breathed. It was a world alive with color and sound — a world that *should* have felt like peace.

But to those who lingered near his bedside, there was something else.  
At night, when the lamps burned low, they swore they saw faint light beneath his skin — a pulse like liquid fire running through his veins, too slow to be natural, too alive to be death.

The apothecary, watching over him in silence, reached out once — fingertips brushing his wrist — and felt the faintest tremor.

It wasn’t a heartbeat.  
It was something older, deeper.  
Something that did not belong to this place.

Outside, the forest whispered in its sleep. The moon rose, washing the leaves in silver. The village was peaceful — almost sacred.

And within that peace, the man dreamed — though not of the desert, nor of the woman in white, but of something far beneath them both.

A whisper waiting to wake.

# **Chapter 5**

The first thing Esdel felt was *light.*

It pressed softly against his eyelids, warm and golden — the kind of light that didn’t burn, but healed. He breathed in, and the air tasted of earth and herbs, damp wood and sunlight. Somewhere nearby, water trickled gently, and a quiet hum — a woman’s hum — wove through the air like a lullaby.

He blinked, his eyes adjusting to the pale ceiling above him. Wooden beams stretched overhead, woven with dried vines and hanging herbs. A faint breeze drifted through the open window, carrying with it the sound of leaves rustling and birds singing outside.

For the first time in what felt like eternity, the world was *still.*

He turned his head — and froze.

His *arm.*

Whole. Real.

The memory of the creature’s bite, the unbearable pain, the heat of blood pouring into the sand — it all came rushing back like a fever. He stared in disbelief, flexing his fingers. The flesh looked unbroken, perfectly normal, save for a faint pattern of dark, thumb-sized marks running from his elbow to his wrist. They pulsed once — faintly, rhythmically — before fading back into stillness.

His stomach twisted. “What…?”

A voice answered from the corner of the room, calm and steady.

“Ah. You’re awake.”

An elderly woman stepped into the light. Her silver hair was braided simply, her hands folded in front of her apron. Her eyes — soft green, touched with wisdom and patience — studied him with quiet care.

“You’ve been asleep for three days,” she said. “The caravan that found you left you here in our care. You’re safe now, child.”

Esdel’s voice came rough, uncertain. “My… arm. It was gone. I saw it— I *felt* it gone.”

The woman tilted her head slightly, her expression calm but puzzled. “Gone? No, no. When you arrived, your body was whole. Weak and feverish, yes, but no wounds of that kind. Only exhaustion and starvation.”

“That can’t be right,” he whispered, clutching the arm as if it might tear itself away again. “I remember losing it. The pain… the blood…”

The woman’s expression softened. “Sometimes, when the mind is pushed past its limits, it weaves dreams so vivid they seem real. Perhaps the desert played tricks on you.”

Esdel looked away, the words echoing hollowly in his head. *Dreams.* But the memory of that agony, the tearing sound — it hadn’t felt like a dream.

Before he could speak again, another voice entered — lighter, curious, full of life.

“Grandmother, is he finally awake?”

A young woman stepped in, her arms full of freshly picked herbs. Her hair was dark brown and loose, tied back with a simple cloth. The sunlight caught the green of her eyes, bright as spring leaves.

She smiled at him, soft and relieved. “You had us worried,” she said. “I thought you might never wake.”

The older woman gave a small chuckle. “This is my granddaughter, Lyora Ardynne Aurelisse. I’m Arwen Lysandra Aurelisse.”

Esdel looked between them — the calm, weathered grace of the grandmother and the warm, curious glow of the granddaughter — and for a moment, he forgot his fear.

“Esdel,” he murmured. “My name is Esdel.” The name rose from some deep well inside him — the only fragment of truth that hadn’t slipped away.

Lyora repeated it softly, as though testing its sound. “Esdel… That’s a strong name.”

He gave a faint, tired smile. “It’s all I remember.”

Arwen nodded gently. “Then it’s a good start. You’re in the village of **Edenridge**, Esdel — a small place, tucked at the edge of the forest. You can rest here until your strength returns.”

He glanced toward the open window. Outside, sunlight spilled across cobblestone paths. Villagers passed by, their laughter mingling with the sound of flowing water. A dog barked somewhere near the river. It felt… unreal, after so much death and sand.

“This place,” he murmured. “It’s too peaceful.”

Arwen smiled faintly. “Peace is a fragile thing. You should hold on to it while you can.”

Lyora began arranging herbs in small clay jars, the air filling with scents of mint and rosemary. “You can stay here as long as you need,” she said, not looking up. “Grandmother doesn’t turn anyone away. Not even men who wander in from the desert claiming they lost an arm.”

Her teasing tone made him almost laugh — *almost.*

For the first time in what felt like lifetimes, Esdel’s heartbeat slowed. The tension in his body began to ease, and the world, for the briefest moment, felt kind.

But as he let his head sink back against the pillow, his gaze drifted to his arm again.

The dark spots beneath his skin shimmered faintly, pulsing once before fading.

Neither Arwen nor Lyora noticed.

And in the quiet hum of the apothecary’s clinic, beneath the smell of herbs and sunlit peace, something within Esdel whispered faintly — not in words, but in memory.

“You were never meant to heal.”

# **Chapter 6**

The days in **Edenridge** passed like ripples across still water — slow, gentle, and quietly alive.

For the first time since awakening beneath the desert sun, Esdel began to understand what it meant to *rest.*

Mornings came soft and golden. The air smelled of dew and crushed mint. He would wake to the sound of Lyora grinding herbs in a wooden bowl, her humming a steady rhythm beneath the morning light. Birds sang outside the open windows, and the faint laughter of villagers drifted through the cobblestone paths.

His body healed quickly — almost too quickly. The strength in his limbs returned within days, though the faint spots on his arm remained, pulsing faintly under his skin whenever the light touched them. He hid them beneath his sleeve. He didn’t know why.

Arwen said little about his recovery, only offering simple wisdom in her calm, deliberate tone. “The body remembers pain long after the wound closes,” she said once, while tying a bandage around his wrist though he no longer needed it. “Sometimes, it’s the memory we must tend to — not the flesh.”

And so he worked.

He swept the apothecary’s floor, fetched water from the river, and helped hang bundles of dried herbs from the rafters. The scent of rosemary and sage clung to his clothes, seeping into his skin until it became familiar.

Lyora would often watch him with an amused smile, her hands always busy — sorting, crushing, measuring. “You’re too serious for someone just learning the difference between thyme and basil,” she’d tease, eyes glinting.

Esdel would glance up, pretending to frown. “If I mix the wrong one, someone might die.”

“That’s only if you *feed* them the wrong one,” she countered, laughter light and clean. “You’re just sweeping, Esdel.”

Their banter became its own rhythm — quiet, unspoken, but comforting. She had a way of drawing small smiles from him, like sunlight coaxing life from the soil.

In the evenings, when the work was done, he would sit by the stream just beyond the apothecary’s garden. The water was clear and cold, running over smooth stones that caught the light like fragments of glass. The villagers passed by with baskets of fruit or bundles of firewood, always greeting him with polite warmth. No one asked where he had come from. No one seemed to care.

Life here moved at a pace that felt almost sacred — steady, deliberate, untouched by fear or haste. People rose with the dawn and rested by dusk. They shared their harvests, tended to each other’s wounds, and spoke with reverence about the quiet gifts of the land.

To Esdel, it felt like another world entirely — one that couldn’t possibly exist beyond the desert’s edge.

One night, he asked Arwen quietly, “How long have you lived here?”

She looked up from her mortar, her hands never pausing. “Long enough to know that peace is a rare visitor,” she said. “And rarer still to be welcomed.”

When she finally declared him fully recovered, the decision came not as ceremony but as a simple truth.

“You’re strong again,” she said, setting aside her tools. “Stronger than when you arrived. You may stay here as long as you wish, Esdel — or leave when the road calls you back. The door will remain open.”

For a long moment, he couldn’t speak. The words caught somewhere in his chest, heavy with emotion he didn’t know how to name. Gratitude, perhaps. Or guilt — for accepting warmth he hadn’t earned.

He bowed his head. “Thank you,” he said quietly. “I… don’t know where else I could go.”

Arwen smiled faintly, the lines around her eyes softening. “Then perhaps that’s your answer.”

Lyora appeared in the doorway, sunlight pooling behind her. She was smiling again — that same patient, teasing warmth he was beginning to rely on.

“Come on,” she said. “If you’re truly recovered, you can help me replant the garden. The roots won’t wait for indecision.”

Esdel met her gaze — the glimmer of light in her green eyes — and, for the first time since his awakening, he felt something shift inside him.

Something *alive.*

The world outside the apothecary seemed to hum softly, the leaves whispering in the wind. He could almost believe this place was untouched by the cruelty of time — a hidden corner of the world where broken things were allowed to breathe.

But beneath the peace, a pulse still echoed faintly under his skin.  
A whisper, patient and waiting.

“Breathe while you can.”

# **Chapter 7**

Days folded quietly into one another, weaving themselves into something that almost resembled a life.

Esdel had become part of **Edenridge’s** rhythm — not quite one of them, but no longer a stranger. The villagers would greet him with a nod, sometimes a smile, and always a word of thanks when he passed carrying crates of vegetables, repairing a fence, or hauling buckets of river water to the communal garden.

He worked hard, though no one ever asked him to. It gave him purpose — something solid to hold onto in a world that felt too soft, too forgiving.

He fixed broken stalls, mended tools, helped children chase their goats back into pens. In time, the villagers began to rely on him in small, quiet ways — a gesture here, a wave there. They called him *the man who never complains.* He found the title strangely fitting, though it carried a loneliness he couldn’t name.

Each evening, he returned to the apothecary. The place had become his quiet refuge — its wooden walls lined with drying herbs and hanging flowers, the scent of lavender and moss lingering in the air.

Arwen always greeted him with the same gentle nod, offering tea without a word. Lyora would follow soon after, setting plates on the low table with the casual ease of someone who had never known war or loss. They’d eat together, sometimes in conversation, sometimes in silence.

Lyora spoke most often — about small things that filled the world with color.  
 “The river’s higher this week,” she’d remark one evening, stirring her soup. “The children say there’s a fish in it with golden scales — they’re trying to catch it with crumbs of bread.”  
Another time, she spoke of her dreams — strange, luminous ones. “I keep seeing lights floating in the forest,” she said, resting her chin on her hand. “They hum like voices, soft and far away.”

Esdel would listen quietly, content to let her words fill the air. He rarely spoke of himself — there was little to tell, and less he could remember. But Lyora never pressed. She had a gift for making silence feel safe, not suffocating.

Sometimes, when she laughed — that light, unguarded sound — something inside him would stir, faint and unfamiliar. It frightened him more than any wound ever had.

Then the weeks slipped by, quicker than he expected.

---

At the end of the month came the **Devotional Gathering** — a night the villagers awaited all year.

It began at dusk. The entire settlement gathered by the riverbank, holding slender wooden lanterns shaped like flowers. The air shimmered with anticipation, and the scent of burning oil mingled with the sweetness of blooming lilies.

Esdel watched from the edge of the crowd, uncertain. He didn’t understand what they were celebrating — only that the night felt sacred, somehow.

Lyora found him standing alone and offered him a lantern with a grin.  
“You’re supposed to light one,” she said. “For gratitude. Or hope. Or… something that still hurts.”

He hesitated. “I don’t know what I should wish for.”

“You don’t need to wish,” she said, kneeling beside him to help strike the flint. “Just let it go.”

The flame caught weakly, flickering as if resisting life. Esdel frowned, trying to shield it from the wind, only to nearly drop the lantern into the river. Lyora laughed — a sound that scattered the night’s stillness like falling petals.

“Here,” she said, steadying his hands with hers. “You’re trying too hard.”

He looked at her, her face illuminated by the soft glow of the lantern’s light — warm, golden, impossibly alive.

“It’s beautiful,” she whispered, eyes on the drifting light. “When you stop trying to understand it.”

The lantern floated away, joining dozens of others as they glided across the dark water. For a moment, the river looked like the sky — a mirror filled with stars that only existed here, in this small forgotten village.

Esdel’s gaze lingered on the glow as it faded downstream. Something inside him ached — not from pain, but from the quiet realization that he *felt* again.

Warmth. Connection. Fragile, but real.

When he looked back at Lyora, she was still smiling — softly, unknowingly — and he thought, perhaps for the first time since his awakening, that not everything lost must be found.

But as the crowd’s laughter faded into the sound of flowing water, he noticed it again — a faint pulse beneath his skin, echoing like a whisper too quiet to name.  
The dark spots on his arm throbbed once, faintly, in rhythm with the river’s current.

He flexed his fingers, hiding the tremor, and forced a smile when Lyora turned to speak.

For now, he decided, it didn’t matter.

For now… he could breathe.

# **Chapter 8**

The days in **Edenridge** had become a seamless thread — woven with the rhythm of simple things.

Mornings filled with the sound of roosters and laughter. Midday sun pouring over green fields. Evenings painted with amber light through the apothecary’s windows, the scent of drying herbs wrapping the air like memory.

Esdel worked as always — steady, quiet, dependable. The villagers no longer treated him as an outsider. Children waved when he passed; merchants offered him fruit in thanks. Lyora teased that he was becoming “boringly reliable,” and he pretended to take offense.

Peace had a strange texture — unfamiliar, fragile, yet impossibly precious.

---

It was during one of those ordinary days that the rain came without warning.

A sudden downpour swept through the village — hard, silver rain slashing across rooftops and paths. Esdel and Lyora were returning from the eastern fields when the storm broke. Laughing breathlessly, they ran for cover, slipping beneath the overhang of an old storehouse at the edge of the village.

The sound was deafening — the rhythmic drumming of rain against wood, the hiss of wind through the eaves. They stood close, their breaths misting in the chill. Lyora shook the droplets from her hair, strands clinging to her cheeks.

“Rain never used to come this early,” she said, watching the sheets of water blur the world outside. “It’s the mountain’s doing, I think. The air’s changing.”

Esdel glanced at her — at the way she spoke to the world as though it could listen. “Do you like it?” he asked.

“The rain?” She smiled faintly. “I think it makes everything honest. You can’t hide anything when the sky’s crying.”

He didn’t know how to respond to that.

For a long while, they simply listened to the storm. The smell of wet earth filled the air. Somewhere nearby, a bell clanged faintly in the wind.

Lyora’s voice broke the silence again, softer this time. “When I was little, I thought the ocean must sound like this,” she said. “Like rain that never ends. My parents used to tell me stories about it — said it stretched farther than the eye could see.”

Her gaze drifted outward, unfocused, her expression caught between longing and loss.

Esdel studied her quietly. “You’ve never seen it?”

She shook her head. “No. Edenridge is all I’ve ever known. But I’d like to, one day. To stand where the land ends and just… let the world breathe around me.”

There was a pause — the kind that feels too delicate to break.

Without realizing it, Esdel smiled. Not the cautious, polite kind he’d learned to offer, but something unguarded — something real.

Lyora noticed. Her eyes softened with surprise, then warmth. “You should do that more often,” she said gently.

“Do what?”

“Smile like that. It suits you.”

He looked away, suddenly unsure what to do with his hands. “I didn’t realize I was.”

She laughed — a small, bright sound against the rain.

---

That night, the quiet returned.

Esdel lay awake in the small room Arwen had given him, listening to the soft patter of lingering drizzle outside. The day’s warmth still lingered faintly in his chest — a fragile comfort, like the last glow of a dying fire.

But sleep brought something else.

He dreamt of the desert again. The endless dunes, the burning wind, the taste of dust and iron. And in the distance — a pale figure, walking against the storm.

The **woman in white**.

Her face was blurred, but her presence filled the dream like gravity. She didn’t speak, yet her silence felt heavier than words. Around her, the air shimmered — not with heat, but memory.

When he tried to move toward her, the sand beneath his feet bled red. The scent of rain turned to rust.

Esdel jerked awake.

The room was still dark, his breath ragged, his skin slick with cold sweat. The faint light of dawn seeped through the window, and he could hear roosters calling across the village. Everything was quiet. Ordinary. Safe.

He sat up slowly, pressing a trembling hand to his chest.

“It was just a dream,” he whispered.

But deep down, he wasn’t sure he believed it.

Outside, the last drops of rain slid from the rooftops like tears.

And far beyond the forest, unseen by anyone in Edenridge, the wind shifted — carrying with it the scent of sand.

# **Chapter 9**

By the time the next moon rose over **Edenridge**, Esdel had become a part of its quiet pulse.

He woke with the villagers at dawn — to the crow of roosters, the soft ring of temple bells, the scent of morning bread drifting through the air. The days no longer blurred together; each one carried its own rhythm, its own shape.

The fences around the western fields had rotted from years of rain, and so Esdel took it upon himself to repair them. The work was simple, honest, and satisfying in a way he couldn’t explain. The hammer’s rhythm was steady, the sun warm on his back. The villagers often stopped by, offering cool water or small words of thanks before continuing their way.

Once, an old farmer patted his shoulder and said with a smile, “Never thought I’d see someone patch that fence properly. You’ve got good hands, boy.”

Good hands.  
He had forgotten what it felt like to be praised — not as a stranger or a survivor, but as a *man*.

---

When he wasn’t mending wood or hauling grain, Esdel helped carry supplies to the **river shrine**, a simple stone structure draped with flowers and ribbons. The villagers left offerings there — herbs, coins, even carved figures of birds and leaves.

Lyora joined him often, under the excuse of delivering medicines to the shrine keeper. She always lingered longer than her duties required.

Once, as he set down a crate of offerings, she knelt beside him and watched curiously as he sharpened a farmer’s old chisel.

“You work like someone who’s done this all his life,” she said.

He shrugged. “Maybe I have. I wouldn’t know.”

Her lips quirked into a grin. “You say that so calmly. I’d lose my mind not remembering who I was.”

“Maybe that’s why I haven’t,” he replied, half-smiling. “There’s nothing to lose if I never had it.”

She tilted her head, studying him. “That’s… sad.”

He meant to answer, but she laughed before he could — a bright, effortless sound that carried over the stream. “You talk like an old man, Esdel. You need more sun.”

He looked up at her then, watching how her laughter seemed to draw color into the world. The weight in his chest eased.

“I think I’ve had enough sun for a lifetime,” he said softly.

---

As weeks passed, small things began to change.  
When Esdel walked through the market square, vendors greeted him with familiarity.  
“Morning, stranger,” had become “Morning, friend.”  
Children ran to him with broken toys, asking him to fix them, their small hands smudged with dirt and hope.

Even Arwen, usually reserved, seemed quietly content watching him from the apothecary’s porch as he taught the children how to carve simple wooden animals or sharpen dull blades. She said nothing, but her faint smile spoke enough.

He didn’t notice the change at first — how easily his name passed between the villagers’ lips, how naturally he fit into their stories.

Once, while fetching water, he overheard two women by the well.  
“Esdel helped me repair the wheelbarrow,” one said.  
“Ah, that man’s a blessing,” replied the other. “I don’t remember when he arrived, but it feels like he’s always been here.”

He almost laughed at that. *Always been here.*  
If only it were true.

---

That evening, as twilight painted the sky violet, Esdel sat by the apothecary’s garden. Lyora was tending the herbs — kneeling among rows of green and white, her hands dusted with soil.

“You’ve been quiet lately,” she said without looking up.  
“I’ve been busy.”  
“You’re always busy.”  
He paused. “…Maybe I like it that way.”

She smiled knowingly. “You like feeling useful.”

He looked down at his hands — scarred, steady, alive.  
“I think I just like feeling real,” he murmured.

For a long while, neither spoke. Only the chirping of crickets filled the dusk.

Lyora finally said, “You are real, Esdel. Everyone here knows it. You’re part of this place now.”

He wanted to believe her. Truly.

And as the first stars appeared above Edenridge, he realized something quiet but undeniable:  
He was no longer drifting.

He belonged.

Or at least… he was starting to.

---

But far beyond the calm village — beyond the forest, where the river met the dry edge of the desert — the wind shifted again. The trees shivered though no storm touched them.

And if one listened closely, past the hum of cicadas and the rustle of leaves, there was a sound — faint and distant.

A whisper.

Calling his name.

# **Chapter 10**

The early summer air in **Edenridge** already hung heavy with heat. The fields to the east had begun to dry — their soil cracked and thirsty despite the river flowing just beyond the hill.

That was when **Reyn Challon**, the village engineer, arrived at the apothecary one morning — sleeves rolled up, boots caked in dust, and a wide grin beneath his copper beard.

“Arwen, I’m borrowing your helper,” he announced cheerfully, nodding toward Esdel. “I’ve got a problem the village needs fixed, and from what I hear, this man’s got a head for odd ideas.”

Arwen merely looked up from her mortar and smiled faintly. “Odd ideas have built more than they’ve broken, Reyn. Take him.”

And so, Esdel followed.

---

The project was simple in purpose, impossible in practice — or so Reyn believed.

The fields on Edenridge’s outskirts were dying. The ground there was higher than the main irrigation lines, and the village’s small enchantment wells could no longer sustain the flow uphill. 

“We’ve always used water-guiding charms,” Reyn explained as they walked. “But the last enchanter moved away three winters ago. The river doesn’t listen anymore.”

Esdel listened silently, scanning the terrain — the slope of the land, the way the river curved lazily through reeds. His mind worked like clockwork — measuring distance, imagining flow.

“Magic,” he said after a long pause, “isn’t the only way water moves.”

Reyn blinked, caught off guard. “You have something else in mind?”

“Bamboo,” Esdel said simply, kneeling by the riverbank. He picked up a long, hollow stalk from a discarded pile nearby and ran his fingers along its smooth surface. “It grows in abundance, and it’s hollow. If we cut and join it properly, we can make a channel system to redirect flow. The pressure will take care of the rest.”

Reyn frowned, trying to picture it. “You’re saying… to *carry* the river through these? Without enchantments?”

Esdel nodded. “Water doesn’t need permission to move. It only needs a path.”

The engineer stared at him, incredulous at first — then thoughtful. “Well,” he said finally, scratching his beard. “I’ve never met a man who talks like a scholar and works like an assistant apothecary. Let’s see if your path holds.”

---

They worked for days under the sun.

Together, they split bamboo stalks, burned their interiors clean, and connected them with resin and cloth binding. Reyn’s apprentices watched curiously, whispering among themselves as Esdel calculated angles and adjusted inclines by hand — his movements methodical, his reasoning effortless.

At first, the engineer doubted. Then, he began to listen.

There was something hypnotic about Esdel’s focus — the way he knelt by the river, tracing its edge with his hand, speaking softly as if it might answer. He didn’t rush. He didn’t guess. He *understood.*

And when at last they opened the flow, the village gathered to watch.

The water surged forward — clean and steady — running through the bamboo channels like veins of living glass. It curved around the slope, reaching the farthest fields where the soil had long turned to dust. The earth drank eagerly, darkening with life once more.

No spells. No sigils. Just wood, gravity, and thought.

For a moment, no one spoke.

Then Reyn let out a stunned laugh, hands on his hips. “By the gods… it *works.* It actually works!”

The villagers cheered, rushing to the edges of the fields as the first shoots of green broke through the soil. Some called it a miracle. Others whispered it was luck.

Reyn only stared at the bamboo channels, his voice low, awed.  
“So, it can be done without magic…”

Esdel wiped the sweat from his brow, blinking against the sun. He didn’t understand their wonder. To him, it had been simple. Logical. The kind of solution that should have always existed.

He smiled faintly, though his eyes lingered on the water’s surface — watching as it rippled in strange, concentric circles.

For just a heartbeat, his reflection wavered — his face shifting into something unfamiliar, his eyes a shade too bright.

He blinked, and it was gone.

---

That evening, Reyn clapped him on the back as they walked home. “You’re something else, Esdel. I don’t know what place you came from, but it’s one I’d like to see.”

Esdel chuckled softly, though his mind was far away. “I’m not sure it’s a place worth seeing.”

Reyn arched a brow. “Maybe not. But whatever it taught you — keep it close. This village needs more men who think like you.”

The words lingered long after they parted ways.

As Esdel passed by the stream under the fading light, the air grew oddly still. The bamboo channels creaked softly in the wind, carrying water that glowed faintly under the moon.

And somewhere beneath that gentle sound — so soft it might’ve been imagined — a voice whispered through the current:

*“You were never meant to stay.”*

Esdel turned sharply, but there was no one there.

Only the sound of water…  
flowing endlessly through the bamboo veins he had built.

# **Chapter 11**

A week passed since the river had been tamed.

Morning mist still hung over the fields when Esdel and Reyn stood side by side, watching the water run clean through the bamboo lines they had built. The system hummed softly, alive yet unenchanted — its rhythm steady as breath.

For the farmers, it was nothing short of a miracle.

Where there had once been dry, cracked soil, green shoots now rose in perfect rows. The elders gathered near the banks, murmuring prayers of thanks, though none could quite agree on *whom* to thank — the gods, the river, or the quiet man who had simply shown it a path.

That afternoon, the **Village Chief**, an elderly man with silver hair tied neatly behind his head, called for a small gathering in the square. The villagers came bearing fruits, rice cakes, and laughter.

Reyn was the first to speak, voice bright with pride.

“Never thought I’d see water climb a hill without a spell,” he said, grinning toward Esdel. “But this man here—he made the river listen. All I did was follow his lead.”

A ripple of cheers followed.

The Chief nodded deeply, resting his hand on Esdel’s shoulder.  
“You’ve given more than a solution, Esdel. You’ve given us trust again — in our own hands.”

Esdel said nothing for a moment. He wasn’t used to being thanked — not like this.

He bowed his head slightly. “I only did what anyone could have done.”

“Maybe,” the Chief chuckled, “but no one *did* — until you came.”

Laughter rippled again, light as wind through wheat.

---

From that day onward, more people in the village began to change around him.

The farmers waved when he passed — “Morning, Esdel!” — and the children ran to greet him, their hands muddy from play. Some offered him roasted chestnuts, others small trinkets carved from wood. At first, he tried to refuse, but they insisted until his pockets were full.

The merchants at the market began asking for his thoughts — on tools, measurements, small ways to make life easier. It was the kind of respect that required no ceremony, no worship — only trust built quietly, day after day.

And every time his name was spoken, **Lyora** would smile.

At the apothecary, she’d beam as villagers came to thank him, setting down their baskets of herbs or jars of honey as gifts.

“You’ve become the village’s favorite topic,” she teased one afternoon, setting a cup of tea beside him. “Even Grandmother mentioned your water pipes when she spoke to the shrine-keeper. Said they might last longer than the old spells.”

Esdel chuckled softly, brushing dust from his hands. “If they do, it’s only because nature prefers order.”

Lyora tilted her head, eyes bright with that quiet curiosity that always caught him off guard. “Order,” she echoed. “You talk about things like they’re alive.”

“Sometimes they are,” he said with a faint smile. “You just have to listen long enough.”

She laughed, light and melodic — the kind of laugh that filled the room without effort. “Then maybe that’s your real gift. You make the world want to talk back.”

He blinked, unsure how to answer that. So he didn’t. Instead, he lifted his tea and let her words settle like warmth in his chest.

---

That night, the village glowed beneath a calm sky. Lanterns hung from doorframes, their light swaying with the wind. The bamboo channels gleamed faintly in the moonlight, threading through the village like veins of gold.

Esdel stood by the riverbank alone, watching his reflection ripple in the water’s slow current. The sound of laughter carried faintly from the distance — Lyora’s voice among them, soft and alive.

He hadn’t meant to stay. He hadn’t meant to belong.  
And yet, as he looked out over the fields — green and full, the village lights dancing like fireflies — something within him loosened.

For the first time since he could remember, the silence inside his chest didn’t feel hollow.

It felt… gentle.  
Like water beneath the bamboo.

# **Chapter 12**

The night came alive with color.

Lanterns of every hue swayed above the village square — red, amber, and pale green, their light trembling like captured stars. Music drifted on the air, the low hum of flutes and the steady rhythm of drums echoing through the forest edge.

It was the *Festival of Flow*, held once every decade — but this year, it carried a new meaning. The fields had not just survived the dry season; they had *thrived*. And all because of a few lines of hollowed bamboo and one quiet man who believed the world could be reasoned with.

The villagers gathered with joy that seemed to outshine the moon. Children ran barefoot, carrying lanterns shaped like fish and birds. The smell of roasted rice and sweet herbs filled the air. Laughter rolled like a tide — unrestrained, alive.

At the center of it all sat **Esdel** and **Reyn**, shoulder to shoulder on a wooden bench near the riverside.

Reyn lifted a clay cup, already a little red in the face. “To the man who solved a century-old drought with *bamboo*!” he announced loudly, voice booming over the chatter.

The crowd erupted into cheers.

Esdel tried to protest, but Reyn clapped a heavy hand on his back. “Don’t you dare say it was ‘simple reasoning,’” he teased. “Let a man be proud for once!”

Esdel could only smile, shaking his head as laughter bubbled around them. The sound was strange to him — not the brittle laughter of soldiers or merchants, but real, full-bodied joy. It moved through the people like a pulse.

When the Chief approached with a lantern in hand, the villagers quieted.

“Tonight,” the Chief said, voice warm with age, “we honor not magic, but wisdom — the kind that listens to the earth, not commands it.” He turned toward Esdel. “You’ve reminded us that the world still speaks, if we care enough to hear.”

The crowd bowed their heads, and Esdel, flustered, lifted his cup in return. “Then let’s keep listening,” he said softly.

The night deepened. Music played again. The river shimmered under the reflection of countless lanterns, drifting slowly downstream like fireflies on a silver road.

---

Later, when the laughter faded into quiet murmurs, **Lyora** found him by the water’s edge.

She carried two cups of herbal tea — steam curling into the cool air — and sat beside him on the old stone embankment. The festival sounds felt far away now, muffled by the rustle of bamboo and the rhythm of the current.

Esdel looked at her and smiled faintly. “You should be celebrating,” he said. “You’ve worked as much as anyone.”

“I am,” she replied, handing him a cup. “Just… differently.”

They sat in silence for a while. The water reflected their faces — blurred, shifting with every ripple. Somewhere downstream, children’s laughter still lingered, fading like an echo.

Lyora’s voice came soft. “Do you know what the villagers say about you?”

He shook his head.

“They say you were sent here by the river gods.” She smiled a little, as if embarrassed by the superstition. “That you were carried from the desert as a sign that our village hadn’t been forgotten.”

Esdel looked down at his hands, at the faint dark spots still marking his skin — small, round, and pulsing dimly in the lantern light. “I’m not sure the gods had anything to do with it.”

“I don’t know,” she said, watching the river. “Maybe they just wanted you to rest.”

He turned to her — and for a moment, neither spoke. The air between them was filled only by the hush of the current and the flicker of lanterns drifting away.

Then, softly, she said, “You’ve changed how this village lives.”

He smiled faintly. “I didn’t do it alone.”

Lyora hesitated. Her voice was almost a whisper. “You’ve changed how *we* live too.”

The words hung between them like a breath caught midair.

Esdel’s heart gave a small, unsteady beat. He looked away first, watching a lantern bump gently against the riverbank before drifting free again.

Neither spoke after that. The silence wasn’t awkward — it was full, fragile, alive.

As the night wind softened and the last of the lanterns floated toward the horizon, Esdel realized that something inside him — something long buried beneath sand and blood and pain — had begun to *move*.

Something gentle.  
Something dangerous.

# **Chapter 13**

For the first time in memory, the days began to feel whole.

Esdel rose with the morning bells, helped with the herbs, and greeted familiar faces along the winding paths. The scent of rain lingered in the air — a promise of coming storms — yet the village felt untouched by worry. It thrived like a secret garden tucked between the forest and the dunes.

Even the dark spots on his arm — those strange thumb-sized marks that once pulsed like something alive — had faded. Only pale traces remained now, like memories of wounds that no longer mattered.

He told himself that was enough.  
Peace, work, laughter — a rhythm he could understand.

---

Much of his time was spent with **Reyn Challon**, the engineer. Their friendship had grown quietly, built from sawdust, sketches, and sweat.

Reyn’s workshop stood near the southern river bend, where the hum of bamboo water channels blended with the soft grind of gears and wooden pulleys. Inside, every surface was cluttered — half-built contraptions, scraps of parchment, and the faint smell of oil and rain-soaked timber.

“Hold that beam steady,” Reyn muttered, squinting at a wooden frame.

Esdel obeyed, pressing his shoulder against the post until it fit snugly into place. “You could make this lighter if you adjust the tension here,” he said, tapping the joint.

Reyn grinned. “You and your cursed logic. Fine, show me.”

Together they worked — rebalancing, measuring, testing. Their hands moved with quiet precision, both men absorbed in the language of creation. By midday, they stood before a small prototype water wheel, its blades catching the river’s edge, spinning with a rhythmic hum.

Reyn folded his arms, smiling with satisfaction. “You realize this means less work for half the farmers?”

Esdel shrugged. “Then they can rest more.”

Reyn chuckled. “Spoken like a man who doesn’t own a field.”

---

Sometimes, **Lyora** came by with lunch.

Her arrival always felt like the first breeze before rain — light, refreshing, inevitable. She’d step into the workshop carrying a basket of bread and stew, pretending it was merely *for convenience’s sake.*

“You two work harder than the priests on prayer day,” she teased one afternoon, setting the basket on a cluttered table.

Reyn laughed. “That’s because our miracles actually hold water.”

Lyora rolled her eyes, passing Esdel a cup of broth. “And what about yours, mister miracle worker? How’s the arm?”

Esdel flexed it absentmindedly. “Fine. Almost forget it was ever gone.”

“Good,” she said softly — and for a moment, her smile faltered, replaced by something gentler, almost afraid.

Reyn noticed it, of course. The man was observant in ways he didn’t often show. As Lyora chatted about the weather or the color of the river at dawn, Reyn kept smirking behind his mug, his eyes flicking between them like he was watching a story unfold.

When Lyora finally stood to leave, Esdel rose as well. “I’ll walk you to the path,” he offered, voice even.

She waved him off. “No need. I know the way.”

Still, she paused at the door, brushing a loose strand of hair from her face. “Don’t forget to eat before the rain comes.”

Then she smiled — that small, unguarded smile that reached her eyes — and turned away.

Reyn waited until she disappeared down the trail before whistling low.

“You could at least *pretend* not to care,” he teased.

Esdel frowned. “What are you talking about?”

Reyn leaned back on his stool, grinning. “The way you look at her when she leaves — like you’re afraid the wind might take her away.”

Esdel said nothing. He focused on tightening a bolt that didn’t need tightening.

Reyn chuckled, shaking his head. “You’re a strange one, Esdel. Build water systems out of bamboo, fix a village without magic… but you can’t fix your own heartbeat.”

Esdel ignored him, but a faint warmth crept up his neck nonetheless.

Outside, the wind picked up, brushing through the bamboo grove with a soft, whistling sigh. The sky had turned a deeper shade of gray — not storm yet, but the promise of one.

He found himself glancing at the path where Lyora had walked away, her figure now long gone between the trees.

And though he couldn’t name it, something inside his chest drew tight — not pain, not fear… just *weight.*

The kind that comes when a man realizes he’s begun to want something fragile.

Something the world could easily take back.

# **Chapter 14**

The village thrived.

Each morning, sunlight poured over the rooftops like honey, glinting across the river where bamboo pipes whispered with flowing water. The air was thick with birdsong and the smell of earth after rain. Even the forest seemed to hum with quiet satisfaction, its leaves shimmering in the soft wind.

For a while, it felt as though nothing in the world could go wrong.

Esdel had become a fixture in the rhythm of village life — no longer a stranger, no longer a wanderer. The Chief often sought his advice for planning harvest rotations or repairing the irrigation lines. Reyn relied on him to inspect water wheels, measure pressure, and balance flow between the upper fields and the village core.

And when he walked through the square, people called his name with easy familiarity.  
“Morning, Esdel!”  
“Watch your step — the kids left their kites again!”  
“Chief says your idea saved us another day’s work!”

He’d answer with a quiet smile, still surprised at how easily laughter seemed to follow him now.

---

The world had become small, predictable, safe — but in that simplicity, Esdel found something he never thought he’d crave: *peace.*

Much of it came from **Lyora**.

She was the constant thread that tied each day together — bringing him small meals while he worked, teasing him about his “unromantic logic,” or sitting beside him during long meetings with the Chief, chin resting on her hand as she listened.

“Do you ever stop thinking?” she asked one afternoon, watching him sketch plans for a pulley system on parchment.

He looked up, brow slightly furrowed. “I don’t know how to stop.”

She laughed softly, tucking a strand of hair behind her ear. “Then it’s a good thing I’m here to remind you to breathe.”

And she did. Constantly.

When she visited the workshop, Reyn would give them both a knowing grin before conveniently finding excuses to leave — “I, uh, just remembered I left the measuring rod by the bridge.” He’d vanish, leaving them alone with the soft rustle of paper and the lazy hum of the river outside.

Sometimes they would just sit in silence, content to listen to the rhythm of tools, the distant voices of villagers, and the song of wind moving through bamboo.

---

But it was during the *evenings* that Esdel felt most human.

He and Lyora would meet in the apothecary’s garden at dusk, where rows of herbs glowed faintly in the fading light. The air smelled of rosemary and wet soil; fireflies drifted lazily between the flowers.

They’d sit on the old stone bench beside the pond, talking about everything and nothing — her dreams of traveling beyond the forest, her fascination with how the stars always returned to the same places each night.

Sometimes, her grandmother would glance at them from the clinic window, smiling quietly before drawing the curtain closed.

As the sky turned violet and the first stars began to bloom, Lyora would tilt her head back, eyes reflecting the fading light.  
“Do you ever think about what came before you came here?” she asked once.

Esdel hesitated. “Sometimes.”

“And?”

He looked down at his hands, at the faint scars that still lined his skin. “It feels like trying to remember a dream after waking. The pieces are there… but the meaning isn’t.”

Lyora smiled gently, her voice barely above a whisper. “Then maybe it’s better this way. Maybe you were meant to start over.”

He wanted to believe that. He wanted to believe this quiet life was his — not borrowed, not fragile, not a reprieve from something darker.

But some part of him — the part that still remembered the burning desert and the taste of blood — whispered that it couldn’t last. That peace, for him, was only ever *temporary.*

---

That night, as he walked home beneath the sound of crickets and distant laughter, Esdel paused by the river.

The water reflected the moon perfectly — calm, silver, undisturbed. Yet when he stepped closer, something rippled beneath the surface. A tremor, almost like a breath.

He frowned, but when he blinked, it was gone. Only the soft sound of flowing water remained.

He shook his head, exhaling slowly. “Just tired,” he murmured.

But as the night deepened, and the scent of rain crept back into the wind, that quiet dread returned — faint but unmistakable.

The kind that comes when the world is *too* still.

The kind that knows warm days never last forever.

# **Chapter 15**

The village had fallen into a rhythm of joy.

Children’s laughter echoed between the fields; the smell of baked bread and roasted corn drifted through the afternoon air. The riverside buzzed with gentle chatter as families gathered for the midsummer feast — a celebration of harvest, rain, and simple gratitude.

Lanterns hung from poles along the banks, their reflections trembling in the slow-moving water. The sky burned gold as the sun sank behind the trees, washing everything in warmth.

Esdel stood among the crowd, relaxed in a way he hadn’t been for years — or perhaps in forever. He listened to Reyn arguing cheerfully with the millwright about wheel design, to Lyora laughing somewhere near the cooking stalls, to the steady rhythm of life moving easily around him.

He had become, unknowingly, a thread in their fabric — a presence taken for granted, trusted, and loved in quiet ways. And for a brief, perfect hour, he believed he truly belonged.

Then came the question.

A small hand tugged lightly at his sleeve.

Esdel turned, smiling down at a young boy — perhaps seven or eight — with hair tousled by the wind and eyes bright as riverlight.

“Esdel,” the boy said, tilting his head curiously, “how did you make the water flow so well?”

Esdel knelt so they were eye level, his voice kind and calm. “Ah, that’s easy enough. I just used a bit of math and physics to calculate the slope.”

The boy blinked. “Physic?”

The word hung in the air like a stone dropped into still water.

Esdel chuckled softly, expecting the child to laugh or nod in mock understanding. But instead, the boy’s face was blank — not shy, not playful. Just confusion.

“You don’t know what Physic is?” Esdel asked gently.

The child shook his head. “No, Esdel.”

Esdel hesitated, glancing up — and noticed a few nearby villagers watching with mild curiosity. Their smiles were kind, but uncertain. Reyn stood a few steps away, expression unreadable.

Trying to ease the awkwardness, Esdel picked up a stick and drew in the dirt.

“See here?” he said, sketching a line on the ground. “This slope lets water flow downward because of gravity — the pull of weight toward the earth. So, if you measure the—”

He stopped.

Their faces were polite, attentive… and completely lost.

“—the pull of weight,” he tried again, searching for simpler words. “The way water wants to fall, not rise. If we guide it just right, it moves faster, smoother.”

“Like asking the river nicely,” one farmer offered with a chuckle.

There was laughter then — gentle, well-meaning, but it missed the point entirely.

Esdel forced a smile. “Something like that,” he murmured.

The boy giggled, satisfied, and ran off toward the others. Conversation resumed, the noise swelling back around him. But for Esdel, the air felt strangely hollow.

He stared at the marks he’d drawn in the dirt — lines and symbols that once shaped his world. To him, they were language. To them, they were meaningless scratches.

He knelt there a moment longer, his hand hovering over the drawing. Then he brushed it away, the sand swallowing every mark.

Behind him, the villagers laughed, traded cups, sang softly beneath the lanterns. The world went on, bright and kind.

But Esdel felt something shift — faint as wind under the skin, quiet as the first drop of rain before a storm.

He looked toward the river, the water glinting gold under the lantern light. It moved as it always had — steady, obedient. Yet in its reflection, he saw something that unsettled him: *his own eyes,* distant and uncertain.

He straightened slowly, the noise of the festival washing over him like an echo from somewhere far away.

For the first time since he had awakened in this peaceful place, Esdel felt… separate.

Not lonely. Not rejected.  
Just *different.*

And though no one else noticed, he could feel the fragile fabric of belonging pull — ever so slightly — at the seams.

The laughter continued. The river flowed.  
But the night had grown just a little colder.

# **Chapter 16**

The change was not sudden.  
It came like the shifting of wind — subtle, nearly invisible, yet impossible to ignore.

In the days that followed the riverside gathering, Esdel noticed the silence first.  
A pause in conversation as he walked by.  
A smile that came a heartbeat too late.  
Whispers that stilled the moment his shadow crossed the path.

No one said anything cruel. No one accused or questioned.  
Yet something unspoken had settled in the air — a faint distance, as if the village had taken a single step back from him.

He tried to brush it off. Told himself it was nothing. But even Reyn, usually quick with laughter, seemed quieter now — studying him sometimes as though trying to understand something that didn’t fit.

By the third night, Esdel couldn’t sleep.

The moon hung pale and heavy above the rooftops, casting silver across his small room. He sat by the window, the cool air brushing against his face, his thoughts turning in slow, uneasy circles.

His gaze fell to his hand resting on the sill — the same hand that had once been gone.

It looked ordinary now. Flesh, lines, warmth. No seam, no shadow of what had been lost.  
And yet, the memory of that missing limb haunted him — not in pain, but in mystery. How had it returned? Why had the dark mark faded only after he came here?

He flexed his fingers slowly, watching them move in the moonlight. They obeyed perfectly.  
But something in his chest tightened — a quiet, wordless dread that he couldn’t explain, even to himself.

He didn’t understand what he was anymore. Or who.

A gentle knock at the door pulled him from his thoughts.

When he opened it, Lyora stood there — her hair loose, her robe light against the night breeze. She held a small cup of steaming herbal tea, the fragrance of chamomile and wild mint curling softly through the air.

“You haven’t been resting,” she said quietly. Her voice was soft, but there was concern beneath it. “You’ll make yourself sick again.”

Esdel hesitated before taking the cup. The warmth seeped into his fingers, grounding him.

“I’m fine,” he murmured. “Just… thinking.”

She didn’t press for more. She never did.

Instead, Lyora stepped closer, her bare feet soundless against the wooden floor, and settled beside him by the window.  
The two sat in silence, the moonlight painting their faces pale and tender.

And so, Esdel fought alone.

He parried a spear, ducked under a blade. His movements were desperate but precise — the rhythm of someone remembering the music of survival.  
Three soldiers fell before him, their bodies hitting the earth like broken drums.

Then came the arrows.

Seven of them — dark streaks slicing through air and sound.  
He turned, too slow.

Each found its mark.

Shoulder. Thigh. Side. Back.  
The last pierced his arm, driving through flesh and fabric, pinning his sleeve to his skin.

The force spun him. He fell hard, the mud cool against his face. The world blurred around the edges — shouts distant, shapes dissolving. He tried to lift his sword. His hand shook. Blood dripped down his wrist, black in the shifting light.

Then, beneath the noise, he heard it — the pounding of his own pulse.  
Louder.  
Sharper.  
Until the world itself began to bend around it.

And then the pain turned white-hot. His vision split open. The color bled away until only crimson remained.

Time fractured.

The world froze in place — soldiers mid-swing, arrows hanging like shards of glass in the air. The smoke curled in still spirals.  
Esdel stood inside that stillness, his breath catching.

He could *see.*

Every heartbeat in the air, every trembling motion beneath armor, every intention waiting to become movement. The forest was no longer sound — it was pulse.

And amid those frozen fragments, one rhythm burned brighter than the rest.  
A heartbeat stronger, steadier. Commanding.

The enemy captain.

Something inside him screamed *go.*  
And his body obeyed.

He moved through the still world like a blade through water. Each step tore fire through his veins. The Karunākṣa pulsed — every motion sharper, every breath drawn from a place deeper than instinct. The light from his eyes painted the ground in red veins.

He reached the captain.  
His sword rose — or perhaps it was already falling.

The sound of the strike never came.

Only silence.

Then —  
the world caught up.

Blood burst outward, sudden and bright. The man collapsed.  
Time rushed back in. The noise returned in a roar — steel, shouting, terror.

Esdel dropped to his knees, gasping. His vision dimmed; the glow in his eyes flickered and died. What remained was ruin — smoke curling through air heavy with death.

He could hear someone shouting behind him.  
His name, perhaps.  
A familiar voice.

But it was faint, distant — as though it belonged to another life.

The Karunākṣa’s power left him hollow. His muscles trembled. His heartbeat slowed.  
All around him, confusion spread — the enemy reeling at their fallen leader, the formation breaking. For a moment, the battle tilted in their favor.

But Esdel didn’t see it.

He knelt in the dirt, staring at the horizon —  
where the sky was cracked with fire and ash, and something inside him whispered of a promise  
he could no longer remember.

# **Chapter 37**

The world had gone still, as if time itself held its breath. The headless body of the enemy captain collapsed in slow motion — blood arcing through the dusk like molten glass before it darkened the soil. And at its heart stood Esdel.

His blade hung loose at his side, dripping red.  
Seven arrows jutted from his body, each one trembling slightly with the faint rhythm of his breath. His eyes, a quiet brown in any other light, now burned dimly with that cursed crimson glow — the Karunākṣa still pulsing faintly behind them.

He didn’t speak. He didn’t even move.  
He was a silhouette against the dying sun — battered, alone, and somehow still standing.

Then came the sound.  
 A single gasp.  
 A ripple through enemy ranks.

From the back ranks, Captain Erven of the 3rd Logistic Company — calm as ever, armor smeared with soot and earth — saw the moment of weakness. His jaw tightened.

“Now,” he breathed.

“Now’s our chance. For the wounded!”

The survivors gathered under the black canopy of the forest, where only embers dared to breathe. The cries had faded, replaced by the still hum of night. The wounded lay in uneven lines, their faces half-lit by dying firelight, and the rest — too tired to rest — stared at nothing.

Esdel sat apart, his back against a tree, the world swaying faintly around him. His breath came slow, uneven, dragging through his chest like sand through glass. He pressed his hands to the dirt, grounding himself, feeling the earth pulse faintly beneath his palms.

The Karunākṣa was still open.

He could see everything — the curve of every ember as it fell, the minute tremor of a moth’s wing, the faint mist of breath escaping a sleeping soldier’s lips. Every second unfolded painfully, drawn out, raw.

*Too much.*

“...Enough,” he rasped.

Sound rushed back in, colors crashed together, the weight of reality slammed through his bones. He choked, coughing blood onto his palms. His body convulsed. The glow in his eyes flickered and died, leaving veins of red tearing through the whites.

His wounds were gone. The arrows had fallen from him somewhere in the dark, and the holes they left had vanished as though they’d never existed. His skin was smooth, unmarred, too perfect.  
And yet his blood still covered the ground.

He stared at it.  
At his hands.  
At the trembling that wouldn’t stop.

“Hey…”

He startled. The voice was soft, familiar.

The soldier who had spoken of his pregnant wife earlier — a man with kind eyes and exhaustion carved deep into his face — crouched beside him.

“You shouldn’t be out here alone,” he said, voice low.

Esdel tried to smile, but it faltered.

The soldier’s gaze fell to the dried blood streaking Esdel’s face, the faint crimson web in his eyes. For a moment, he looked as if he might speak, then thought better of it. Instead, he placed a steadying hand on Esdel’s shoulder.

Esdel’s voice came hoarse, heavy.  
“I didn’t think. I just moved.”

The soldier exhaled, staring into the embers. “Been in this war too long,” he murmured. “Every time I think I’ll make it home, something tries to take it away.”

Esdel looked at him, at the small flicker of fragile hope in the man’s eyes, and felt something stir — faint, unfamiliar, painful.

“I think you will,” he said. “You’ll make it home.”

The soldier nodded, stretching as he rose. “You look like death itself, though. Try to rest, Hero.”

He said it lightly, teasing, but Esdel flinched all the same.

Hero.

He didn’t know the man that word belonged to.  
He didn’t even know if he wanted to.

When the soldier’s footsteps faded, Esdel was alone again.  
The night pressed close — damp, silent, smelling of iron and ash.

He touched his face. The blood was already drying. The faint red lines beneath his skin were fading like ghosts returning to sleep. He looked at his reflection in the flickering emberlight — eyes still faintly glimmering, not with rage, nor divine power, but something softer.

Something *human.*

He tilted his head back, staring at the sky where the stars returned, scattered and imperfect.

And for the first time since those cursed eyes had opened,  
Esdel smiled — faintly, almost uncertainly — but real.

A mercy that burned, and yet… did not destroy.

# **Chapter 38**

The dawn came pale and thin — the kind that felt hesitant to touch the ground after a night soaked in death. Mist still lingered low, ghostlike among the trees, when the order came.

“Move out. We don’t linger here.”

Captain Erven’s voice was quiet but firm. No one argued. The soldiers packed in silence, each movement heavy with exhaustion and the muted awareness that they had survived where others hadn’t.

The air carried the faint tang of iron, smoke, and damp earth — the scent of a battle that refused to leave their skin.

Esdel walked near the rear, the weight of his satchel pressing faintly against his side. Every few steps, his vision wavered — a brief, sharp darkness that cut through the edges of his sight. Beneath his skin, something pulsed too deep, too alive.

The ache behind his eyes whispered of the Karunākṣa, of the price that still lingered in his veins.

He said nothing. He simply walked.

Hours passed before the forest thinned into open land — and with it, the low hum of a living field.

The warfront.

Tents stretched far across the horizon, canvas sagging beneath the morning dew. Smoke rose in thin trails from cookfires. Somewhere, a man cried out in pain — somewhere else, laughter broke briefly before being swallowed by the wind. The ground was trampled, muddied, alive with both the living and the dying.

Esdel’s eyes followed the sound of groans until he reached the largest medical tent. He ducked inside.

The air changed immediately — thick with herbs, rot, and something like despair.

An elderly man with graying hair and a surgeon’s hands knelt beside a soldier whose arm was darkened near the shoulder. His voice cut sharp through the din:

“Clean that wound properly! You think this is a tavern floor? Gods above, wash the blood off before you touch anything!”

Esdel approached carefully. “Excuse me,” he said. “I’m an apothecary. I can help treat—”

The man turned with the ferocity of someone who’d forgotten sleep. His eyes flicked over Esdel’s face — too young, too calm, too clean — and hardened.

“An apothecary?” he scoffed. “From where? Some quiet village with chickens and soup?”  
He turned back to his patient. “This isn’t your little hut, boy. Fetch water if you want to help.”

Esdel bowed slightly. “Yes, sir.”

And so, he did.

He fetched water until his arms ached. He boiled it, cleaned cloths until his fingers wrinkled, and crushed herbs with hands that blistered under the pestle’s weight. He didn’t speak, not even when the doctor barked commands sharp enough to cut. But when the man’s back was turned, Esdel checked pulses, adjusted doses, and whispered soft reassurances to the wounded who trembled beneath their bandages.

Days bled together.  
Sleep became a stranger.

When the doctor — Halden, as Esdel later learned — collapsed mid-procedure from exhaustion, Esdel simply stepped in.

He finished the sutures in silence, wrapped the wounds with steady hands, cleaned every instrument, and started the next patient without pause.

He never sought praise. Only order.

By the second week, something shifted.  
The tent no longer reeked of decay. The air carried less fever. The dying slowed.

Halden noticed.

One morning, Esdel was boiling water outside, sleeves rolled high, scrubbing instruments with ash and vinegar until the metal shone faintly in the dawn light. His movements were slow, methodical — almost reverent.

Halden approached, mug in hand. His voice was still gruff, but softer at the edges.  
“Boy,” he said. “You’re wasting good hours of sleep.”

Esdel looked up. His tone was calm, quiet.  
“We’re wasting more good men if we don’t clean properly.”

Halden blinked, then exhaled through his nose — something between a scoff and reluctant amusement.

“You think keeping things tidy wins wars?”

“It saves lives,” Esdel replied.

For a moment, the old man just stared. Then he muttered, “…Damn it, maybe you’re right,” and walked off, the corner of his mouth twitching into the ghost of a smile.

From that morning onward, the tone in the tent changed.  
Halden still barked orders, but his eyes held respect now — or something close to it.

Esdel became indispensable.  
When Halden’s hands trembled too much to hold a needle, Esdel took it from him without a word. When Halden’s voice slurred mid-command, Esdel finished his sentence and carried on.

By the end of the second week, even the soldiers had begun to notice.

“Morning, Apothecary!”  
“Esdel, that salve ready yet?”  
“Hey, don’t forget to eat!”

The name Esdel passed through the camp like a soft, steady wind — spoken in tones that held trust, gratitude, and something warmer.

At noon one day, he stopped by a soldier’s bed. The man pushed away his bowl of soup.

“Not hungry,” he muttered.

Esdel crossed his arms. “Then chew anyway.”

The man frowned. “You can’t order me—”

“I can,” Esdel interrupted, voice even but gentle. “If you want to live. You’ve lost enough blood. Don’t lose your will, too.”

The soldier glared for a heartbeat — then sighed and started eating.  
Around them, others laughed quietly.

Later that night, the same men called across the fire:  
“Hey, Apothecary! You treat our wounds — now treat our stomachs! Got anything for bad soup?”

Esdel looked up from his work, a faint smirk tugging at his lips. “Discipline.”

The camp burst into laughter. Even Halden, sitting by the flames with his tea, cracked a grin.

When the laughter ebbed, Halden leaned closer, his voice carrying only to Esdel.  
“You’ve done well, boy. I was wrong about you.”

Esdel blinked, caught off guard. “…Sir?”

“You’re not just some backwater apothecary. You’ve got healer’s hands.”  
Halden paused, watching the fire. “And a soldier’s heart.”

Esdel looked into the flames. His reflection shimmered there — a face he still didn’t recognize, but one that felt less empty.  
“I just do what I can,” he said.

Halden chuckled softly. “That’s what the best of us always say.”

Silence settled again, but it was a softer kind — not heavy, not suffocating. The kind born of work done well, of lives saved, of purpose rediscovered.

The night breathed around them — the crackle of fire, the murmur of tired men, the steady rhythm of life continuing despite the ruin.

And in that fragile calm, Esdel found something he hadn’t felt in a long, long time.

Not glory.  
Not pride.  
But *purpose.*

And though his eyes still ached from the lingering ghost of the Karunākṣa, for the first time since the village of Edenridge, Esdel allowed himself to rest — not as a hero, not as a weapon, but simply as a man with healing hands.

# **Chapter 39**

The week began with an uneasy rhythm.

Esdel noticed it first — the beds filling faster, the groans heavier, and the faint stench that clung to the air no matter how many times he cleaned. At first, he thought it was exhaustion. Too many wounded, not enough hands. But soon, the pattern was impossible to ignore.

Fever. Vomiting. Skin pale, lips dry.

These weren’t battle wounds. These were **illnesses**.

He checked the records, spoke with the quartermaster, and reviewed the food supply. The rations hadn’t changed. The herbs were measured, the instruments boiled. Yet the patients kept coming.

By the fourth day, mortality crept higher than wounds from the front.

“...It’s the water,” he murmured.

“Sir, I believe the contamination comes from our water source,” Esdel said firmly.

Halden sighed, rubbing his forehead. “Esdel, we don’t have the men or time to chase every shadow. We heal. That’s our job.”

Esdel shifted his stance, eyes narrowing.

The cave fell silent, save for the drip of water and the crackle of flame.

Then, both men moved.

# **Chapter 40**

Esdel sat outside the tent, finally breathing air that didn’t smell like medicine. His shirt was still stained with old blood, but he didn’t care.

Behind him, Halden appeared carrying a pot of steaming coffee and a small basket of skewered meat. Benn followed, half-asleep on his feet, holding three cups.

Esdel groaned, covering his face. “I hate both of you.”

And longing, once articulated in the quiet privacy of her sitting room, became, to her altered perception, **love**.

In the months that followed, she defended him from every whisper, every outright warning that managed to slip past her doors. When the old, established families of the Capital muttered that Aldric’s rise was too swift, his acquisition of favor too conveniently paved, she dismissed them all with a look of cool disdain.

“They never knew him as I do,” she would say, her voice soft but absolute, almost pleading for others to understand the truth she believed she possessed. “He is not like them. He possesses a heart that understands sorrow.”

So when he proposed—softly, with perfect timing, on a rain-soaked evening when the manor felt cold and damp and his presence was the only source of heat—his strong, steady hand placed surely over her trembling one, she did not hesitate. The idea of returning to the terrifying isolation of her grief without him was unthinkable.

The entire kingdom gasped when the news spread, sending shockwaves through the elite circles: Lady Wei Anlan, the devastated widow of the late, disgraced traitor Lián Hengzhi, had married Aldric Reng—the very man who had once stood as a crucial, damning witness to her husband’s downfall and execution. It was too fast, too bold, too convenient. The violation of protocol was staggering.

Yet, in the quiet, inner halls of the Lián estate, where the heavy scent of sandalwood incense had once burned for mourning, a new sound began to echo: the light, silvery sound of Anlan’s laughter.

To the world, it was crafted into the perfect, timeless story of redemption: the compassionate Count who healed the shattered heart of a traitor’s innocent widow; the noble man reborn through the purifying fire of forbidden love.

But in truth, it was a tale of something colder, sharper, and far more transactional.

For the marriage gave Aldric Reng everything he had desired with surgical precision: the last remaining Lián financial holdings, which were secretly tied to Anlan’s dowry; the immediate social legitimacy of being connected to an ancient, noble house; and, most importantly, a perfect, unassailable disguise.

No one in the court would dare question the loyalty, the ethics, or the motivations of a man who had successfully navigated the difficult path of winning forgiveness from the victim’s own wife. He was now protected by the emotional complexity of his own actions.

No one looked beneath the tenderness of his touch to see the calculated precision of his long-term plan.

And Lady Wei Anlan, once sharp, fiercely intelligent, and unyielding in her defense of her husband, smiled beautifully beside him—her voice calm, her movements graceful, her lovely eyes still touched by the shimmering, distant remnants of some invisible, profound sleep. The memories she cherished were not organic; they were artifacts.

She would never know, or perhaps never be able to care, how much of her affection, her gratitude, and her ultimate love had been painstakingly brewed, drop by controlled drop, in the vials of amber liquid he once brought to her door. Her devotion was his final, most enduring poison.

# **Chapter 59**

Lián Xueyao sat beneath the dim canvas of her tent, a structure meant for temporary deployment yet one that had felt like her permanent, suffocating cage for years. The **oil lamp** on the campaign chest flickered violently, less like a heart beating and more like a dying star fighting against the endless draft that snuck through the crude seams of the tent flaps. The scent of stale oil, leather, and woodsmoke clung to the fabric, a harsh, constant reminder of the life she was forced to lead.

Three years. Three agonizingly long years had passed since she last saw her home—the palatial, sun-drenched grand corridors of the **Lián estate**, where light always seemed to fall just right on the polished wood; the ancient **peach tree** outside her mother’s chamber that bloomed violently pink every spring; the deep, resonant calm of her **father’s voice** echoing across the central courtyard during evening lessons. Now, those cherished memories were brittle, beautiful ghosts, buried deep beneath the relentless mud of war and the cruel certainty of betrayal.

Her father, Lord Hengzhi—her one and only father—had been a pillar of the Kingdom. Yet he had died under the weight of a meticulously woven lie, a traitor to the very crown he spent his life defending, his honor sacrificed to **Count Aldric Reng’s** carefully laid plan.

Xueyao ran a thumb over the crease in the dark, heavy message—a message that had arrived like a dagger, delivered by one of Lord Hengzhi's most loyal, and now most desperately dead, vassals. It confirmed the unthinkable: the charge of rebellion was a sophisticated fraud. Her father, the man who had instilled in her the core value of unwavering loyalty, was now officially deemed a betrayer, allied with the rebel factions. He was denied a proper burial, his bones were fed to the dogs.

The same Aldric Reng, the architect of this ruin, now occupied the most sacred seats of her life. He sat in her father’s place at the high table, he wore her father’s name, not by right, but by virtue of her mother’s new vows, and he drank her family’s prized, decades-aged plum wine as though it were his birthright. This same Aldric, she thought with a tightening in her chest that felt like physical pain, smiled the serene, reassuring smile of a savior before the court, his hands outwardly clean, while he inwardly celebrated the destruction of the Lián clan.

When the news finally arrived—carried not by official messenger but by quiet, horrified rumor—that **Lady Wei Anlan** had married the very man who orchestrated her husband’s demise, Xueyao’s heart didn’t just break. It clenched with such a cold, terrible force it almost tore her in half. The pain was so sharp it eclipsed the grief she felt for her father. This was desecration. This was the final blow to her family's dignity.

She had begged her commander for leave, once, twice, a third time. First, to attend the sham of her father’s funeral, then again, just to see her mother, to look into her eyes and discover the terrifying truth for herself. Each time, the response was the same icy refusal, masked in the necessity of duty: *The war needs you.*

And behind those cold words lay the true conspiracy: **Grand Duke Rowan Hale** was pulling the strings. His influence stretched across the map, a complex, sticky web ensnaring every aspect of her life, from the lowliest courier to the highest command. She, the "Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon," the fiery symbol of morale for the entire Imperial Army, was merely a piece on the board—a distraction, an asset to be controlled, and most importantly, a dangerous disturbance to the plans of Count Aldric Reng and Grand Duke Rowan Hale. Outwardly, she was the epitome of Imperial loyalty. Inside, she was just a daughter, grieving and trapped, torn between her solemn oath to the empire and the raw, desperate ache to return home and confront the ghosts that wore the faces of her loved ones.

But the night the late Lord Hengzhi’s most trusted vassal, in his final, guilt-ridden message, revealed the devastating truth about the **"tonic,"** something in Xueyao finally shattered—and from that rubble, something dark and unyielding was born.

The draught Aldric brought her mother wasn’t the restorative medicine it was claimed to be. It was a chain. A subtle, invisible, and insidious poison designed not to kill, but to **subdue**. It worked by softening her mother's brilliant mind, dampening her proud spirit, and binding her emotions, making her pliant, suggestible, and utterly unable to resist the smooth, destructive charm of the man who had methodically dismantled her life.

Xueyao’s hands had trembled, not with weakness, but with a terrifying, contained fury, as she read the confession. Her mother—proud, gentle, and one of the most brilliant political minds of her generation—was reduced to a puppet, manipulated into a false marriage through chemical deceit.

The apothecaries the vassal had secretly consulted confirmed the presence of an unknown substance, a thing alien to any known healer, described in the message as being both chemical and unnatural—a **"whisper of control"** in every seemingly benign drop.

“It’s too late,” the vassal's shaky script concluded, heavy with failure. “She won’t listen to reason, not from anyone. The bonds are too deep.” But Lián Xueyao, the Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon, refused to accept that finality. If her mother was trapped behind an enchanted wall of false affection, then she—her eldest child, her true blood—would be the one to break it down and free her, no matter the unforgivable cost to her career, her honor, or her very life.

Her golden eyes, usually calm and reflective as frost under the dawn sun, now burned with a light that was colder, harder, and infinitely deadlier than any winter. She began to plan, the strategy taking shape in the depths of the night like a dark constellation. She could not go herself—that would be an immediate death sentence and ruin all chances of discovering proof. She would send someone in her place—someone unnoticed, sharp-witted, and utterly expendable. Someone who could blend into the background of the Lián estate, observe the poisonous reality, and, crucially, survive.

She turned to **Changli**, her most trusted lady’s maid, who was more than an attendant—she was a childhood friend, a shadow who had followed her loyally since girlhood, even into the grime and danger of the military camps.

“Is there anyone we can trust for this?” Xueyao asked quietly, the question stripped of her usual commanding authority, revealing the vulnerability beneath.

Changli, whose loyalty was a known quantity but whose caution was well-earned, hesitated. A long, charged silence settled between them, broken only by the hungry lick of the oil lamp flame. Then, with a subtle but firm nod, she spoke. “There’s one,” she said, her voice barely a murmur. “**Esdel**. The apothecary who transferred to our post seven months ago.”

Changli's tone carried a curious blend of assessment and slight, weary amusement. “He’s strange. He cracks unexpected jokes when everyone is grim, and he keeps rigidly to himself when the rest of the company is drinking away the horrors. The soldiers like him well enough, but he never lets anyone truly get close. He’s clever, my Lady… maybe too clever. But most importantly—he has no past. No family. No visible roots to anchor him here, or anywhere else. If things go wrong, if Aldric’s network catches him, we can claim no knowledge of him. We can, politely, **‘take care’** of him.”

Xueyao listened, her gaze distant. *Esdel.* She had not forgotten him — a pair of tired brown eyes by the roadside the night the Titan fell, and the flicker of red she had seen in them. Seven months, and she had never gotten her answer. When Changli finished, a faint, chilling smile touched the corner of the Lady’s mouth—a smile devoid of warmth, fueled only by the steel of her new resolve.

“Perfect,” she murmured, the single word sealing Esdel’s fate. Her fingers brushed the hastily drawn map of the Kingdom on her campaign table, tracing the long, impossible path back to her lost home. “Then he’ll go.”

Outside the tent, the relentless wind of the north howled through the camp, carrying the stark scent of iron, ash, and distant fear. The muffled, heavy rhythm of war drums echoed faintly, a constant reminder of the external conflict. But for the first time in three years, Lián Xueyao’s thoughts were no longer consumed by the battles. They were fixed entirely on her mother—and on the man who had taken everything from the Lián family.

And as the small lamp flame trembled, reflected in her golden eyes like two twin shards of cold, deadly light, Lián Xueyao swore quietly to the night wind—that even if she had to burn the empire she served to the ground, she would walk through the ashes to save her mother and reclaim her family’s name.

# **Chapter 60**

The wind had not ceased its whisper since dawn. It slid beneath the tent seams like an uninvited ghost, carrying with it the thin, metallic scent of old blood and burned wood. In the distance, the battlefield slept uneasily—its silence was the kind that came not from peace, but from exhaustion, a temporary cease in the endless, grinding conflict.

Within the heart of the command encampment, General Lián Xueyao stood before a war table scarred with years of conflict. The map before her was a tapestry of ambition and loss. She had endured the cost, rising from a mere knight to the rank of General through sheer competence and unyielding will. But three years was an eternity, and the deep, pervasive fatigue of command now claimed her, demanding payment.

In her gloved hand, she held the official **grant of leave**, signed by the Supreme Commander: a three-month respite before she was due back to the fray. It was a lifeline and a timer, a reprieve that felt too short to matter. After three refusals, her fourth petition had been granted without a word of explanation — and that unsettled her more than any refusal had.

Despite the heavy, dark General's uniform she wore—its shoulders wide and rigid—she remained a woman of startling, almost ethereal beauty. Three years of harsh campaigning, sleepless nights, and the cruel northern sun had failed to diminish the **smooth, pale fairness of her skin**. Her figure, though currently concealed beneath the thick, protective steel fittings and the dark wool of her uniform, was exquisitely proportioned, lending her movements a rare, controlled grace that few men on the front possessed. Her **long, dark hair**, was pulled back severely into a soldier’s queue, yet loose strands that escaped framed a face of startling classical beauty: high cheekbones, perfect lips, and **golden eyes** that now mirrored the lamp's light with an almost painful, world-weary sorrow. She was a vision of perfection draped in the brutal tools of war.

She looked up, her gaze settling on **Changli**, her most trusted attendant and friend since childhood. Changli was the same age and possessed a beautiful, gentle elegance that was the perfect counterpoint to Xueyao’s hardened command presence. Changli was dressed in simple, yet exquisitely tailored traveling silks, ready for their journey.

“The orders are confirmed,” Xueyao said, her voice dry and low. “Three months. The Lián Estate is within the Kingdom’s walls. We can make the journey in five days. I have secured **fifteen soldiers** of Lián House from the veteran ranks to form our personal escort. We need a medical staff too.”

Changli stepped closer, her expression shifting to one of keen calculation. “My Lady, for that slot — the same man we spoke of. **Apothecary Esdel.**”

Xueyao raised an elegant eyebrow, her exhaustion momentarily forgotten. “I meant to send him ahead alone. Now that I am going myself — why a field apothecary, Changli? I could summon a physician from the royal academy in the capital.”

Changli’s voice softened slightly, laced with practical cunning. “Precisely, My Lady. This journey, despite the official seal, worries me. And when we are in the capital, we may need a medical mind who is not already loyal to the politics of the Kingdom or the Estate. Esdel is already here, embedded in the Ninth Division.”

She paused, then delivered the final, cold calculus. “More importantly, his term of levied service is nearly spent. He is due for discharge in three months — about when our leave ends. He is a man with no deep roots, no political ties, and is utterly dependent on this letter of deployment for his future records. Should the journey prove hostile, or should his knowledge become… *inconvenient* in the capital, he is **expendable** without repercussion to the House.”

Xueyao stared at the map, running a gloved finger over the marked road to the capital. *Expendable.* The word settled in her gut, tasting like the bitter tang of gunpowder. She was tired of the front, but she knew the capital’s court was merely a different kind of battlefield. The thought of bringing a man whom she could easily **dispose of should things turn sour** held a ruthless appeal. This trip was not a vacation; it was a strategic withdrawal.

“Very well,” Xueyao confirmed. “Apothecary Esdel it is. Summon him. I need his official letter prepared, designating him as medical personnel attached to the General’s private security detail.”

Esdel arrived not long after, summoned from the fever-scented medical tents. He entered the General’s personal chamber with the quiet, professional air of a man used to urgent calls. His uniform bore only the stains of tinctures and blood.

“Apothecary Esdel,” Xueyao said. “You are to accompany me, General Lián Xueyao, to the capital for my period of sanctioned leave. You are designated as medical auxiliary for my private escort of fifteen soldiers. This letter secures your position and officially grants you leave from the Ninth Division.”

Esdel accepted the thick parchment—stamped with the General’s personal seal—with careful hands. It was the official sanction he needed to leave the front without being marked as a deserter. He looked briefly at the letter, then at the General.

“I understand, General,” he said, his voice low and even. “I will gather the necessary supplies immediately. My focus will be on the health and discretion of the unit.”

“Excellent,” Xueyao replied. “Changli will finalize the logistics.”

Esdel bowed precisely—a formal, precise movement—and then turned to leave. He disappeared back toward the medical tents, the official letter clutched in his hand, a soldier obeying the highest command.

---

The air in the outer barracks was cold and dark, smelling only of damp canvas and distant woodsmoke. It was the deepest part of the night, when even the sentries moved with weary silence.

**Changli** found Esdel checking the weight distribution of his medical satchels outside his tent. She approached silently, her silk travel trousers rustling barely audibly.

“Preparing for our journey, Apothecary?” she murmured, her voice a low, melodic purr that was entirely out of place in the desolate camp.

Esdel immediately straightened, his movements economical. “Attendant Changli. Yes. I must ensure the medicinal herbs are properly stored to withstand the five-day journey.”

Changli stopped directly in front of him, close enough that he was forced to inhale the soft, expensive floral perfume that clung to her—a scent that stood in stark contrast to the battlefield stench. The lantern light caught the faint shimmer of her dark, beautiful hair and the graceful curve of her neck. Changli was utterly aware of her own presence: her features were exquisite, her gown intentionally tailored to hint at her **attractive, elegant body**. She had used this quiet power many times to gain influence, information, or favors.

“Such serious focus,” she said, her voice dropping lower, teasing. Her **strikingly beautiful** eyes, dark and knowing, held his gaze with a glimmer of mischief. “The General will expect absolute dedication, of course, but even soldiers need comfort on the long road, wouldn’t you agree?”

Esdel did not flinch, nor did his expression change. The cold, distant reflection of the lamplight in his eyes remained.

“My duty is to ensure the General and the escort remain in peak medical condition,” he replied evenly. “Comfort is a luxury reserved for the victorious, not for those of us in service.”

Changli took a slow step closer, her hand rising to brush lightly against the rough wool of his sleeve. Her seductive smile was wide now, challenging.

“You’re too harsh on yourself, Apothecary. We leave the war behind tonight. Think of the warm beds, the quiet rooms, and the soft company that awaits in the capital. Sometimes,” she leaned in just close enough that her breath feathered his ear, “a small indulgence ensures better service.”

She allowed her body to press subtly against his arm, a direct, undeniable invitation, relying on the primal response her beauty typically elicited.

Esdel remained utterly still. The pressure of her touch, the potent fragrance, and the blatant challenge in her gaze seemed to wash over him without penetration. He didn't pull away, but he didn't lean in either. He was a statue, carved of duty.

“Attendant Changli,” he said, his voice perfectly steady, carrying neither judgment nor temptation. “I am assigned to General Lián Xueyao for medical auxiliary support. My presence is secured by an official order, and my focus is solely on my commission. Any action that risks my reputation or distracts from my designated role is unacceptable.”

He then lifted his satchel, forcing her hand away from his sleeve with the necessary motion of adjusting the strap.

“If you will excuse me,” he finished, his tone utterly professional, “I have inventory logs to finalize before the dawn assembly. Good night, Attendant.”

He bowed slightly—formal, precise—and then turned away, disappearing toward the dark, quiet lines of the auxiliary tents. The crunch of his boots against the packed dirt was the only sound.

Changli watched him go. For a long moment, the teasing, seductive smile that had captivated countless men remained frozen on her beautiful face. Then, slowly, as the realization of his complete lack of reaction sunk in, the smile faded. Her expression went **flat, quiet, and supremely thoughtful**. Her striking beauty and seductive methods had failed entirely. Unmoved.

She murmured softly to the dark air, “Unmoved. A true man of principle, or merely a machine?”

A short while later, Changli slipped into General Xueyao’s private quarters. Xueyao was still awake, finishing a final set of complex cipher messages.

“Report, Changli,” the General ordered, not looking up.

“He is steady, My Lady,” Changli said immediately, her tone entirely businesslike, the seductive languor gone. “I delivered the test as requested. He did not succumb to charm, vanity, or temptation. He cited only his duty and his official commission as reason for refusal. He is a man who adheres strictly to the letter of the law.”

Xueyao finally lifted her gaze. Their eyes met—a silent exchange of calculation and understanding.

“He barely noticed your beauty?” Xueyao asked, a faint trace of satisfaction ghosting across her lips.

“He barely noticed me at all, General. Only the proximity to his task. He is exactly what we need: a man of **principle, predictable, and fully controlled by his orders**. He will not betray us for gold or lust. If the situation requires, he will be perfectly manageable.”

A long, significant silence followed. General Lián Xueyao had spent three years trusting her life to soldiers, but rarely trusting their characters. Changli’s test, though risky, provided the ultimate assurance.

“Good,” Xueyao said softly, her golden eyes reflecting a newfound, brittle confidence. “That is why I **trust him** now, Changli. His integrity is based on his own disciplined will, not on his devotion to my rank. Finalize the escort. We leave at the change of the guard.”

And outside, as the first cold signs of dawn began to grey the eastern horizon, General Lián Xueyao and her escort—now including the predictable, expendable, and indispensable Apothecary Esdel—began their journey toward the secured walls of the Kingdom, carrying the scent of war into the promise of a brief, beautiful, and strategically volatile respite.
