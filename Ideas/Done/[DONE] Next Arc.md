## **🐉 The Fall of House Lián (Revised Heavenly Dragon Version)**

1. Lián Xueyao was born into one of the wealthiest families in the realm. Her father, **Lord Lián Hengzhi**, controlled half the eastern ports and could tilt the kingdom’s trade with a single order. The Liáns were powerful, polished, and—let’s be honest—too used to winning.

2. Their greatest ally was **Count Aldric Reng**, a silver-tongued politician who’d once helped the Liáns climb to prominence. He could talk a snake out of its skin—but when one of his cousins joined a failed rebellion, the King executed ten members of the Reng line “to make an example.” Overnight, Aldric’s name became poison.

3. Ruined and desperate, Aldric struck a secret bargain with **Grand Duke Rowan Hale**, his brother-in-law. In return for protection and political rehabilitation, Aldric promised to deliver eight-tenths of the Lián assets—ports, companies, trade rights—to the Duke.

4. Together they devised a trap. Rowan would gain wartime supply lines and wealth; Aldric would rebuild his reputation by “uncovering” treason.

5. One of Hengzhi’s attendants—bribed by Aldric—loaded crates of contraband and forged rebel letters onto a Lián ship. Nobles were invited to a “routine inspection,” and Aldric just happened to attend.

6. When the crates were opened, chaos erupted. Aldric’s booming accusations echoed across the docks while guards and merchants whispered. Within hours, rumor outran proof, and by nightfall the capital believed Lord Hengzhi had betrayed the crown.

7. The Grand Duke stayed silent, letting the rumor become fact. The King, drained by war and reliant on Rowan’s counsel, signed Hengzhi’s death decree “for the safety of the realm.”

8. After the execution, the prizes were neatly divided: Rowan gained the ports, and Aldric earned redemption as “the man who protected the realm.”

9. Aldric began visiting the Lián estate under the guise of comforting the grieving widow, **Lady Wei Anlan**. He brought her a “restorative tonic” from his private physician. The mixture dulled pain, relaxed the mind, and quietly bound her emotions to him.

10. At first, Lady Wei resisted the court gossip around him, but Aldric’s sympathy and attention filled the silence left by her husband’s death. The tonic made her calmer, lighter, strangely drawn to his presence. Her dependence became affection—and affection, love.

11. In time she defended Aldric from every whisper. When he proposed, she accepted. The marriage gave him the remaining Lián holdings and, more importantly, a perfect disguise: the image of a man redeemed by devotion.

12. **General Lián Xueyao**, on medical leave from the front, returned home to find her mother changed—soft-spoken, pliant, and endlessly tired. She brought her personal guard and her apothecary, **Esdel**, a quiet observer with a soldier’s discipline.

13. Esdel studied the tonic and discovered it contained a mild paralytic and compounds that heightened emotional susceptibility. He reported the poison to Xueyao but stayed silent about the rest, unsure whether revealing it would shatter her mother completely.

14. Xueyao confronted Aldric, accusing him of poisoning Lady Wei. Aldric turned the accusation into theatre—raising his voice, looking wounded, insisting she was “tormented by grief.” Courtiers and servants witnessed the scene, and whispers spread: *the general has lost her mind.*

15. When Xueyao tried to present her evidence, no one stood beside her. “A general may command soldiers,” one noble said, “but not truth.” Even her allies avoided her; no one dared cross the Grand Duke.

16. Meanwhile, her baby brother **Lián Yueming** became a bargaining chip. Rumors claimed the King would appoint Aldric as the boy’s guardian “to preserve the Lián legacy”—meaning Aldric would legally inherit everything.

17. Not long after, Lady Wei died in her sleep. Aldric performed grief like art, hosting a lavish funeral and earning the court’s sympathy. The King, weary of intrigue, granted Aldric full guardianship of the Lián estate.

18. With the House now his, Aldric’s ambition curdled into obsession. He turned his eyes on **Lián Xueyao**—not just for her beauty or status, but because she was **the Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon**, a bloodline blessed to command divine beasts. To marry her would make his heirs half-divine and place the crown itself within reach.

19. When Xueyao learned his intention, something in her broke. She had lost her father to treachery, her mother to manipulation, and now the same man claimed her as his next prize. The court dismissed her protests; no one believed a woman branded “unstable.”

20. So she stopped begging. One storm-dark night, Xueyao kidnapped her infant brother and entrusted him to her most loyal maid. “Raise him far from this city,” she ordered. “Let him live free of our name.”

21. Before dawn, she vanished from the capital. **Esdel** and her loyal guards followed, fully aware that helping her meant exile and death warrants on their heads.

22. By morning, the royal court declared **General Lián Xueyao** a traitor. Anyone associated with her was stripped of rank or executed. Esdel and the soldiers could never return.

23. And thus the Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon disappeared. Some say she wanders the borderlands, her heart a forge of vengeance; others whisper the Holy Dragon itself sleeps beneath her exile, waiting for her call.
