# **Chapter X**

The first thing Esdel felt was *light.*

It pressed softly against his eyelids, warm and golden — the kind of light that didn’t burn, but healed. He breathed in, and the air tasted of earth and herbs, damp wood and sunlight. Somewhere nearby, water trickled, and a woman hummed, low, like a lullaby.

Pale beams hung with dried vines and bundled herbs. A breeze through the open window, carrying leaves and birdsong.

For the first time in what felt like eternity, the world was *still.*

He turned his head — and froze.

His *arm.*

Whole. Real.

The memory of the claws, the tearing, the heat of blood going into the sand came back like a fever. He stared at it, flexing his fingers. Unbroken flesh, save for a faint line of dark, thumb-sized marks running from elbow to wrist. They pulsed once, rhythmically, and faded.

“What…?”

“Ah. You’re awake.”

An elderly woman stepped into the light. Silver hair braided simply, hands folded in her apron, eyes soft green and patient.

“You’ve been asleep for three days,” she said. “The caravan that found you left you in our care. You’re safe now, child.”

“My arm,” he said roughly. “It was gone. I *felt* it gone.”

“Gone? No, no. When you arrived your body was whole. Weak and feverish, but no wounds of that kind. Sometimes, when the mind is pushed past its limits, it weaves dreams so vivid they seem real. Perhaps the desert played tricks on you.”

*Dreams.* But the tearing sound had not felt like a dream.

“Grandmother, is he finally awake?”

A young woman came in with her arms full of fresh herbs. Dark brown hair tied back with a cloth. Eyes the green of spring leaves. She smiled at him, soft and relieved.

“You had us worried. I thought you might never wake.”

“My granddaughter, Lyora Ardynne Aurelisse,” said the old woman. “I am Arwen.”

“Esdel,” he murmured. The name rose from some deep well inside him — the only fragment of truth the desert hadn’t taken. “It’s all I remember.”

“Then it’s a good start,” Arwen said. “You’re in Edenridge, at the edge of the forest. Rest until your strength returns.”

Beyond the window, sunlight spilled across cobblestones. Villagers passed, laughing. A dog barked by the river. After so much death and sand, it felt unreal.

“It’s too peaceful,” he said.

“Peace is fragile,” said Arwen. “Hold on to it while you can.”

A sharp green smell rose near him. Around his wrist, where the sand had scoured the skin raw, a strip of linen had been wound neatly. Arwen had turned to the scarred table by the window and taken up a stone pestle. Slow, round grinding filled the room.

“What’s in it?” he asked. “On my wrist.”

“Plantain leaf. Honey. A little yarrow.”

“Why honey?”

“It keeps a wound clean.”

“And the yarrow?”

The pestle paused. “Three days asleep, child, and the first thing you want is my receipts.”

“I thought I should know,” he said. “Since I’m wearing them.”

“It stops the bleeding. And it stops young men asking questions, if they sleep on it long enough.”

By the shelves, Lyora had gone quiet, holding a sprig of rosemary and doing nothing with it.

“I tied that one,” she said. “The first night. I think I tied it too tight. Your hand went cold, and Grandmother had to undo it, and I—” She stopped. “I’m sorry.”

Esdel lifted the hand into the light and flexed it, one finger at a time, with great seriousness.

“Five. I counted. You didn’t lose any.”

For a heartbeat she only stared. Then a laugh slipped out of her, surprised, and her shoulders came down from where they had been hiding.

Arwen’s pestle kept its rhythm, but he thought he heard her breathe out through her nose, the way a person does when she has decided not to laugh.

---

That night he woke in the dark with a name on his tongue that was not his.

A single lamp burned low on the table. Lyora was asleep on a stool beside his cot, sitting upright, a damp cloth gone slack in her hand and her head tipped against the wall. She had not gone to bed. Of course she had not. Three nights she had sat there, and she had been sitting there when he woke at last, and she had said *you had us worried* as if it were nothing.

There was a faint, dried track down her cheek, silver in the lamplight.

He lay very still and looked at it.

He did not know who had cried over him. No one had ever — he did not know. He did not know of anyone who might have. But a stranger had come out of the desert with no name and no arm, and a girl had sat beside him until the tears dried on her face, and he lay there with a feeling he had no word for. It sat in his chest like warm water. It hurt.

She woke with a small start. Saw his open eyes. Scrubbed at her cheek with the back of her wrist, quickly, brusquely, the way you wipe off flour.

“Chamomile dust,” she said. “It gets everywhere.”

There was no chamomile on the stool. There was no chamomile in the room.

“Mm,” said Esdel.

She looked at him for a moment, as if she had heard what he had not said. Then she smiled — a small, crooked, grateful smile — and tucked the cloth back over his forehead, and he did not argue with her. He would learn, in time, that this was the first of many mornings and nights when it would be dust, or steam, or smoke from the lamp, and he would answer *mm*.

He did not lie to her. He only let her keep it.

---

The days after were slow ripples across still water.

He swept the floor, fetched water from the river, hung herbs from the rafters. Then Arwen gave him the jars — rows of them, clay and glass and stoppered horn, each with a scrap of paper pasted to its belly in her upright hand. He washed the empty ones and read the labels on the full ones while he worked.

Marshmallow root. Feverfew. Willow bark. Comfrey. Elder flower and elder berry, in separate jars, side by side like quarrelling sisters.

“Why separate?”

“Because one settles a cough,” said Arwen, “and the other, eaten raw, will turn a stomach inside out.”

He nodded and put that away somewhere.

The next morning, before the kettle had boiled, he stood before the shelves with his hands behind his back and said every name aloud, left to right, top to bottom. He missed none.

Arwen, coming in from the yard, stopped in the doorway and listened to the end.

“Hm,” she said.

It was, he would learn, one of her highest compliments.

The questions did not stop with the jars. Why willow for a fever and not for a cut. Why lavender hung upside down. Why this root was boiled and that one only steeped. Arwen answered each one without lifting her eyes. But one morning, around the ninth, she set her pestle down.

“Child. Do you know how long it took Lyora to ask me that many questions?”

“How long?”

“She’s still working on it.”

From the garden, Lyora’s voice floated in. “I heard that.”

When Arwen finally declared him recovered, it came not as ceremony but as a simple truth.

“You’re strong again. You may stay as long as you wish, Esdel — or leave when the road calls you back. The door will remain open.”

The words caught in his chest, heavy with something he couldn’t name. Gratitude. Or guilt, for warmth he hadn’t earned.

“I don’t know where else I could go,” he said.

“Then perhaps that’s your answer.”

Lyora appeared in the doorway with the sun behind her. “Come on. If you’re truly recovered, you can help me replant the garden. The roots won’t wait for indecision.”

He met her eyes, and for the first time since he had woken, something inside him shifted.

Something *alive.*

Beneath the peace, a pulse still echoed faintly under his skin. A whisper, patient and waiting.

*“Breathe while you can.”*
