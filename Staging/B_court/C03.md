# **Chapter 3**

Rain had returned to the capital, soft and unrelenting, as if the sky had settled into permanent sorrow.

The Lián estate stood shuttered behind it. The stone well was silent, its rim dark with moss. The garden Lord Hengzhi had loved had been left to grow wild, and the vines were suffocating his orchids. Inside, behind drawn silk, Lady Wei Anlan sat before an untouched cup of bitter tea, its surface holding the single flame like a small frozen moon.

She had not spoken a sentence of her own accord since the verdict. Some said she was in denial. Others, crueler, said she had lost her mind. The truth was simpler: her voice had forgotten how to sound in a world that no longer made sense.

A polished black carriage stopped at the gate.

“Lord Aldric Reng,” the footman announced.

It was a name once met in this house with scorn. Now the city said he had tried to save her husband, the only noble who pleaded before the court. The story seemed to spread by itself. It was a quieter, darker, and more precise thing than that.

She looked up when he came in, and for one moment something like fear crossed her eyes. Then it was replaced by weary politeness.

“I come to pay my respects, Lady Anlan.” He set a small lacquered box of rosewood on the table and opened it. Two vials of amber liquid lay in dark velvet, catching the candlelight. “My physician prepared these for you. For the sleeplessness, and the sorrow that clings after tragedy. They cannot replace what was lost. But they may dull the pain, enough for you to breathe again.”

Her fingers, skeletal beneath the shawl, trembled as she touched the glass.

“Sometimes I still expect him to walk through that gate,” she whispered, to the rain on the window. “I hear a carriage, and for one awful moment I am certain it is him. He promised he would come back to me.”

“And he will, in every memory, in every act of honor that bears his name.”

A single tear traced a path down her cheek. “They said you tried to save him. Is that true?”

He lowered his eyes to the box. “I begged. I risked every ounce of capital I had. But the court was set on blood.” He let the silence hang, thick and volatile, knowing this was the moment. “You must believe me.”

Slowly, she nodded. Her face relaxed for the first time, with the exhausted relief of finding an ally in a hostile world.

Outside, he stood a moment under the stone awning. *Pity is the easiest mask of all. It invites you inside the vault.*

Inside, Anlan stirred the amber drops into a cup with a spoonful of honey. The bitterness vanished. A gentle heat spread through her chest, and the calm that followed was almost blissful. When she opened her eyes again her thoughts were slow, wrapped in cotton. Only his voice remained, gentle and sorrowful, echoing in the quiet of her mind.

---

He did not stay away.

Each week at dusk, he came with something new: a second set of vials, a tincture for her headaches, a salve for the nights she paced until dawn. Never longer than fifteen minutes. Always long enough for his low, careful voice to fill the rooms and linger after he left.

The tonics soothed the trembling in her hands. They slowed the looping accusations in her thoughts and made the edges of memory dull. The hours she had once spent staring at the ceiling, remembering her husband, became hours of oblivion. She began to listen for the knock at the door, and the faint clink of glass.

The servants whispered that he had too much polish for sincerity. That he smelled opportunity, not sorrow. Lady Wei defended him with a quiet fierce conviction that surprised even herself. “He treats my grief as grief, not as gossip.” He never spoke Hengzhi’s name unless she did first.

When he missed a visit, the tremors came back. She would find herself waiting at the window over the drive, a posture she despised.

One dusk he came with empty hands and a sheaf of pale camellias. Their fingers brushed as she took them, and both of them knew what it meant. What began as gratitude blurred into affection, and affection, in a mind kept soft and slow, into a longing she took for love.

He proposed on a rain-soaked evening, when the manor was cold and his presence the only warmth. His steady hand lay over her trembling one. She did not hesitate. To return to the terror of her grief without him was unthinkable.

The kingdom gasped. Lady Wei Anlan, widow of the disgraced Lián Hengzhi, had married the very man who had stood as the witness to his downfall. It was too fast, too bold, too convenient. And yet in the quiet halls of the Lián estate, where sandalwood had burned for mourning, a new sound began to echo: Anlan’s light, silvery laughter.

To the world it was a story of redemption. In truth, the marriage gave Aldric the last of the Lián holdings tied to her dowry, the standing of an ancient house, and a perfect disguise. No one would question a man who had won forgiveness from the victim’s own wife.

She smiled beautifully beside him, her movements graceful, her eyes still touched by some distant, invisible sleep. The memories she cherished were not her own. They were artifacts.

She would never know how much of her gratitude, and her ultimate love, had been brewed drop by drop in the amber vials he once brought to her door.

---

Far to the north, a letter went out under the Count’s seal to the Supreme Commander. It was short, and respectful, and said only that a daughter might be allowed, in time, to come home and see her mother happy.

It would not be read as a favor. It would be read as an order from a man too useful to refuse.
