# **Chapter 4**

The week began with an uneasy rhythm. The beds filled faster, the groans grew heavier, and a faint stench clung to the air no matter how many times he cleaned.

Fever. Vomiting. Skin pale, lips dry. Not battle wounds. Illnesses.

The rations hadn’t changed. The herbs were measured, the instruments boiled. By the fourth day, more were dying of it than of the front.

That night he stood outside the tent, staring at moonlight on the water barrels. The smell — slightly metallic. The surface — cloudier than it should be.

“...It’s the water.”

Halden did not look up from his reports. “Nonsense. The source has been clean since last inspection.”

“That was nearly two months ago, sir. If the water’s poisoned, every patient you heal will return here again.”

Halden’s pen stopped. Then Benn, one of his young assistants, burst in with a flask drawn from the barrel. The water shimmered dull brown. “We filtered it twice, and it still reeks of metal. Even the livestock refuse to drink it.”

“Damn it all.” Halden turned. “Fine. You’ll check the source. But you’re not going alone. Four men go with you.”

They followed the stream uphill until it vanished into a narrow fissure in a cliff, a dark opening no wider than a man’s shoulders. The water was unnaturally cold and tasted of iron.

“We’ll go first,” said a soldier. “You stay behind us, Apothecary.”

One by one they squeezed through. The last struck a light, turned back, gave a thumbs-up, and vanished.

Then silence. A minute. Two. He called. No reply.

Esdel lit a torch and pressed into the gap himself. In the larger cavern beyond, the stench of blood and decay hit him first. The four soldiers lay motionless, their throats cut cleanly, their weapons untouched.

“...Who did this?”

“Who, indeed? The dog of the Kingdom dares to ask?”

A figure sat atop the bodies like a man upon a throne of corpses. He was young, perhaps Esdel’s age, in armor with no insignia. His eyes gleamed faintly crimson in the torchlight.

“I wondered what kind of fool they’d send to fetch their poison. But it’s just a healer.”

“You killed them.”

“I *freed* them. Better dead than serve that rotting kingdom.”

The blade was fast. It grazed Esdel just below the jaw, and warm blood ran down his neck. His own steel found the man’s chest — then the torch slipped and drowned in the water, and the dark closed over them.

“Don’t light it,” the voice said, soft, almost conversational. “I won’t attack. Why did you come here?”

“I didn’t come for war. I’m an apothecary. I came to save lives, not take them.”

A quiet laugh, faintly bitter. “Where are you from, healer?”

“A small village. Far from here.”

“What’s its name?”

He did not answer. The silence between them was neither trust nor hostility.

*He couldn’t see them before they lit the cave,* Esdel thought. *He isn’t used to the dark.*

He moved slowly, following memory, counting breaths, each exhale timed with the drip of water, until a faint wind brushed his cheek. Behind him the voice sharpened. “Coward! Come back and die properly!”

He slipped through the gap into daylight, soaked, bleeding at the neck, alive.

---

Captain Rorik, who commanded the post, sent fifty men with torches. They came back at dawn with pale faces and a covered wagon. Rotted flesh, human and animal fused together, rusted iron jutting from it like bones. The man himself was gone.

And the four soldiers. Their skin had turned dark, almost charred. Their eyes were pitch black. And every one of them wore the same expression: a smile. Wide. Unnatural.

Halden turned away, bile in his throat. Esdel stood still. The smile seemed to look *through* him, cold and knowing.

*Whatever that man was, he wasn’t alone.*

The smiling dead were burned at noon. No one in the tent spoke of them.

For a week afterward Halden roared at everyone — at Benn, who had tested the water too shallowly; at the stream; at the war — until his voice broke. Then he said, hoarse, “Just do your damn work. Fix what you can.”

They worked. By the week’s end the last fever patient limped out of the tent with a shaky salute. The tent stood still.

That night Halden carried out a pot of terrible coffee and a basket of skewered meat, and the three of them sat by the fire Esdel had made. “If you tell anyone about my private stash, I’ll make you scrub bedpans for a month.”

“Sir,” Benn gagged, “this is *terrible.*”

“It’s called *strong.*”

“You mixed antiseptic with sleeping tonic, Esdel,” Halden added.

“It was an experiment!”

“It was *attempted murder!*”

They laughed, real laughter, the kind that bubbles up from exhaustion. Then Benn stared at the sky. “What would happen if all this just stopped?”

“I’d sleep for three months,” Halden said.

“I’d go back home,” Esdel said. “My village is quiet. You can hear the wind in the wheat fields.”

“Sounds boring.”

“It is. But it’s peaceful.”

Halden looked at him a moment longer than the words deserved, and said nothing.

Later, when the others slept, Esdel sat alone with the fire going down. The cut under his jaw had stopped bleeding; it stung when he swallowed. He touched it, and thought of a certain pair of hands that would have pressed a clean cloth there, not gently, scolding, and held it a moment longer than needed.

He had refused the cave-man the name of his village. It was the only thing he had kept from a stranger in the dark. He had not been guarding a place. He had been guarding a lamp on a stair.
