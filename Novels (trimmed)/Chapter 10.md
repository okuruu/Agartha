# **Chapter 10**

He had been spending more time alone by the river lately. The ache in his chest had grown, not from labor but from something nameless, and sometimes tears escaped him without warning. He wiped them quickly. No one could see. Not here.

The cave, the monster, the arm gone and returned — and then the pulsing marks, gone too. Was any of it real?

A wild thought took hold of him: *What if I try again?*

His hands shook as he picked up a sharp stone from the riverbank. *If it doesn’t heal—* He thought of Lyora, of her worried face. *I can’t make her worry again.* But his hand moved anyway, and with a quick shallow cut he did it.

A sharp sting, a bead of red, and then more. It bled, warm and frightening. He dropped the stone in the river and wrapped the cut with a strip of his shirt and slipped home, avoiding their eyes.

In the dark attic he washed the wound with water boiled and cooled, the way Arwen insisted. Yarrow salve, thin. A folded pad. A strip wound firm but not tight, two fingers’ slack, the end tucked flat in Arwen’s small square knot. He did it all without thinking. His hands were careful with his arm in a way the rest of him had not been.

In the morning it had not healed.

No pulse of dark light. No miracle. Just blood, dried and human. *Maybe that’s all I am now. Just human.*

He heard Lyora’s soft footsteps below. “Esdel? You missed breakfast.” “I wasn’t hungry.” A pause. “Leave it by the stairs,” he said. “I’ll have it when I can.”

When he finally came down, she brightened too hard. “I was starting to think you’d turned into a ghost.” He tried to smile. “Not yet.” “Don’t joke like that.”

“You hurt yourself,” she said.

A tiny bloom of red had soaked through his sleeve. “It’s seen to.”

“Esdel. Please.”

He held out his arm. Her fingers stopped at the knot first. Small, square, tucked flat. Her grandmother’s knot. She unwound it, and lifted the pad, and there beneath it was the green sheen of yarrow salve, spread thin. The cut was clean. Straight. Shallow. Deliberate.

“You did this to yourself,” she whispered.

Her voice sharpened, and broke. “And then you dressed it. Yarrow, thin. A pad. Two fingers’ slack. Grandmother’s knot.” A sound that was not quite a laugh caught in her throat. “You did everything right. You knew exactly what you were doing.”

“It’s not what you think.”

“Then tell me what it is.”

He could not. *I wanted to see if I could still heal* sounded ridiculous even in his own head, and it was not the whole truth, and he would not give her half of it dressed as the whole. So he said nothing, staring at the floor.

“You’ve been acting strange for days. Keeping to yourself. Not eating. And now this—”

“Lyora. Let it go.”

“You can’t ask me to. You’re bleeding. You’re—”

“Please. Not now.” It came out louder than he meant, and the silence after was suffocating.

She flinched. She stepped back. She looked at him with an expression he would remember for a long time — she did not believe him, but she would stop anyway. Then she took his arm again, and began to rewrap it with gentle hands.

He watched her do it. Her head was bent, and her hair had fallen forward, and a drop fell onto the linen and darkened it. Then another. She did not make a sound. Her hands did not shake. The bandage grew a small grey bloom of water, then two, and her fingers went on smoothing it flat.

“Kettle steam,” she said, to the bandage. “It gets in my eyes.”

The kettle had not been on since dawn.

He said nothing. He had nothing that was true and also kind. He sat with his arm in her hands and let her weep onto his wound, and he felt each drop like a hot coal on bare skin, and it was a greater pain than the stone had been. *I did this,* he thought. *I took a sharp thing to my own arm, and this is what it cost her.*

“You shouldn’t bear things alone,” she whispered, and tied the knot.

“I’ll be careful next time.”

“You always say that.” She looked at him for a long moment, as if searching his face for something that wasn’t there anymore. Then she gathered the soiled cloth and left, and he heard the pump in the yard go on and on, running water that did not drown out anything at all.

That night the wound throbbed under its bandage, slow and hot, as if something beneath it were waking. And for the first time since he arrived in the village, Esdel was truly afraid. Not of monsters. Not of death. Of what was growing inside him, and what would happen when the truth could no longer be hidden — and of the girl who would be standing closest when it broke.

---

In the morning the wound was gone.

No scar. No blood. Clean, unbroken skin. He dug his nails into his forearm, almost hoping for pain to prove he was real, and there was pain, warm and sharp, but no mark.

From below came laughter — Lyora’s, bright and soft, tangled with Arwen’s. She had put her face together again overnight. He did not know how it was done. He envied it, and was ashamed of the envy.

At the market Marin the miller was unloading grain, and grinned at him. “You’ll tire yourself out before the Festival of Wishing Threads even begins.”

“Wishing Threads?”

“Ah, right, you’ve never seen it. Once a year, when the twin stars cross above our valley, we hang silken threads across every street — red for love, gold for fortune, blue for health, white for remembrance. They say if you tie your hope beneath the starlight, the heavens will listen. The first night we hang the threads. The second, everyone writes a wish on a ribbon and ties it to the great tree.” He winked. “Plenty of girls who’d happily tie a thread with a quiet lad like you.”

Esdel laughed. It felt hollow.
