# **Chapter 85**

They planned it in the old stillroom behind the stables, where Lord Hengzhi’s grandmother had made plum wine and Esdel had made the tonic that gave a dead woman six days.

Eight of them, around a scarred table, by the light of one shuttered lamp. The General. Changli. Han Ruo. Pei Lun, who had not been invited and had come anyway, and whom no one had the heart to send away. Suyin, the archer — a narrow, quiet woman of forty with grey in her braid, who had not spoken ten words to Esdel on the whole road from North Ridge, and who spoke no more now, but listened with her whole body, the way a hunting cat listens. Two others of the Lián guard whom Han Ruo trusted with his life, and had said so.

And Esdel.

“It has to be soon,” said Xueyao. “Before my leave ends. Before he finds a way to make my answer not matter.” She laid her hands flat on the table. “Eight days from now is the new moon.”

No one argued.

---

It was not complicated. That was the thing that frightened Esdel most, afterward — how simple it was, laid out on a scarred table in the lamplight. How few the moving pieces were. How easily any one of them could break.

The house would sleep. That was his part.

“How many?” said Han Ruo.

“Everyone who eats from the main kitchen. The Count’s household — the steward, the maids, his guards, the gatekeepers.” Esdel had it written out, in his small strange hand, on a sheet of paper that would be burned before dawn. “It can go into the evening soup. Valerian and poppy and a measure of greyveil. Not much. Enough to make a strong man sleep like a child for six hours, and wake with a headache and no memory of going to bed.” He paused. “No one will be harmed. I’ve measured it for the smallest of them. The kitchen girl who brings the bread. She’s twelve.”

He did not say: *I have measured it four times. I have checked it against my notes, and against the notes from the Eastern Post, and against the memory of a young soldier in a tent whose muscles went quiet one by one until the ones that breathed.* He did not say it. Changli looked at him across the table, and he knew she heard it anyway.

“The cook?” said Suyin. The first word she had spoken.

“She won’t know. I’ll give it to Changli. Changli will see to the pot.”

Changli inclined her head.

“And the Count,” said Han Ruo.

Esdel was silent a moment.

“The Count,” he said, “doesn’t eat from the main kitchen. He never has. Master Vale prepares his meals himself, in the west wing.” He looked down at the paper. “I can’t reach him. Not without being seen.”

The lamp hissed.

“Then he’ll be awake,” said Pei Lun, very quietly.

“He sleeps,” said Xueyao. “Every night, from the second watch to the fifth. His bedchamber is on the far side of the house, above the west gallery. He walks beneath the peach tree before dawn. I’ve watched him for weeks.” She did not look up from the table. “If we are quick, and quiet, he won’t know until the fifth watch. By then we’ll be through the gates.”

“If,” said Han Ruo.

“If,” she agreed.

---

The nursery was the second part.

Yueming slept in a small room at the end of the south gallery, with a wet-nurse in the room beside him and one of the Count’s guards on a stool outside the door. Both would have eaten the soup. Xueyao would go in herself.

“And then?” said Han Ruo. “A child that age. On horseback, at night, with the whole city behind us—”

“He doesn’t come with us,” said Xueyao.

The room went silent.

She lifted her head and looked at Changli.

Changli had known. Esdel saw it — saw that she had known before any of them, perhaps since the night beneath the peach tree, perhaps before. She was sitting very straight in her chair, with her hands folded on the table, and her beautiful face had gone perfectly calm.

“He’ll be looking for me,” said Xueyao. “The whole Crown will be looking for me. A general, a dragon-maiden, riding out of the capital with a stolen child. There isn’t a road in the Kingdom I could take him down that they wouldn’t follow.” She held Changli’s eyes. “But no one will be looking for a lady’s maid. A young widow, say, with a baby, going home to her family in the south. By the river road. With a quiet woman for a servant.” She glanced at Suyin, who nodded once. “Not north. Not east. Somewhere so small and so far that the name of Lián has never been heard there.”

Changli did not move.

“You’ll raise him,” said Xueyao. “Far from this city. Far from me. You’ll give him another name. You’ll teach him to read and to ride and to be kind, and you won’t tell him who he is until he’s old enough to choose what to do with it.” Her voice did not waver. “Let him live free of our name, Changli. That’s all I want for him. That’s all my mother wanted.”

For a long moment Changli said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the stables, a horse shifted in its stall.

Then she said, very quietly, “You’re sending me away.”

“I’m giving you the only thing I have left that matters.”

“You’re sending me *away*.” Changli’s voice broke, just slightly, on the last word. “Seventeen years. Since we were six. I followed you to the front. I followed you into the *mud*, Xueyao, into the camp, into the — and now you want me to go down the river with your brother and a stranger’s name and never—”

She stopped. She pressed her lips together. Her folded hands on the table were white.

Xueyao reached across the table and put her own hand over them.

It was the first time Esdel had ever seen her touch anyone except her mother.

“Yes,” she said. “I want you to go. Because you’re the only one I trust with him. Because you’re the cleverest person I have ever known. Because you will lie to every soldier and every magistrate and every innkeeper between here and the sea, and smile while you do it, and they will thank you.” Her voice softened, very slightly. “And because if you stay with me, you’ll die. And I’ve buried enough.”

Changli looked down at the hand over hers.

Then she turned her own hand over, and gripped Xueyao’s, hard, and held it — and did not say anything else at all.

---

The third part was the gates.

Esdel sat through it without speaking. Horses in the stable yard, saddled after dark. The river gate for Changli and Suyin and the baby, the great gate for the rest. The streets of the eastern quarter. The north gate of the city, and the watch there, and how many, and when the watch changed. The roads beyond. Han Ruo drew them on the scarred table with a finger dipped in plum-wine dregs, and the lines glistened in the lamplight and slowly dried.

“The watch at the north gate is twelve men,” Han Ruo said. “Crown soldiers. Not the Count’s. We can’t drug them and we can’t bribe them. If they won’t open—”

“Then we go through,” said Xueyao.

No one said anything after that.

---

When the others had gone, one by one, out into the dark, Esdel stayed behind to burn the papers.

He fed them into the lamp flame, one by one, the way he had fed the letters. The plan for the soup. The list of the household. The measures of valerian and poppy and greyveil, written out four times in his small strange hand.

When he had finished, he sat for a while at the scarred table, with the ash in the dish and the lamp burning low.

Then he took a fresh sheet of paper from his coat, and the stub of charcoal, and wrote.

He had not meant to. He had told himself, the night the Count came to his room, that he would not write to her again. That a letter was a thread, and a thread could be followed. He had burned all the others. He knew it was foolish.

He wrote anyway. It came out of him the way the knots came out of his hands — without deciding, from somewhere beneath memory.

He did not write about the Count, or the tonic, or the funeral, or the plan. He did not write where he was going, because he did not know. He wrote about the peach tree. How old it was, and how it was bound with iron where the storm had split it, and how it had flowered anyway, every spring, for longer than anyone living could remember. He wrote that the blossoms had fallen now and the fruit was setting, small and hard and green. He wrote that he thought she would have liked it.

He wrote that he was sorry.

He did not write what for. There were too many things. He did not know how to put them in order.

And at the end, after a long time with the charcoal lifted above the page, he wrote one more line.

Then he read the letter through, once, by the low lamp. And he folded it in half, and in half again. And he held it over the flame.

He held it there for a long time. The edge of the paper browned. Began to curl.

He took it away.

He sat looking at it — the browned corner, the faint smell of scorching — with his heart doing something slow and heavy in his chest.

Then he unfolded it again, and tore it very carefully in half, and in half again, and again, until it was pieces too small to hold a word. And he fed the pieces into the flame, one by one, and watched each one go.

All but one.

The last piece, he kept. It was very small — a corner, no bigger than his thumbnail. There was nothing on it but a single word, in his small strange hand, from the very end of the letter.

He looked at it for a long time in the lamplight. Then he tucked it into the silver chain at his throat, behind the little flower, where it lay against his skin.

He did not know if that was foolish too. He thought it probably was.

He blew out the lamp and went out into the dark.
