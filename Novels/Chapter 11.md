# **Chapter 11**

By dusk on the festival’s first night the village was transformed. Silk crisscrossed between the rooftops, glimmering in the twilight. Lanterns swayed from beams and branches, each flame caught like a captive star. Above the lane by the apothecary, blue threads hung thickest — blue for health. Arwen’s doing, he suspected.

Esdel stood at the edge of it with a basket of ginger packets on his arm for anyone who looked green.

Lyora was in the crowd in pale blue, her hair braided loosely down her back, a small flower tucked behind her ear. She was helping a child tie a ribbon high above their heads, and her smile was one he had not seen in days.

For a moment he forgot to breathe.

She caught him staring. She hesitated, then lifted a small, uncertain wave. He gave one back. It felt fragile on his lips.

He didn’t go to her. He couldn’t. Not with the thing beneath his skin, not with the wound that healed without reason. As the twin stars crossed above them, their light spilling down in threads, he whispered:

“If I were to make a wish — I’d wish I could still be who she thinks I am.”

Something deep inside stirred, a pulse, faint but certain, and the air around him shivered.

---

The next evening, the second night, they went together to write their wishes on ribbons for the great tree. Arwen walked ahead, then fell into talk with an old friend, and waved them on. “Go on, you two.”

They walked side by side into the crowd, their silence heavy with unspoken things. At a stall of glowing petal-lanterns he said, without thinking, “They use heat and air to rise. A pocket of warm current. Nothing magical. Just physics.”

“Physics?” she said, grinning. “You always say such strange things.”

“It’s logical.”

“Then explain why you always look so serious when you work.”

“Because I’m thinking.” A smile betrayed him, and her laughter brushed against something long dormant in him. Her teasing no longer struck at his uncertainty. It mended it, thread by thread, like warmth stitching through an old wound.

Then, a familiar voice. “There you are!” Eldric strode toward them with his sleeves rolled and his smile sunlit. “Come on, they’re waiting for you!” He caught Lyora’s hand to lead her off. She glanced back, lips parting.

“Is it all right if I go?”

Esdel’s throat tightened. “Go on. They’re waiting.”

She searched his face for permission, or reassurance, and found only restraint. “Have fun,” he said.

She turned. Halfway there she tried, with quiet grace, to slip her hand free. Eldric’s hold only tightened, unaware, and a small unease crossed her face.

Esdel stood where she had left him, with the ribbons above whispering other people’s wishes and his own heart wordless.

A tug at his sleeve. A little girl held up a finger, red and shining where she had touched a petal lantern. He knelt. “The lantern wanted to hold your hand too.” He cooled the finger in the rain barrel and dabbed on calendula and blew on it. “There. Now it smells like a flower. Don’t let any bees find out.” She ran off with her finger held high, and before she went she tied a ribbon on his wrist: *strength.* She tied it herself, tongue between her teeth.

He found Reyn on the hilltop with a cup of fruit wine in each hand, and they watched the fireworks open red and gold over the square.

“You know,” Reyn said later, “sometimes the ones who try hardest to help forget they’re allowed to be happy too.”

Esdel did not answer. When he lifted his eyes again, he saw her.

---

Lyora was crossing the square below with a folded ribbon in her hand, Eldric and their friends in tow, their laughter easy and carefree. As if drawn, she looked back. Their eyes met across the distance — one heartbeat, two — and the noise of the festival fell away.

Then she turned again, and the moment slipped like sand through his hands.

Esdel stayed on the hill until the square emptied. The lanterns dimmed one by one. When it was nearly dark he went down alone, by a lane that took him past the great tree, for no reason he could have named.

She was there.

Alone, under the branches, with a ribbon in her fingers. She was not tying it. She stood with her forehead against the rough trunk, and one hand flat against the bark, and her shoulders were shaking with a grief so quiet it made no more sound than the leaves. The wishes of the whole village hung over her, red and gold and blue and white. She had written hers. It was folded small in her fist, and she held it against her mouth as if to keep something in.

Esdel stopped in the dark at the end of the lane.

He could have gone to her. Every part of him leaned. But a woman who cries alone under a tree at the end of a festival does not want to be found, and he knew that the way he knew his name — because it was the only thing he had ever been sure of — and he let her have it. He stood in the shadow, hands useless at his sides, and kept watch over her silence as if it were something fragile that might be broken by a footstep.

At last she straightened. She wiped her face with both hands, quickly, and tied the ribbon on a low branch with a small hard knot. She pressed her palms to her cheeks and took three long breaths, and then she smiled — a bright, practiced smile, put on like a shawl — and walked home to her grandmother with her head up.

He did not look to see what the ribbon said.

He looked, instead, at the two threads in the wind above it, silver and blue, twining together for only a breath before the night took them apart.

How close they came, he thought. And how quietly they drifted away.

Back in the attic he turned his hand over in the moonlight and watched the child’s ribbon sway with his pulse. *Strength.* He did not feel strong. He felt like a man who had stood a long time in the cold outside a lit window, and had called it kindness.
