# **Chapter 91**

They rode north all morning, and the Count rode after them.

They saw him first at mid-morning, from the top of a long rise — a line of dark specks on the road behind them, three or four miles back, coming on steadily through the wet green country. Thirty riders. Perhaps more. The sun had come out behind the thinning clouds, and it glinted, now and then, on something at the head of the line. A helmet. A sword. The white of a mourning coat, blazing like a flag.

“He came himself,” said Pei Lun. His voice was hoarse. He had not stopped crying since the Street of Lanterns, not really — only quietly, steadily, the tears running down his bruised face while he rode, as though they belonged to someone else. “He came himself. After—”

“Yes,” said Xueyao.

She did not look back again. She only lifted her hand, and they rode on.

---

The horses were tired. That was the trouble. They had been saddled in a storm and ridden hard through the streets and out through the gate and on up the north road without a halt, and they were blowing now, lathered and stumbling, their heads low. The Count’s horses were fresh. Esdel did not need to be told that. He could see it in the way the dark line behind them grew, hour by hour — slowly, slowly, but always growing.

By noon it was two miles back. By early afternoon, one.

They came down out of the hills into a broad green valley with a river running through it, wide and shallow and fast, brown with the night’s storm, loud over its stones. The road ran down to a ford — a long, pale causeway of gravel and flat stone, just under the surface of the water, marked on either side by old leaning posts. On the far bank, the road climbed again into a dark belt of pine forest.

“If we reach the trees,” said one of the old soldiers — Gao, a lean, grey, silent man who had not spoken all day — “we can lose them. The forest’s thick past the ridge. There are hunting paths.”

“If we reach the trees,” said another.

They rode down to the ford.

They were halfway across, the water surging white around the horses’ knees, when Esdel heard the horns behind them. He looked back. The Count’s riders were coming over the last rise into the valley — close now, very close, so close he could see the faces under the helmets, and the wine-colored livery, and at their head, on the tall grey horse, the white coat and the silver hair and the sword.

Half a mile. Less.

“*Go,*” said Gao.

He had turned his horse in the middle of the ford. So had two others — the oldest ones, Esdel saw, the ones with the grey in their beards and the old wounds that ached in the damp. They sat their horses in the rushing water, side by side, facing back the way they had come.

Xueyao reined in. “Gao—”

“The ford’s narrow, General. Three men can hold it a while.” Gao drew his sword. His lean face was perfectly calm. “Han Ruo would have done the same. You know he would.” He did not look at her. “Get to the trees.”

Xueyao stared at him.

Then she turned away. She turned her face toward the far bank and the dark pines, and spurred her horse, and did not look back. Esdel saw her face as she passed him. He would not forget it.

The others went after her. Pei Lun, sobbing aloud now. The four remaining soldiers.

Esdel did not.

---

He did not decide to stay. He would think about that afterward, a great deal. He did not decide. He only found that his horse had stopped in the middle of the ford, in the rushing brown water, beside the three old men — and that his hand had gone, by itself, to the satchel at his side. To the small glass vial at the bottom of it, wrapped in linen, that had lain there untouched since the Eastern Post.

*Emergency stimulant,* Halden had said. *Don’t use it unless you have to. And if you do, try not to die afterward.*

Gao looked at him sidelong. “Apothecary. Go.”

“No,” said Esdel.

He did not know why. Except that he had watched Han Ruo turn his horse beneath the carved arch of the Lián gate, and heard him laughing in the dark, and then heard the bell stop. And he had ridden away. And he found that he could not do it twice.

Gao looked at him a moment longer. Then something in his lean grey face eased, almost into a smile.

“The General’s going to be very angry with you,” he said.

“I know.”

The Count’s riders hit the water.

---

It was not like the forest. Not like the burning plain. There was no time to be afraid, and no time to think, and the world was only noise — the roar of the river and the screaming of horses and the shouting of men, and water everywhere, surging white and brown and cold, dragging at the legs, blinding the eyes. The Count’s riders came into the ford four abreast, as many as the causeway would carry, and the three old soldiers of House Lián met them in the middle, and the first rank broke on them like a wave on rocks.

Esdel saw Gao take a man out of the saddle with a single cut. He saw the old soldier beside him go down under two blades, and the water close over him. He saw the third — the oldest — hold the left edge of the causeway alone, his horse rearing, his sword going up and down, up and down, as steady as a man chopping wood.

Then a spear came out of the press and went into Esdel’s side.

He felt it go in. Low, on the left, below the ribs. A great flat blow, like being kicked by a horse, and then a heat, spreading. He looked down and saw the shaft of it standing out of him, and the water around his horse’s legs going red.

He did not fall. He did not know why he did not fall.

For one moment — only one — he was not in the river.

Mint. Honey. Not too hot. A small gold flame on the second step, turned low. A girl on the floor at the foot of the stairs with her face ruined and her fist curled in his shirt, saying it over and over into his neck until it was not a word anymore. *You promised. You promised.*

*I know,* he thought. *I know. I’m trying.*

His hand was in the satchel. His fingers closed on the vial. He brought it out, and bit the wax seal off with his teeth, and drank it — all of it, in one swallow. It tasted of nothing. Cold, and faintly blue, like biting into ice.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened.

Then everything did.

---

It came up through him like fire through dry grass. Not the Veil — not the slow, careful, deliberate thing he had taught himself in the tent at the Eastern Post, with the blink and the breath and the needles in his skull. This was the other thing. The first thing. The thing that had come on him in the forest with seven arrows in his body, when he had thought he was going to die.

He *was* going to die. Some part of him knew it, very clearly. The spear had gone deep. And that part of him — the part beneath memory, the part that had moved his hands through every wound and every ambush of the last year — rose up, all at once, through the blue cold fire of Halden’s vial, and opened its eyes.

The world stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped.* The river hung in the air in great frozen sheets of brown and white, every droplet a bead of glass. The horses hung mid-rear, their manes spread like wings. The men hung with their mouths open and their swords raised and their faces caught in the middle of a shout. The light was red. Everything was red — the water, the sky, the pines on the far bank — as though the whole world had been painted in blood.

And he could see.

He could see every heart in the ford. Every one. Thirty small red lights beating in the frozen world, each at its own pace. He could see which were afraid. He could see which were angry. He could see which would strike next, and where, and how hard — the lance on the left already committed, the sword on the right a half-breath behind it, the oldest soldier’s guard sagging on the side where his shoulder had been broken years ago. Every movement in the ford was written in the bodies before it came.

He looked for the Count.

The Count was in the second rank. On his grey horse, in his white coat, with his sword raised and his face — Esdel saw it, frozen in the red light — alight with a kind of fierce, radiant joy. The look of the men in the field tents in their last hour. The fever-brightness.

And his heart—

Esdel stopped.

Of the thirty lights in the ford, twenty-nine were afraid. Even Gao’s. Even his own.

The Count’s was not. It beat slow and even and unhurried, the heart of a man sitting down to supper — while his face, above it, blazed with that terrible joy. The two did not belong together. Esdel had seen that look before, on the pallets in the field tents. He had seen it, softer, on a woman in a pavilion, turning toward a man like a flower toward the light.

Esdel stared at him.

Then the frozen world began, very slowly, to move again, and he did not have time to think about it anymore.

---

He moved through it the way he had moved through the rain in the Street of Lanterns — but faster, much faster, a blade through water. The spear was still in him. He did not feel it. He felt only the cold blue fire, and the red light, and the slow terrible drift of the world around him.

He did not go for the Count’s heart. He could have. It was there in front of him, beating slow and even and unafraid, and his short sword was in his hand, and there was no one in the frozen ford who could have stopped him. Afterward, lying in the dark, he would think about that for a long time. About how easy it would have been. About Han Ruo, and Gao, and Lady Anlan with her folded hands, and a dead lord with no grave.

He did not go for the heart.

He went for the hand. The right hand, on the raised sword, with the pale scar across the back of it — the theatrical scar, the costume scar. His blade went in across the back of the wrist, clean and exact, the way he had once opened a festering wound in the tent at the Eastern Post. He felt it part the cords beneath the skin.

And then the grey horse. He put his shoulder into its chest as he passed, in the frozen world, with all the weight of his body and the cold blue fire behind it. He felt the great animal go back and over, slowly, slowly, its forelegs lifting from the causeway, its head coming up, the Count’s face turning toward him in the red light with the joy just beginning to leave it.

Then Esdel blinked, and the world came crashing back.

---

Noise. Water. Pain — pain like nothing he had ever known, pain in his side and his skull and his eyes, pain so vast it did not feel like pain at all but like weather. He was on his knees on the causeway in the rushing water. He did not know how he had got there. The spear was gone; he had pulled it out, or it had fallen. There was blood in his mouth. There was blood running out of his eyes.

And in front of him, in the ford, the grey horse was down.

It had gone over backward into the deep water off the edge of the causeway, and the current had it — the Count had it, was tangled with it, was going under and coming up and going under again in a white welter of foam, his mouth open, his right hand flailing uselessly, the sword gone. His riders were screaming. Not at Esdel. At the river. At their lord. They were throwing themselves off their horses into the deep water, grabbing at the white coat, at the silver hair, at the reins of the drowning horse — five of them, eight, ten, the whole front of the charge turning in on itself in the middle of the ford, all of them, every one, forgetting the fugitives and the General and the war and everything in the world except the man going under in the brown water.

*Of course,* Esdel thought, very clearly, through the pain. *Of course they would. He’s the one who pays them.*

Someone had his arm. Someone was dragging him up.

Gao. Bleeding from the scalp, his lean grey face streaked red, his horse gone. He had Esdel’s arm over his shoulders and was hauling him along the causeway, through the surging water, toward the far bank.

“*Idiot,*” Gao was saying, over and over, through his teeth. “Idiot. Idiot. The General’s going to kill you. Idiot.”

Esdel tried to say something. It came out as blood.

Behind them, the oldest soldier — the one who had held the left edge alone — was still sitting his horse in the middle of the ford. He was not fighting anymore. There was no one left to fight; they were all in the river with their lord. He was only sitting there, very straight, with his sword across his knees, watching them go. His heart, Esdel had seen, in the red light, had been very short. Shorter than Gao’s.

He raised his sword to them, once, in salute.

Then he turned his horse’s head back toward the Count’s men, and the river, and waited.

---

Esdel did not remember reaching the far bank. He did not remember the pines. He remembered only a voice — the General’s, very close, very sharp, saying his name over and over in a tone he had never heard her use. And hands on his side, pressing hard. And Pei Lun somewhere, crying. And the smell of pine needles and blood and river water. And over all of it, in the back of his skull, the cold blue fire going out — slowly, slowly, like a lamp being turned down — until there was only dark.

In the dark, before he went all the way down into it, he felt his hand go to his throat. To the silver chain. The small flower. The scrap of paper tucked behind it, with one word on it, in his own strange hand.

It was still there.

*Good,* he thought.

And then nothing at all.
