# **Chapter 20**

Esdel had been spending more time alone lately. After his work in the apothecary ended for the day, he would slip away to the riverbank, where reeds swayed gently and the water shimmered like glass. It was peaceful there — but that peace was starting to feel heavy, like a weight pressing down on his chest.

This evening, he sat on a smooth rock, clutching at his shirt, fingers digging faintly into his skin. The ache in his chest had grown worse lately, not from labor, but from something deeper, something nameless. He didn’t know why, but sometimes tears would escape his eyes without warning. He wiped them quickly, forcing his expression back to calm. No one could see. Not here.

His mind kept circling back to the memory of the cave, the monster, the pain of losing his arm — and then… nothing. He remembered the spot that had once pulsed where the wound should be, but that too was gone now. Was it real? Was any of it real?

A wild thought gripped him: *What if I try again?*

His hands trembled as he picked up a sharp stone from the river’s edge. The idea terrified him — it felt insane — but he needed to know. Maybe the monster had been a dream. Maybe the regrown arm had been nothing but an illusion.

He held the stone above his arm, his heart pounding. His breath came shallow and fast. *If I’m wrong… if it doesn’t heal…* He thought of Lyora, of how she had taken care of him when he first arrived. Her worried expression flickered in his mind. *I can’t make her worry again.*

But his hand still moved. Slowly. Shaking. The stone pressed against his skin, cold and rough. For a heartbeat, he hesitated — every instinct screaming at him to stop.

Then, with a quick, shallow cut, he did it.

A sharp sting, a bead of red, and then more. The pain was real. The wound was real. It bled, warm and frightening, down his arm. He dropped the stone into the river and clutched at the cut, his breath trembling. It hadn’t sealed.

He wrapped it with a strip of his shirt and slipped back into the village quietly, avoiding Arwen and Lyora’s gaze. Climbing the stairs to his small attic room, he moved slowly, each step sending a pulse of pain through his arm.

He lit no lamp. His hands knew the way.

He poured water into the basin from the jug he kept filled from the kettle — boiled, then cooled, the way Arwen insisted — and washed the cut until the water ran pink, and then clear. He pressed it dry with clean linen. From the little tin by the bed he smoothed on yarrow salve, thin, never thick. *So the wound can breathe, child.* A folded pad of linen over it. Then a strip, wound firm but not tight, two fingers’ slack, the end tucked flat and tied off in Arwen’s small square knot.

He did it all without thinking. That was the strange part. His hands were careful with his arm in a way the rest of him had not been.

Lying on his bed, he turned away from the door, pressing his face into the pillow. He couldn’t let them see. He couldn’t explain. His body writhed as the pain burned through him — but he bore it, silent, until exhaustion finally took him.

Morning came pale and uncertain. The sunlight through the attic window was soft, almost hesitant, dust floating lazily in the air like drifting memory. Esdel stirred beneath the thin blanket, his body heavy with a dull ache that pulsed in time with the wound beneath his sleeve.

When he finally sat up, his arm throbbed sharply. The linen had darkened to a rusty brown at its center. He unwound it carefully, wincing where the pad had stuck to his skin. The cut was shallow, but raw and angry, an ugly reminder of his foolishness.

He stared at it for a long while.

It didn’t heal.

There was no pulse of dark light, no strange warmth, no miracle that mended flesh the way it had before. Just blood, dried and human. His heart clenched.

*Maybe that’s all I am now. Just human.*

He rewrapped the wound quickly — fresh salve, a fresh pad, the same small knot — when he heard the floorboards creak below — the sound of Lyora’s footsteps. She always moved softly in the mornings, careful not to wake anyone. He used to find that sound comforting. Now it only made him tense, a reminder of what he was hiding.

“Esdel?” her voice came from the bottom of the stairs, light but edged with concern. “You’re awake, aren’t you?”

He hesitated before answering. “Yeah. Just a bit slow today.”

“You missed breakfast. Are you okay?”

“I wasn’t hungry.”

There was a pause. Then, quietly, “You should still eat something. Grandmother made soup.”

“Leave it by the stairs,” he said. “I’ll have it when I can.”

It was true. He only did not know when that would be.

Her footsteps lingered, then retreated, fading into the soft clatter of jars and glass downstairs. The faint scent of herbs filled the air — mint, dried hyssop, crushed petals. Normally, that smell meant safety. Now, it only made the room feel smaller.

He stayed there until the light shifted, until the attic was half-shadow and his arm burned beneath its bandage. Then he forced himself to move, to act as if nothing was wrong.

When he finally came down, Lyora was sorting dried leaves by the counter. Her expression brightened faintly when she saw him — the kind of small smile that tried too hard to reach her eyes.

“You finally came down,” she said. “I was starting to think you turned into a ghost.”

Esdel tried to return the smile, but it faltered halfway. “Not yet,” he murmured. “Guess you’re stuck with me a little longer.”

She laughed softly, but there was a strange stiffness in it. “Don’t joke like that.”

He busied himself with the mortar and pestle, pretending not to notice how her gaze lingered on his arm. He could feel it — that quiet scrutiny, like she sensed something off.

“You hurt yourself,” she said suddenly.

His heart jolted. “What?”

She nodded toward his sleeve. A tiny bloom of red had soaked through the fabric near his wrist.

Esdel froze. Then he forced a laugh, awkward and brittle. “Ah, that. Just a scratch. I— I dropped something sharp last night.”

Lyora didn’t look convinced. “Let me see.”

“It’s fine.”

“Esdel.” Her tone was firmer now — still gentle, but edged with the authority she inherited from Arwen. “Please.”

He hesitated, then slowly extended his arm. Her fingers stopped at the knot first.

Small. Square. Tucked flat. Her grandmother’s knot.

She unwound the strip slowly, and lifted the folded pad, and there beneath it was the faint green sheen of yarrow salve, spread thin. The cut was clean. Washed. Not a thread of cloth in it.

When she saw the cut itself — straight, shallow, deliberate — her breath hitched faintly.

“This isn’t just a scratch,” she whispered. “You did this to yourself.”

Then, before he could speak, her voice sharpened. “And then you dressed it.” She held up the linen pad between two fingers. “Washed it. Yarrow, thin. A pad. Two fingers’ slack. Grandmother’s knot.” Something that was not quite a laugh caught in her throat. “You did everything right, Esdel. Everything. You knew exactly what you were doing.”

Esdel’s jaw tightened. “It’s not what you think.”

“Then tell me what it is.”

He opened his mouth, but no words came. The truth — *I wanted to see if I could still heal* — sounded ridiculous even in his own head. So he stayed silent, staring at the floor.

Lyora’s voice trembled. “You’ve been acting strange for days. Keeping to yourself. Not eating. And now this—”

“Lyora,” he cut in, his voice barely holding steady. “Just… let it go.”

Her eyes flashed with something — hurt, frustration, fear. “You can’t ask me to do that. You’re bleeding, Esdel. You’re—”

“I said I’m fine.” His voice rose without meaning to, and the silence that followed was immediate, suffocating.

Lyora flinched, then stepped back, her lips parting as if to say something — but she didn’t. She only looked at him, her eyes glimmering with the kind of sadness that said *she didn’t believe him, but she would stop anyway.*

She turned away quietly, rewrapping his arm with gentle hands, not saying another word. When she finished, she whispered, “You shouldn’t bear things alone.”

Esdel wanted to respond. To tell her everything — about the cave, the monster, the arm that vanished and returned. About how he woke sometimes feeling like something inside him didn’t belong. But when he looked at her — at the soft tremor of her fingers, the quiet care in her movements — he couldn’t.

Because if he told her, she’d worry again. And he couldn’t stand the thought of seeing that same fear in her eyes.

So he only said, “I’ll be careful next time.”

Lyora looked at him for a long moment, as if searching for something in his face that wasn’t there anymore. Then she smiled faintly, the kind of smile that didn’t reach her heart. “You always say that.”

That night, Esdel couldn’t sleep. The wound pulsed beneath its bandage, slow and hot, as if something beneath his skin was waking. Outside, the wind whispered through the eaves, carrying the distant sound of the river — the same place where he’d cut himself.

He thought of Lyora again — her steady hands, her trembling voice. The way she looked at him as if he were slipping away and she couldn’t stop it.

And for the first time since he arrived in the village, Esdel felt truly afraid. Not of monsters, not of death — but of what might be growing inside him, and what might happen when the truth could no longer be hidden.
