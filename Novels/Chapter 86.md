# **Chapter 86**

Grand Duke Rowan Hale did his arithmetic at night.

It was a habit of forty years. In the day there were petitions, and councils, and ministers with their endless small hungers, and men like Aldric Reng patting his arm with two fingers in front of three hundred people. In the day he smiled and said very little and let other men talk themselves into corners. But at night, in the high study of his house on the western hill, with the windows open to the sound of the river and a single lamp on the desk, he sat alone with a sheet of paper and a pen, and worked out what everything was worth.

Tonight, the paper had three names on it.

*Reng. Lián. The King.*

He looked at them for a long time.

---

He had learned of the proposal the same evening it was made. The Count’s steward was a Reng man, but the steward’s cousin kept the books for a merchant house that owed Rowan a great deal of money, and money, in the Grand Duke’s experience, was the only thread in the world that could be followed all the way to the end.

*He asked her,* the cousin had whispered, in the anteroom, clutching his hat. *In the east pavilion. The steward heard it all from behind the silk. He asked her to marry him, and she said no, and he said there was always the boy.*

Rowan had given the man a silver coin and sent him away. Then he had come up here, and sat down, and written the three names.

He dipped his pen. Beside *Reng*, he wrote: *The estate. The boy. The Maiden, if she can be broken. The dragon, through the boy, if she cannot.*

He looked at it.

Beside *Lián*, he wrote: *A dead lord. A dead lady. A girl with no friends at court and three months’ leave. A child in a nursery. And a beast at North Ridge that will do anything she asks.*

He looked at that longer.

Beside *The King*, he wrote nothing at all. He only drew a small circle, very carefully, with the tip of his pen, and sat looking at it.

---

He had thought, once, that he understood Aldric Reng.

A clever man. A patient man. A man with nothing — a ruined house, ten kinsmen waiting for the rope — who would therefore be grateful for anything he was given. Rowan had given him a great deal. He had given him the docks, and the crates, and the rehabilitation of his name. He had given him the twenty parts in a hundred of the Lián fortune, and the widow, and the house. And in return he had taken the eighty parts, and the ports, and the ships, and a quiet, useful tool who would always, always remember whose hand had lifted him out of the dust.

That had been the arithmetic.

But the arithmetic had been wrong. Rowan saw that now, sitting in the lamplight with the river sounding beneath his windows. He had thought Aldric wanted wealth. Or safety. Or revenge on the King who had sentenced his kin. Things a man could be given, and bound by the giving.

He had not understood that Aldric wanted the one thing Rowan could not give him. The one thing no one could give. The thing that was only ever *taken*.

*A throne,* the steward’s cousin had whispered. *He said it would be a throne.*

Rowan set down his pen.

---

He thought about the Maiden.

He had met her twice. Once as a girl of fourteen, at the court presentation after the dragon first spoke to her — a thin, pale, silent child in ceremonial silk, with golden eyes that looked at every man in the room as though she were measuring the length of his throat. And once at the victory banquet, three weeks before the garden, across the hall, when she had looked at him over the Crown Prince’s shoulder.

He had kept her at the front for three years because a dragon was the single most useful weapon in the Kingdom, and weapons belonged where the war was. He had blocked her petitions for leave because a general who comes home sees things, and asks questions, and a general whose father had been hanged on your evidence might ask some very inconvenient ones. It had been simple. It had been sensible. Aldric had gone around him — had gotten her home by some back door into the Supreme Commander’s office that Rowan still had not found — and Rowan had been angry. He had told Aldric so, in the pavilion, beneath that ridiculous tree.

*You have your widow and your house. Leave the Maiden where she is useful.*

And Aldric had laughed. And said, over the rim of his teacup:

*Useful to you, Your Grace. I have found a better use.*

Rowan picked up his pen again. Beneath *Reng*, he wrote one more line, very small.

*If he marries her, he holds the dragon. If he holds the dragon, he does not need me.*

He looked at it.

Then, beneath that: *If he does not need me, he will not stop at the Lián estate.*

And beneath that, after a long time: *Nor at mine.*

---

The river sounded beneath the windows. Somewhere in the house a clock chimed the second watch.

There were, Rowan thought, several ways a sensible man might proceed.

He might go to the King. He might tell him what Aldric intended. But the King already knew. Rowan was certain of it. The King had given Aldric the boy, in front of six hundred mourners, with a white flower and a sad face — and the King did nothing without having counted it first. Whatever the King was counting, it was not in Rowan’s favor. The small circle on the paper looked back at him, empty.

He might move against Aldric himself. But Aldric was, just now, the most beloved man in the capital. The grieving widower. The man redeemed by devotion. To touch him now would be to set the whole court against the hand that did it.

Or.

Rowan looked at the second name on the paper. *Lián.*

A girl with no friends at court. Who would not bend. Who had said *no* in a pavilion to a man with the King’s blessing behind him, and walked away.

Who would not, Rowan thought, stay to be married.

He sat very still.

It was quite obvious, once he looked at it. She would run. Of course she would run. She was her father’s daughter, and she had nothing left to lose, and she had fifteen old soldiers who would follow her into the sea. She would take the boy, if she could reach him. And she would run.

And if she ran, she would be a traitor. A deserter. The Crown would hunt her. Aldric would have the house, and the name, and his grief to perform — but he would not have the boy. And he would not have the Maiden. And he would not have the dragon.

And a Reng without a dragon, Rowan thought, was a Reng who still needed friends.

He dipped his pen one last time.

Beneath everything else on the page, he wrote: *A Reng with a dragon is worse than a traitor without one.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he folded the paper, and held it to the lamp, and burned it, and brushed the ash from his desk into his palm and let it fall out of the open window into the dark, toward the river.

---

In the morning, the Grand Duke sent for the captain of the city watch, who was his wife’s nephew and owed him his post.

It was a short interview. They spoke of small things. The price of grain. The state of the roads. The Duke mentioned, in passing, that he had heard complaints about the north gate — the watch there was too large, he understood, too idle, eating their heads off at the Crown’s expense while the war went hungry for men. Twelve soldiers to guard a gate that saw nothing after dark but farm carts. It was wasteful. The Duke did not like waste.

Perhaps, he suggested, on the nights of the new moon, when there was no traffic at all, four would be enough.

The captain of the watch bowed very low, and said that it would be seen to.

When he had gone, Rowan Hale stood at the window of his study for a while, looking out across the city at the eastern quarter — at a long wall by the river, and pale roofs, and the dark green crown of an old tree — with his hands clasped behind his back and his pale eyes perfectly calm.

He did not smile. He was not a man who smiled when he was alone. But something in his face eased, very slightly, the way a man’s face eases when a long column of figures finally, at the bottom, comes out even.
