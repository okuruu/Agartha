# **Chapter 87**

The storm came up out of the west on the evening of the new moon, as though someone had ordered it.

All afternoon the air had been thick and still and yellow, the kind of heat that presses on the back of the neck like a hand. The river lay flat as oil. The leaves of the peach tree hung without moving. In the kitchens the cook complained that her bread would not rise and her milk had turned, and the maids fanned themselves with their aprons and said it would break before nightfall, and it did.

It broke at dusk. First the wind — a long, low, rising moan out of the west that set every shutter in the house banging. Then the dark, rolling up over the city like a lid being closed. Then the rain.

By the time the evening soup went up from the kitchens, it was coming down so hard that the maids carrying the trays along the galleries had to lean into it, and the lanterns hissed and guttered on their hooks, and no one — not the steward, not the Count’s guards at the gate, not the old gatekeeper in his little stone house — thought twice about going to bed early.

---

Esdel did not eat. None of them did.

He sat in his narrow room at the end of the east wing with his pack at his feet and his medicine satchel over his shoulder, and listened to the house.

He had heard it every night for three months. He knew its sounds the way he had known the apothecary’s — which boards creaked, which doors stuck, which shutter on the south gallery had a broken latch and knocked all night in any wind. He listened to the soup go up. He listened to the bowls come down again, empty. He listened to the voices in the servants’ hall, loud with the storm at first, then quieter. Then slower. A laugh that trailed off into a yawn. A chair scraping back. Footsteps on the back stairs, heavy and uneven.

Then nothing. Only the rain, and the wind, and the shutter on the south gallery knocking, and knocking.

He looked at the candle on his table. He had marked it that afternoon with a thumbnail, in six even notches. Two had burned away.

He stood up.

---

Han Ruo was in the corridor, in his armor, with his sword at his hip and his helmet under his arm. Behind him stood the soldiers of House Lián — fourteen of them, for Suyin was already with Changli — in the dark, silent, their armor wrapped in black cloth so it would not ring. Pei Lun at the back, very pale, with a bow in his hands that was too big for him.

No one spoke. Han Ruo only looked at Esdel, and nodded once, and turned.

They went down the gallery in single file.

---

The house was asleep.

It was the strangest thing Esdel had ever seen. Stranger than the Titan, stranger than the dragon, stranger than a caravan disappearing into the sand. They went down the east gallery and across the inner courtyard and along the south gallery toward the nursery, and everywhere they went there were people sleeping. A maid curled on a bench in an alcove with her apron over her face. Two of the Count’s guards at the courtyard door, slumped against the wall, one with his chin on his chest, the other with his head on the first one’s shoulder. The steward, sitting upright in a chair outside the Count’s study with a ledger open on his knees and his mouth open and his spectacles askew, snoring softly.

They stepped around them. No one woke.

He found himself looking at each one as they passed. Watching their chests rise and fall. Counting their breaths without meaning to. *Slow. Even. Deep.* Not too slow. Not too shallow. He could not stop himself. He had measured it four times, and still, with every sleeping face they passed, his heart stopped, and waited, and started again only when he saw the breath come.

*The kitchen girl who brings the bread. She’s twelve.*

He did not see her. He did not know where she slept. He would never know if she had woken in the morning with a headache and no memory of going to bed, or not at all.

He carried that with him down the gallery, and out of the house, and a long way after.

---

The General was waiting at the end of the south gallery, outside the nursery door.

She was in her riding clothes — dark, plain, with a cloak over them — and her sword was at her hip. The Count’s guard lay on the floor at her feet, curled on his side beside his overturned stool, breathing slowly. Changli stood behind her with a bundle in her arms. Not the baby. Clothes, blankets, a small bag. Suyin stood behind Changli, with her bow on her back and a traveling pack over one shoulder.

Xueyao looked at Esdel as he came.

“The wet-nurse?” he said quietly.

“Asleep.”

He knelt by the guard. He put two fingers to the man’s throat, and counted, and felt the pulse slow and steady against his fingertips. He stood up again.

“Go,” he said.

She opened the door.

---

The nursery was small and warm and smelled of milk and linen and the faint sweetness of the orris powder the wet-nurse used. A single night-light burned in a dish on the chest by the window, turned very low. The rain drummed on the roof. The shutter on the gallery outside knocked, and knocked.

In the cot beneath the window, Yueming was asleep.

He was on his back, with his arms flung up over his head the way very small children sleep — as though surrendering, completely, to something much larger than themselves. His cheeks were flushed. His mouth was open. One fist was curled against the pillow, and the small dark fold at the top of his ear, the one his mother had said was his father’s, caught the dim light.

Xueyao stood over the cot for a long moment.

Then she reached down and lifted him out.

She did it better than the first time. Esdel saw that. She had held him every day for three months — beneath the peach tree, and at her mother’s window, and in her own rooms in the long afternoons after the funeral, when she had sat by the orchids with her brother asleep on her chest and not said a word to anyone. Her arms knew him now. She lifted him and turned him and laid his head against her shoulder in one smooth motion, and he stirred, and made a small complaining sound, and then settled against her neck with a long, damp sigh, and slept on.

She stood very still with him.

Behind her, in the doorway, no one moved. Not Changli, not Suyin, not Han Ruo. Pei Lun had turned his face to the wall.

Xueyao bent her head and put her cheek against her brother’s hair. She closed her eyes.

“Yueming,” she said. Barely a sound, beneath the rain. “I’m sorry. I promised I’d keep you safe. This is the only way I know.”

The child slept.

“You won’t remember me,” she said. “That’s good. That’s how it should be. You’ll grow up somewhere small and quiet, with a different name, and you’ll never hear the word *Lián* except in stories. You’ll never hear a dragon in your head. You’ll never stand at an altar and—” She stopped. Her voice did not break. It only stopped, the way a road stops at the edge of a cliff.

“Be kind,” she whispered. “Be stupid, and happy, and kind. Marry someone you love. Grow old. That’s all. That’s everything.”

Then she opened her eyes, and turned, and held him out to Changli.

---

Changli did not take him at once.

She stood in the doorway with the bundle of clothes in her arms, looking at Xueyao, and the baby, and her own empty hands. The dim light of the night-lamp lay across her beautiful face. It was perfectly composed. Only her eyes were not.

“Xueyao,” she said.

“I know.”

“You don’t. You don’t know.” Changli’s voice was very low. “Seventeen years. You were six, and you bit the girl who pushed me into the koi pond, and your father made you apologize, and you did, and then you bit her again.” Her mouth shook. “You were fourteen and the dragon spoke to you, and you didn’t sleep for a week, and I sat up with you every night, and you made me swear never to tell anyone you cried. I never told anyone.” She drew a breath. “I followed you to the front. I would have followed you into the sea.”

“I know,” said Xueyao again. “That’s why I can’t let you.”

Changli looked at her for one moment more.

Then she dropped the bundle of clothes on the floor, and stepped forward, and put her arms around both of them — Xueyao, and the baby between them — and held on. Hard. The way you hold on to something in a flood.

Xueyao did not move. Then, slowly, with her free arm, she held on back.

They stood like that in the dim warm nursery, with the rain on the roof, for a long moment. No one in the doorway said anything. Han Ruo was looking at the ceiling. His eyes were wet.

Then Changli let go, and stepped back, and held out her arms. And Xueyao put her brother into them.

Changli settled him against her shoulder, the way Xueyao had. The baby stirred, complained, sighed, and slept.

“Raise him far from this city,” said Lián Xueyao. Her voice was perfectly steady. It was the voice she used to give orders on the field. “Let him live free of our name.”

Changli looked at her over the child’s head.

“I will,” she said. “I promise.”

Then she bent, and picked up the bundle of clothes from the floor, and turned, and walked out of the nursery with the last heir of the House of Lián asleep on her shoulder. Suyin went after her, silent, with one hand on her bow.

They went down the south gallery toward the river gate. Esdel watched them go — two women and a child, in the dark, in the rain — until the corner of the gallery took them, and there was nothing left but the shutter knocking in the wind.

Beside him, the General stood in the doorway of the empty nursery and did not move.

He looked at the candle stub in his hand. He had lit it from the night-lamp. The fourth notch was burning.

“General,” he said quietly.

She turned her head.

For a moment, in the dim light, he saw her face — before she put it away. It was the face of a girl of fourteen who had not slept for a week, and made her friend swear never to tell.

Then it was gone. And the Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon looked at him with cool golden eyes, and said, “Yes. Let’s go.”
