# **Chapter 31**

Lián Xueyao sat beneath the dim canvas of her tent, in a structure meant for temporary deployment that had felt like her cage for years. The oil lamp on the campaign chest flickered against the draft that crept through the seams.

Three years. Three years since she had seen the corridors of the Lián estate, the peach tree outside her mother’s chamber that bloomed violently pink every spring, the deep calm of her father’s voice in the courtyard at evening lessons. Her father had died a traitor to the crown he had spent his life defending, and she had learned of it from a rumor.

Then, a message. It had been carried by one of Lord Hengzhi’s last loyal vassals, who did not live to be thanked for it. The charge of rebellion was a fraud. Her mother had married the man who built it. And the draught Aldric brought her every week was not medicine. It was a chain: a slow poison meant not to kill but to soften her mother’s sharp mind and bind her heart to whoever sat beside her. “She won’t listen to reason now,” the vassal wrote. “The bonds are too deep.”

Xueyao’s hands trembled, not with weakness. She had begged her commander for leave once, twice, a third time. Each time the answer was the same, civil and cold. *The war needs you.* She did not know whose voice was behind it.

She did not cry. She had long since learned how soldiers weep, and it was without sound, and with her face turned toward the canvas wall.

“Is there anyone we can trust to go in my place?” she asked Changli, who had followed her since they were six, even into the mud.

“There’s one. Esdel. The apothecary who transferred to our post seven months ago.” Changli’s tone held a weary amusement. “He cracks jokes when everyone else is grim, and keeps to himself when the company is drinking. He’s clever, my Lady. But he has no past, no family, no roots. If things go wrong, we can claim no knowledge of him.”

Xueyao remembered him: a pair of tired brown eyes by the roadside the night the Titan fell, and a flicker of red. Seven months, and she had never had her answer.

“Then he’ll go,” she said.

---

Esdel did not know any of this. He was at the back of the fever tent, with a boy of seventeen asleep at last under his hands, and the lamp burning down.

He had counted the nights without meaning to. It was spring again, and he had lost the seasons in between, in mud and snow and the work. The Ninth Division did not ask where he had come from. He did not tell them.

Under his shirt the silver flower had gone warm against his skin, the way it did when he was still. He closed his hand over it. In his mind there was a lamp on the second step, turned low, and a woman putting it there, and he knew exactly how the light would lie across the worn wood, though he could not see it, and would not.

*Is it lit tonight?*

He didn’t know. That was the worst of it. He knew only that he hoped, and that the hope was a selfish thing, because to hope it was lit was to hope she was still waiting, and she should not wait. He took his hand away from his chest.

The boy stirred. Esdel pulled the blanket up, and the orderly came to tell him the General wanted him.

---

In her hand, the General held a grant of leave signed by the Supreme Commander. Three months. After three refusals, her fourth petition had been granted without a word of explanation, and that unsettled her more than any refusal had.

“I meant to send you ahead alone,” she told Changli, when they were alone. “Now that I am going myself, why a field apothecary? I could summon a physician from the royal academy.”

“Precisely, my Lady. In the capital we may need a medical mind who is not already loyal to the Kingdom’s politics or the Estate’s. He is due for discharge in three months, about when our leave ends. He has no ties. Should the journey prove hostile, he is expendable without repercussion to the House.”

*Expendable.* The word tasted like gunpowder. Xueyao stared at the map, at the long road to the capital, and she did not like how easily she let it stand.

“Very well. I have secured fifteen soldiers of House Lián from the veteran ranks as our escort. Summon him.”

Esdel came with tincture stains on his sleeves and bowed. “You are to accompany me to the capital for my period of sanctioned leave,” she said, “as medical auxiliary to my private escort.” She held out the letter, stamped with her seal. It would let him leave the front without being marked a deserter.

He looked at the letter. Then at her.

“I understand, General,” he said. “I’ll gather the supplies.”

He did not ask why. She noticed that, and did not know what to make of it.

---

Deep in that night, Changli found him by his tent, weighing the medicine satchels in his hands.

She stood close enough that he could smell her perfume, so out of place among the iron and smoke. She said something about comfort on the long road, and warm beds in the capital, and laid her hand on his sleeve.

Esdel did not move toward it, or away. He only looked at her as one looks at someone very cold.

“Attendant Changli,” he said gently. “I’m grateful. But I can’t. There’s someone I made a promise to, and I’d like to keep the one I have left.”

It was the whole truth, and he said it softly, so there was no insult in it. He moved her hand from his sleeve by adjusting the strap, and bowed, and went to finish his inventory.

Changli watched him go. The practiced smile slid slowly from her face.

“Not a machine,” she told the dark. “A man with a locked door.”

She reported it to Xueyao as it was: he had refused charm, vanity, and temptation, and given a reason that was not duty at all. “He did not even look as if he were refusing, my Lady. He looked as if it hurt.”

Xueyao was silent a moment. Whatever he carried, it was his own, and he guarded it as she guarded hers.

“Then I trust him,” she said softly. “More than I would a man who could be bought.”

---

Esdel did not sleep. He sat on his cot with the satchels packed and a scrap of paper on his knee, and a stub of pencil.

*Lyora,*

That was all he had. The rest would not come. Five days to the capital and who knew how far to Edenridge, with no courier he could trust and a name he could not afford to put on a letter. Everything he could say would either be a lie, which he would not write, or true, which she should not have to read.

*I am well. I think of the lamp.*

He crossed that out. It was too much like asking.

He sat a long time with the pencil hovering over the paper, and the necklace lay warm in his fist. At last he folded the page once, and again, small enough to burn, and held it to the wick of the little stove until the name *Lyora* curled and went black.

He watched it all the way down. Then he put out his hands to the warmth of the ashes, as if that could be the same as being warm.

At the change of the guard, a pale line of grey rose at the edge of the east, and fifteen soldiers in dark cloth mounted in the cold. The General rode at the head, with her attendant at her side. The apothecary took his place at the back with the pack mule and the medicine chests, where the dust would be thickest.

No one spoke to him. They left at dawn, toward the walls of the Kingdom, and a house with a peach tree, and a mother who did not know her daughter was coming.
