# **Chapter 31**

Night had settled gently over Edenridge, a hush wrapping the village like a soft quilt, broken only by the rhythmic chirping of crickets and the sigh of the cool breeze that slipped through the wooden shutters of the apothecary’s home. 

From the second floor, Esdel could smell faint traces of herbs drying, the familiar scent of mint and sage mingling with the comforting aroma of tea drifting up from below. 

The house creaked softly, the way old wood does when it remembers too much, and he found himself restless — not from unease, but from that quiet ache that sometimes followed laughter-filled days. 

His feet carried him down the narrow stairs, each step careful, as if unwilling to disturb the peace that had settled like dew. Downstairs, in the dim glow of the small oil lamp, he saw her — Lyora, sitting by the kitchen table, sleeves rolled up, a few loose strands of her hair escaping the tie at her nape, haloed by amber light. 

Her face was calm, eyes lowered toward the cup of tea she was cooling with slow, steady breaths, the soft curve of her lips moving with the quiet rhythm of thought. 

“Couldn’t sleep?” she asked, not looking up, but he could hear the faint smile in her tone.

“Not really,” Esdel replied, his voice low, roughened slightly by the quiet. 

“Guess I got used to the sound of the market still ringing in my head.” She chuckled softly, the sound light as ripples over water. 

“Same here. It feels strange when everything goes quiet again after such a lively day.” 

The silence that followed wasn’t empty — it hummed with something fragile, something they both felt but couldn’t name. 

Then, almost shyly, she asked, “Do you… like it here? Living in this village, I mean?” 

Esdel took a slow sip of tea, the warmth spreading through his chest before he answered. 

“Yeah,” he said at last. 

“It’s peaceful. People are kind. It’s the first time I’ve felt… that I actually belong somewhere. Even if I don’t remember anything, being here feels right.” 

Her gaze softened, the lamplight catching in her green eyes like small reflections of spring. 

“I’m glad,” she said simply, and he tilted his head, studying her in that quiet way of his. 

“Why are you and your grandmother so kind to me, anyway?” he asked, his voice carrying more sincerity than teasing. 

“You didn’t even know who I was when I came here.” 

She smiled — the kind of smile that seemed to reach back through years, gentle and wise.

“Grandma always said people who’ve lost their way just need someone to remind them what warmth feels like,” she murmured. “I guess we just followed her words.” 

He lowered his gaze, his fingers tracing the rim of the cup. 

“Then I’m lucky you did.” 

For a moment, neither spoke. The fire in the lamp flickered, its glow bending the shadows between them. Then Lyora, perhaps to lighten the heaviness that had begun to linger, grinned suddenly. 

“By the way, I heard you like cats.” 

Esdel’s head lifted, laughter glinting in his eyes. 

“Who doesn’t? They’re cute. Independent. Chaotic, but elegant.” 

Lyora nodded gravely. 

“I love cats too. But don’t tell Grandma — she still hasn’t forgotten the stories about me gluing that poor cat’s fur years ago.”

Esdel laughed so hard he nearly spilled his tea. 

“Oh no, that story again!” She groaned, burying her face in her hands. 

“I can’t believe she told you that! I was five!” 

He leaned forward, teasing. “You glued the fur together, Lyora. That cat must’ve looked like a plush toy!” 

“Stop it!” she cried, laughter escaping between her words. 

The two of them laughed until the air itself seemed to join in, the warmth in their laughter filling every corner of the little kitchen. When the sound finally faded, it left behind a soft stillness that felt different — gentler, sweeter somehow. 

They drifted easily into other talk — food, childhood memories, the ordinary pieces of life that somehow felt extraordinary when shared between them. 

“So,” she asked, twirling her cup slightly, 

“What do you think of tomato soup?”

“It’s great,” he said without hesitation. 

“Especially when it’s warm and slightly sour. Comfort food.” 

She froze, then stared at him as if he’d said something unthinkable. 

“You like tomato soup?” 

“Yeah. Why?” 

“I can’t stand it,” she said with exaggerated disgust. 

“The texture is… slimy. It feels like it’s judging me.” 

He blinked, then laughed in disbelief. 

“Tomato soup is judging you?”

“Yes!” she insisted, pointing dramatically at her cup as though it were the soup in question. 

“It stares into your soul. It’s the kind of food that knows your secrets.” 

Esdel shook his head, smiling.

“That’s the strangest food philosophy I’ve ever heard.” 

“Well, it’s true,” she said, crossing her arms. 

“Then I guess I’ll have to eat your share from now on.” 

“Fine by me,” she said proudly. 

“You and your judgmental soup can live happily ever after.” 

Their laughter rose again, easy and bright, and when it faded this time, it left something softer behind — like the hush that follows music, lingering in the chest. Lyora’s gaze lowered, her fingers curling loosely around her cup.

“Have you ever thought about the future?” she asked quietly. 

He tilted his head. “Future?” 

She nodded, a faint, wistful smile touching her lips. 

“I mean… what you’d want someday. For me, I think… I’d like a simple wedding. Not too big, not too fancy. Just nice enough. Surrounded by the people I care about. Grandma, my friends… people like you.” 

The words hung between them, gentle but heavy with something unspoken. He blinked, caught off guard, and then smiled, small but sincere. 

“That sounds perfect for you.” 

“What about you?” she asked, watching him closely. 

“What’s your dream wedding like?” 

He leaned back, pretending to think, though his heart had already begun its quiet, steady ache. 

“I don’t know,” he said finally, shrugging with a faint grin.

“Maybe I’ll just follow whatever my future wife wants. Seems easier that way.”

She pouted. “You’re no fun.” 

Esdel chuckled.

“Maybe not. But at least I’m honest.” 

She laughed again, shaking her head. 

“You really are impossible sometimes.” 

“Guess so.” 

The conversation ebbed into silence then, but it wasn’t awkward. It was warm, like the glow of embers that refused to die out. The quiet stretched comfortably between them, filled with the soft creak of wood, the distant hum of the night beyond the walls, and the faint sound of their breathing — steady, almost in sync. 

Their eyes met across the table, just above the rising curls of steam. Her expression was unreadable, a quiet mixture of tenderness and restraint, and he found himself unable to look away. 

The lamplight turned her hair into dark silk, painted her skin with gold. She smiled — small, shy, genuine — and he returned it without thought. In that fragile stillness, something invisible and vast stirred — not spoken, not even fully understood, but undeniably there. 

Outside, the world was swallowed by darkness; inside, the oil lamp flickered softly, painting them both in the kind of warmth that seemed too delicate to last. And though neither said a word, both could feel it — something growing quietly between them, deep and gentle, like the first bloom of spring daring to open in the heart of winter.
