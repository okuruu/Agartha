# **Chapter 93**

He woke in a hut.

It was very small and very dark, and it smelled of smoke and pine and old animals. There was a fire somewhere near his feet — he could feel the warmth of it, and hear it, a soft crackling — and above him a low roof of branches and bark, with a hole in the middle where the smoke went out and a little grey daylight came in.

He lay still and looked at the hole for a long time.

He did not know where he was. For a moment — a long, strange, drifting moment — he did not know *who* he was. He lay on his back in the dark with the fire at his feet and his mind as blank and clean as the desert had been, a year ago, when he first opened his eyes beneath that merciless sun.

Then it came back. All of it. The ford. The spear. The blue vial. The red world. The Count’s face, and the gold thread in his heart, and the grey horse going over. The old soldier on the causeway, raising his sword.

He shut his eyes again.

---

“Don’t you dare,” said a voice.

He opened them.

The General was sitting beside him. On the dirt floor of the hut, with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, the way she had sat outside her mother’s door. She looked terrible. There were dark circles under her eyes, and dried blood on her sleeves, and her black hair was loose and matted. She looked as though she had not slept for a very long time.

“Don’t you dare go back to sleep,” she said. “You’ve been asleep for three days.”

He tried to speak. His throat was dry as sand. She reached for a cup beside her — a rough wooden thing — and held it to his lips, and he drank. It was water, cold, tasting of stone.

“Three days,” he said. His voice came out a croak.

“Three days.” She set the cup down. “Pei Lun and I sat with you the whole time. Gao helped. You had a fever like a forge. You talked.” She looked at him. “A great deal.”

He did not ask what he had said. He was not sure he wanted to know.

He put his hand, slowly, to his left side. Beneath his shirt, beneath a thick pad of linen that someone had bound around his ribs, there was — he pressed — nothing. No wound. Only smooth skin, and a faint deep ache, like a bruise a week old.

“It closed on the second night,” said Xueyao. “I watched it. I was changing the dressing and I watched it close. Like—” She stopped. “Like a mouth.”

He did not say anything.

“Your eyes bled for a day,” she said. “Then they stopped. The fever broke this morning.” She was quiet a moment. “Gao says you took the Count’s hand. And put him in the river. And his men went in after him, all of them, and forgot us, and that’s how we reached the trees.”

“Is he dead?”

“No.” She looked at the fire. “A shepherd came through yesterday. He’d been down to the valley. He says they pulled the Count out half-drowned and carried him back to the capital on a litter. He lives.” A pause. “You could have killed him. Gao says so. He says you were standing right in front of him, with your sword, and there wasn’t anyone in the world who could have stopped you.”

The fire crackled.

“Why didn’t you?” said Lián Xueyao.

---

He thought about it for a long time.

He thought about the red world, and the frozen river, and the thirty small lights. About the Count’s heart, beating strong and long and gold-threaded, right there in front of him. About how easy it would have been.

“I don’t know,” he said at last. And then, because she deserved more than that: “I saw his heart. With the eyes. I could see — how long it had. It was long. A great many years.” He looked at the smoke-hole in the roof. “And there was something in it. Something that wasn’t his. Gold. Moving. Like the thing in the tonic that I couldn’t name.”

She was very still.

“I don’t know what it means,” he said. “I don’t know if it means anything. But I looked at it, and I thought—” He stopped. “I thought, if I kill him, I’ll never know. And whatever that is — whatever’s in him — it won’t die with him. It’ll just go somewhere else.” He was quiet a moment. “And I thought of your mother. In the garden. She didn’t ask you for his death. She asked you for your brother.”

Xueyao said nothing for a long time.

“That’s not the whole reason,” she said finally.

“No.”

“What’s the rest?”

He looked at his own hands, lying on the blanket. Scarred across the knuckles. Steady. The hands that had held the dying, and known, before the pulse failed.

“I didn’t want to,” he said. “That’s all. I didn’t want to be the kind of man who would.”

The General looked at him. And then — very slowly, as though it cost her something — she nodded.

“The eye of mercy,” she said softly.

“Is that what it is?”

“I don’t know.” She looked back at the fire. “I think it might be the only thing it’s ever been.”

---

The others came in, one by one, as the day went on. Gao, with a rag wrapped round his head and a lean grey scowl, who looked at Esdel lying awake on the blanket and said, “Idiot,” with great feeling, and then sat down beside him and did not leave for an hour. The four old soldiers, awkward, bringing rabbit stew in a dented pot. And Pei Lun, last of all, in the evening, with his split lip healing and his bruises gone yellow — who stood in the low doorway for a long moment, looking at Esdel, and then came and knelt beside the blanket and put his face in his hands and wept like a child.

Esdel put his hand on the boy’s shoulder and left it there.

Seven of them. That was what was left. The General, the apothecary, Gao, four old soldiers, and a boy. Seven, out of seventeen who had ridden out of North Ridge in the spring.

---

The trader came on the fifth day.

He was a thin, cheerful, bow-legged man with a string of mules loaded with salt and needles and cheap tin pots, working his way up through the hill country toward the northern passes the way he did every summer. He came up the goat track in the late afternoon and found them there — seven ragged people and a handful of worn-out horses in a shepherd’s hut at the edge of a pine wood — and did not seem surprised at all. He had seen a great many strange things in the hills.

He shared their fire. He shared his salt. He told them the news, the way traders do, in exchange for a bowl of stew — the price of grain in the lowlands, a flood in the river towns, a fire in a monastery.

And the decree.

He had heard it read in a market town two days south of there. It had been nailed up on the door of the magistrate’s house, and a crier had read it out in the square, and everyone had come to listen, because it was not every day that a Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon was declared a traitor to the Crown.

“Five thousand gold crowns,” said the trader, wiping his bowl with a heel of bread. “For her. Living. Can you imagine? Five thousand. I’d be set for life.” He chuckled. “And a thousand each for the ones who went with her. Living or dead.” He shook his head. “They read all the names out. Took an age. Soldiers, mostly. And some apothecary. Nobody knew who he was. Some village near the desert, they said. What was it—”

He snapped his fingers, trying to remember. The fire crackled. No one in the hut moved.

“*Edenridge,*” said the trader. “That was it. Pretty name. Never heard of it.”

---

Esdel went out.

He did not remember getting up. He did not remember the door. He found himself standing at the edge of the pine wood, in the long gold evening light, with his hand pressed against the rough bark of a tree, looking south.

He could not see anything. Only hills — green and grey and gold, rolling away and away, one behind another, until they faded into the haze at the edge of the world. Somewhere beyond them was the capital. And beyond that, the plains. And beyond that, a long way beyond, the forest, and the low hills, and the river running between its willows. And an old house with ivy on the walls.

*Late of the village of Edenridge.*

His name. On a decree. Nailed to the door of every magistrate’s house in the Kingdom. Read out by criers in every market square. With the name of the village beside it, where anyone could hear.

He understood it all at once, the way he understood the slope of a field. Standing at the edge of the pine wood with his hand on the bark, he understood it completely.

The Count had not needed to send a rider on the south road. He had not needed a letter, or a spy, or a map. He had only needed to put one word into a decree. And now every soldier and every bounty-hunter and every magistrate’s man in the Kingdom knew that the traitor’s apothecary, worth a thousand gold crowns, living or dead, had come from a village called Edenridge.

And if the traitor’s apothecary were ever to go home—

They would be waiting. They would be waiting forever. Behind every tree on the river road, in every room of the inn. Watching an old house with ivy on its walls. Watching a lamp on a stair.

He could never go back.

Not in a year. Not in ten. Not while the decree stood, and the Count lived, and there was a single man in the Kingdom who wanted a thousand gold crowns. If he went back, he would lead them to her door. He would bring the hunt to the river, and the willows, and the garden, and the girl who had said *I’ll be waiting*, and they would take her. To make him come. Or to make him watch.

He had promised to come back.

He stood at the edge of the pine wood with his hand against the tree and his other hand pressed flat against his chest, over the silver flower, and looked south at the hills fading into the gold haze, and did not move.

He did not weep. He found he could not. It was too large for that. It was like standing at the edge of the desert again, a year ago, beneath the white sun — looking at the sand stretching away in every direction, and understanding that there was no way back to wherever he had come from. That there had never been.

Only this time he knew exactly what he had lost.

---

She came out after a while. He heard her step on the pine needles behind him, and stop.

She did not come closer. She stood a few paces off, the way he had learned to stand, and waited.

“You heard,” he said. It was not a question.

“Yes.”

“He did it on purpose. Putting the name in.”

“Yes.” A pause. “I think he did.”

The sun was going down behind the western hills. The long gold light lay across the valley below them like honey.

“Go,” said Lián Xueyao.

He turned his head.

She was standing very straight, in her filthy riding clothes with the dried blood on the sleeves, with the sunset on her face. Her golden eyes were steady.

“I release you,” she said. “From my service. From your levy. From whatever you think you owe me. You don’t owe me anything.” Her voice did not waver. “There’s a price on your head and it’s because of me. Because you came into my mother’s house, and did what I asked, and stayed when I told you to go. So go. Now. Tonight. Find a road that doesn’t lead anywhere near that village, and follow it until you’re somewhere so far away no one’s ever heard the name Lián, or Reng, or any of it. Change your name. Grow a beard. Be someone else.” A breath. “You’ve done it once before. You can do it again.”

He looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked south again. At the hills.

“If I go,” he said slowly, “and they catch me — alone, somewhere, some day — they’ll want to know where I came from. They’ll already know. It’s on the decree.” He paused. “And they’ll go there. To make sure. To see who else knew me. Who else might know where the traitor went.”

Xueyao was silent.

“But if I stay with you,” said Esdel, “they won’t look for me alone. They’ll look for *you*. The Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon, worth five thousand crowns. Every man who wants that gold will be looking north, and east, and into the borderlands, wherever you go.” He turned his head and met her eyes. “Not south. Not toward the river. Not toward her.”

The sun touched the edge of the hills.

“You said it yourself,” he said quietly. “Under the peach tree. If I’m with you, he won’t be looking south. He’ll be looking for us.”

“I said that before the decree.”

“It’s truer now.”

She looked at him. He watched her understand — slowly, the way cold water rises — what he was choosing. Not her. Not her cause, or her vengeance, or her war. Only the one thing he could still do for someone he would never see again: be somewhere else. Be the thing the hunters chased. Draw them away from the river and the willows and the lamp on the stair, for as long as he lived, by the simple act of never going home.

It was the only way he had left to keep his promise.

Xueyao did not say anything. She did not thank him. She only stood there in the last of the light, looking at him, with her face very still.

Then she came and stood beside him at the edge of the pine wood, not touching, the width of a person between them, and looked south with him at the hills until the sun was gone.
