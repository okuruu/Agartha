# **Chapter 64**

The first time he came, it was with an air of gentleness that seemed almost misplaced among the heavy, quiet corridors of mourning. It was not the boisterous, self-important kindness of the well-meaning, but a meticulous, almost surgical politeness that cut through the silence.

Lady Wei Anlan was still shrouded in white, the coarse fabric of her mourning robes feeling perpetually stiff against her skin, her sleeves untouched by any color, any pigment of life, since the execution that had broken the world. 

The Lián manor, once a center of attention, had collapsed inward, growing soundless and hollow—like breath eternally withheld by the very stone and timber of the walls. The servants, their faces gaunt with shared despair, moved like ghosts, speaking only in soft, barely audible whispers that died instantly in the high ceilings. 

In every room, the incense burned low, day after day, sticks of rich, dark sandalwood consumed to the quick, and still the heavy, pervasive scent could not drive away the phantom memory of blood and dust that clung to the air.

Aldric Reng stepped into that suffocating silence as if it were a room built solely for him, an environment that welcomed and required his specific, controlled presence.

He brought with him not the usual vulgar display of food or silk, but an **intricately carved ornate box** of lacquered rosewood. The wood glowed a deep, oily crimson, polished so finely that it would later mirror the grief-stricken face of the young maid who carried it to her chamber. Inside, nestled securely in dark, midnight-blue velvet, were two small, perfectly smooth vials of amber liquid. The glass was heavy and cool to the touch, and the contents, thick and slow-moving, caught the dwindling afternoon light, giving off a warm, misleading shimmer.

He said his physician had prepared a specific brew for her shattered nerves, formulated from the rare, calming essence of mountain lotus and the grounding stability of amber root. “A mere palliative, Lady Wei,” he stated, his voice so measured it sounded less like human speech and more like the calm, steady tick of a distant clock. 

He called it a kindness, and insisted it was nothing more than a token of sympathy from himself.

Lady Wei, exhausted not just by the weight of her grief but by the endless, meaningless cycle of formalized condolence visits, accepted it without the slightest flicker of suspicion. Her mind was too fogged, her spirit too crushed to question the sincerity of a man who had stood on the opposite side of her husband’s trial. She simply needed respite from the agonizing clarity of her pain.

Each week, with the consistency of a season turning, he came again. Always at dusk, when the shadows were long and the Estate's isolation was deepest, and always with something new. The first week, it was a second set of vials; the next, a tincture for the searing headaches that plagued her nights; later, a salve for the sleeplessness that kept her pacing until dawn. His manner never changed: a soft-spoken inquiry about her physical health, a courteous bow, and a promise of improved rest. 

He never stayed long—perhaps fifteen minutes at most—but it was always long enough for the sound of his low, careful voice to fill the space and linger long after he’d gone, an aftertaste of quiet warmth in a house of cold ashes.

That voice—measured, low, and careful—began, slowly, to fill the vast, echoing spaces left empty by grief. The court had stripped her of connection, but Aldric Reng offered a fragile, structured bridge back to the world.

The tonics worked with unsettling efficiency. They soothed the violent, involuntary trembling in her hands. They slowed the frantic, looping accusations in her thoughts, turning the sharp edges of memory dull. Above all, they softened her nights. The hours she once spent staring at the ceiling, remembering the memories of her husband, were now hours of oblivion. 

In time, Lady Wei Anlan began to look forward to the familiar, respectful knock against the door, less for the medicine itself and more for the momentary halt to her solitude. The faint, refined clink of the thick glass vials became a strange comfort, a predictable, reliable rhythm in the crushing stillness of her life.

He listened when others merely offered platitudes. He would sit, head slightly inclined, allowing her silence to breathe. And when she did speak, he remembered.

Slowly, imperceptibly, the harsh, paralyzing bitterness she had kept fiercely buried beneath her mourning began to fade—not into true peace, but into a chemically induced **forgetfulness**. 

The pain did not leave, but it became muffled, distant, like a storm seen from behind thick leaded glass.

At first, she resisted the gossip that reached her ears. Whispers, thin and sharp as needles, began to leak from the servant quarters and travel through the visiting family members.

They said Aldric Reng had too much polish and too much charm for genuine sincerity; that he cultivated sympathy and connections like coin; that his swift rise in the court was due less to merit and more to an uncanny ability to identify and exploit vulnerabilities. *“He smells opportunity, not sorrow,”* one distant cousin muttered to a maid.

But Lady Wei Anlan saw none of that calculation. To her, surrounded by fear and condemnation, he was simply, quietly kind—a man who spoke to her without the suffocating weight of pity or the coldness of judgment. Crucially, he never mentioned her husband’s name unless she, in a moment of fragile strength, did so first.

And when he did speak of Lián Hengzhi, it was with the tenderness of one who truly understood loss, a respectful acknowledgment of the man's dignity, a silent sorrow that seemed to transcend the court's judgment.

Still, the whispers grew more insistent, turning from quiet speculation into open disapproval. He lingered too long in her desolate gardens, studying the dead winter orchid with an unnerving, proprietary gaze. His carriage stayed too late at the gates, often until the moon was high and the last of the watchmen had passed. 

The maids exchanged knowing, frightened looks behind their hands, worrying about the scandal that would attach itself to their lady’s already compromised name.

But Lady Wei, experiencing the first genuine human connection in months, defended him with a quiet, fierce conviction that surprised even herself.

“He has done nothing unseemly,” she said once to her head steward, her tone sharp enough to not merely scatter the murmurs but to crush them into dust. “He treats my grief as grief, not as gossip meant for public consumption.”

When she relayed this defense to Aldric, he only bowed, accepting her loyalty as his due. But the **faintest, most fleeting curve of satisfaction** passed through his eyes—a subtle tightening at the corner of his mouth so momentary that any observer, especially Lady Wei, already primed to see the best in him, could easily mistake it for a painful ripple of shared sorrow.

Weeks blurred into months.

The polished rosewood box was now permanent on her bedside table. The amber vials multiplied, their contents slightly altered each time. He explained the shifts to her with medical precision: adjustment was necessary; medicine must evolve with the body’s changing requirements, after all.

And perhaps, in a dreadful way, it did. She felt calmer, certainly. Lighter. Her deep, soul-crushing grief no longer weighed like a monolithic stone chained to her spirit. When she dreamt now, the dreams were no longer vivid, brutal recollections of her husband’s death, but hazy, peaceful voids interspersed with the sensation of a warm, resonant voice speaking softly through the endless night. 

Sometimes, in the half-light of waking, she thought she could hear Aldric Reng's measured tone echoing in the deep shadows of her sleep. The tonics had not cured her grief; they had simply **replaced** the man she mourned with the man who delivered her solace.

This dangerous dependence grew as naturally and silently as breathing. It was less a conscious affection and more a primal, physical requirement.

When he missed a scheduled visit, the subsequent days felt cruelly long, the air in the manor sharper, thinner. The tremors in her hands returned with a sudden vengeance, and a cold anxiety coiled in her stomach. She would find herself waiting by the window overlooking the drive, a posture she despised, waiting with an almost desperate intensity for the familiar, steady rhythm of hooves and the crunch of carriage wheels on the gravel path.

When he finally returned, often with a subtle, apologetic smile that seemed to understand her distress entirely, she found herself responding in kind: a genuine, unbidden smile of relief and welcome, given without calculation or realization.

Then came the day that fundamentally altered the ritual. He arrived at dusk, as always, but his hands were empty of the rosewood box and the soothing glass. Instead, he presented a perfectly balanced sheaf of **pale camellias**, their petals smooth as porcelain, their scent faint and cleanly floral, devoid of the heavy perfume of roses or lilies.

He told her they symbolized admiration and quiet longevity, a wish for her recovery. But as their fingers brushed when she accepted them, both knew better. The exchange of the flowers—an unnecessary beauty—marked the transition from physician to suitor, from sympathy to aspiration.

And when he looked at her—not as a patient to be managed, but as something new, something *desired*—she found herself completely and devastatingly unable to look away. The medicated fog that had suppressed her pain also suppressed her moral compass and her loyalty to the past.

What began as a deep, profound gratitude for his kindness blurred irrevocably into affection.

Affection, encouraged by the manufactured ease of her mind, morphed into a burning, consuming longing.

And longing, once articulated in the quiet privacy of her sitting room, became, to her altered perception, **love**.

In the months that followed, she defended him from every whisper, every outright warning that managed to slip past her doors. When the old, established families of the Capital muttered that Aldric’s rise was too swift, his acquisition of favor too conveniently paved, she dismissed them all with a look of cool disdain.

“They never knew him as I do,” she would say, her voice soft but absolute, almost pleading for others to understand the truth she believed she possessed. “He is not like them. He possesses a heart that understands sorrow.”

So when he proposed—softly, with perfect timing, on a rain-soaked evening when the manor felt cold and damp and his presence was the only source of heat—his strong, steady hand placed surely over her trembling one, she did not hesitate. The idea of returning to the terrifying isolation of her grief without him was unthinkable.

The entire kingdom gasped when the news spread, sending shockwaves through the elite circles: Lady Wei Anlan, the devastated widow of the late, disgraced traitor Lián Hengzhi, had married Aldric Reng—the very man who had once stood as a crucial, damning witness to her husband’s downfall and execution. It was too fast, too bold, too convenient. The violation of protocol was staggering.

Yet, in the quiet, inner halls of the Lián estate, where the heavy scent of sandalwood incense had once burned for mourning, a new sound began to echo: the light, silvery sound of Anlan’s laughter.

To the world, it was crafted into the perfect, timeless story of redemption: the compassionate Count who healed the shattered heart of a traitor’s innocent widow; the noble man reborn through the purifying fire of forbidden love.

But in truth, it was a tale of something colder, sharper, and far more transactional.

For the marriage gave Aldric Reng everything he had desired with surgical precision: the last remaining Lián financial holdings, which were secretly tied to Anlan’s dowry; the immediate social legitimacy of being connected to an ancient, noble house; and, most importantly, a perfect, unassailable disguise.

No one in the court would dare question the loyalty, the ethics, or the motivations of a man who had successfully navigated the difficult path of winning forgiveness from the victim’s own wife. He was now protected by the emotional complexity of his own actions.

No one looked beneath the tenderness of his touch to see the calculated precision of his long-term plan.

And Lady Wei Anlan, once sharp, fiercely intelligent, and unyielding in her defense of her husband, smiled beautifully beside him—her voice calm, her movements graceful, her lovely eyes still touched by the shimmering, distant remnants of some invisible, profound sleep. The memories she cherished were not organic; they were artifacts.

She would never know, or perhaps never be able to care, how much of her affection, her gratitude, and her ultimate love had been painstakingly brewed, drop by controlled drop, in the vials of amber liquid he once brought to her door. Her devotion was his final, most enduring poison.
