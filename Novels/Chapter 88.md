# **Chapter 88**

They went down to the river gate first. The General insisted.

“I will see them through,” she said, when Han Ruo tried to argue — the plan had been for the main company to go straight to the stables, and saddle, and be through the great gate before the fifth watch. “It will take a quarter of an hour. I will see them through the gate, Captain, and then we will ride.”

Han Ruo looked at her face and did not argue again.

So the soldiers of House Lián went to the stables with Pei Lun, and the General went down through the dark garden toward the river, and Esdel went with her, because she did not tell him not to.

---

The garden was a black roaring sea. The rain came down in sheets, so hard it bounced back up off the paving stones in a fine white mist, and the wind tore through the peach tree and set its branches thrashing, so that the small hard green fruit came down around them like hail. The pond had overflowed its stone rim. They waded through the edge of it, ankle-deep, cold, and Esdel felt something soft and dead under his boot and did not look down.

The river gate was a small iron door in the garden wall, below the east pavilion, where Lord Hengzhi had once kept a boat for fishing. There was no boat now. There was only the door, and beyond it a narrow stone stair going down to a narrow stone landing, and beyond that the river — running high and brown and fast with the storm, slapping against the stones, loud even over the rain.

Changli and Suyin were waiting on the landing.

There was a boat after all. Not Lord Hengzhi’s. A low, broad, ugly thing, a farmer’s river-barge, with a patched canvas awning at the stern and a lantern swinging from a pole. A man sat at the steering oar, hunched in an oilskin, his face invisible. Han Ruo had found him, three days ago, in a tavern by the docks — a man who carried turnips downriver to the southern markets and asked no questions, for a price. Esdel did not know the price. He did not want to.

Changli was already under the awning, with the baby in her arms, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain. Suyin stood on the landing with the rope in her hand.

Xueyao went down the stair. Esdel stayed at the top, by the iron door, in the shelter of the wall.

He could not hear what they said. The rain was too loud, the river too loud. He saw only the lantern light swinging across them — the General on the landing, Changli under the awning with the child. He saw Xueyao lean in under the canvas. He saw her put her hand, once, on her brother’s head, where it lay wrapped in oilcloth against Changli’s breast.

He saw Changli say something. He saw Xueyao shake her head.

Then Xueyao stepped back, and nodded to Suyin, and Suyin stepped down into the barge and threw off the rope.

The man at the steering oar leaned his weight on it. The barge swung out from the landing, slowly, heavily, into the current — and the river took it.

---

It went fast. That was the thing Esdel had not expected. He had thought there would be time. That the barge would drift away slowly, the lantern growing smaller and smaller, so that they could stand there and watch it for a long while, the way people watch a ship go out of a harbor.

It was not like that. The river was high and angry with the storm, and it took the barge the way a hand takes a leaf, and whirled it out into the dark, and in the space of three breaths the lantern was already small — a single yellow point swinging on the black water, going away.

Xueyao stood on the landing and watched it.

She did not move. She did not call out. She stood with the rain streaming down her face and her hair plastered to her head and her cloak hanging heavy and black from her shoulders, and watched the small yellow light go down the river.

It went around the first bend. It came back into view for a moment, smaller. Then the second bend. It flickered, far off, between the dark shapes of the moored boats at the edge of the harbor. It flickered again.

Then it was gone.

---

She did not come back up the stair.

Esdel waited. He counted — his heartbeat, the rain, the knock of a loose shutter somewhere in the house behind them. He looked at the candle stub in his hand, sheltered in the lee of the wall. The fourth notch was almost gone.

She did not come.

He went down.

The stone stair was slick and treacherous, and the river was right there at the bottom of it, loud and brown and hungry, slapping at the edge of the landing. He went carefully. He stopped on the last step, beside her.

She did not turn her head. She was still looking at the place where the light had been.

He did not say anything. He had learned, in a year, that there were times when nothing was the only thing worth saying. He only stood beside her on the step above, in the rain, and looked where she was looking. At the dark river, and the black shapes of the boats, and the harbor beyond them, and somewhere very far beyond that, invisible in the storm, the sea.

After a long time, she spoke.

“She said she’d name him after my father.”

Esdel waited.

“I told her not to. I told her it would be dangerous. Someone might hear it, someday, and remember.” Her voice was low and flat and very tired. “She said she’d do it anyway. She said she’d give him Father’s name, and no one would ever know it but her. That she’d whisper it to him at night. His real name.” A pause. “She never does what I tell her. She never has.”

The river roared past below them.

“I’ll never see him again,” said Lián Xueyao. It was not a question. It was not grief, even. It was simply a fact, laid down on the wet stone between them, like a soldier laying down a sword. “Or her. I’ll never know what name she gives him. Or where they go. Or whether they get there.” She was silent a moment. “That’s the point. That was the whole point. If I don’t know, no one can make me tell.”

“Yes,” said Esdel.

She turned her head then, finally, and looked at him — standing on the step above her with the candle stub in his hand and the rain running off his hair.

“You know what that’s like,” she said.

It was not a question either.

He thought of a lamp on a stair. A girl in a doorway at dawn with her apron damp with dew. A necklace put into his hand. *I’ll be waiting. No matter how long it takes.* He thought of all the letters he had burned. He thought of the scrap of paper tucked behind the silver flower at his throat, with one word on it, which he would never send.

“Yes,” he said.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded once, and turned, and put her hand briefly on his arm — the first time she had ever touched him — and went past him up the stair.

He looked back once, at the river, before he followed. At the dark water, and the empty place where the light had been.

Somewhere down there, very far away now, a small barge with a patched awning was going south in the storm, toward the sea, with a sleeping child aboard who would never know his name.

He hoped they got there. He found he was praying it, without meaning to — to what, he did not know. Not to the river gods of Edenridge. Not to anything in particular. Just to the dark.

Then he went up the stair after her, and closed the iron door behind them, and they went back through the roaring garden toward the stables, where the soldiers of House Lián were waiting with the horses in the rain.

Behind them, in the house, the candle in Esdel’s narrow room at the end of the east wing burned down past its fourth notch, and began on the fifth.

And far across the gardens, above the west gallery, in a bedchamber where the curtains were drawn and the lamp was out, a man who had not eaten from the main kitchen opened his clear grey eyes in the dark, and lay very still, and listened to the silence of his house.
