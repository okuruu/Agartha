# **Chapter 78**

The fever broke before dawn on the fourth day.

Xueyao had not slept. She had sat all night in the corridor outside her mother’s chambers, on the floor, with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, the way she had slept in trenches — because the Count had placed a maid at the door, and the maid had said, very politely, *the Lady is not to be disturbed*, and the General of the Northern Front had found that she did not have it in her to break a frightened girl’s arm to get past her.

So she sat outside. And near the grey hour, the crying on the other side of the door went quiet. And a little after that, she heard her mother’s voice — hoarse, and thin, and very clear — say to the maid:

“Where are the orchids?”

---

Lady Wei Anlan went down to the garden alone that morning, in her nightrobe, with her hair loose and her feet bare on the cold stones.

No one stopped her. The maid at the door had fallen asleep on her stool at last. The household was not yet stirring. The Count, who walked beneath the peach tree every morning before the house woke, had been called away the night before to the palace, and had not returned. The gardens were empty and grey and silver with dew, and the peach tree stood in the middle of them like something out of a dream, its blossoms beginning to fall now, drifting down onto the dark water of the pond.

Xueyao followed her at a distance. She did not dare come closer. Esdel, who had been in the stillroom all night and had seen them pass the stables, followed Xueyao — and stopped at the edge of the gallery, as he always did, where the shadows were.

Lady Anlan did not go to the pavilion. She walked straight past it, past the pond, past the peach tree, to a long low bed at the foot of the south wall where rows of orchids stood in stone pots beneath a slatted roof. Most of them had been moved inside, into the General’s sitting room. But a few remained — the oldest ones, too big to carry, their leaves thick and dark and their flower spikes bent over with the weight of pale green blooms.

She stood in front of them for a long time.

Then she knelt — slowly, carefully, the way a woman kneels whose knees are stiff and whose body has forgotten itself — and reached out one trembling hand, and touched a leaf.

Her fingers moved along it. Down to the base, where it met the stem. There was a small clean cut there, old, healed over — the mark of a blade that had once, long ago, trimmed away a spent sheath.

Her hand stopped.

“He trimmed too much,” she whispered. “She always said he trimmed too much.”

And then she bent forward over the orchids, and put her hands over her face, and made a sound Esdel would not forget. It was not weeping. It was lower than weeping, and older. It was the sound of something that had been frozen for a year beginning, all at once, to break.

---

Xueyao went to her.

She crossed the garden at a run — the first time Esdel had ever seen her run anywhere — and fell to her knees on the wet stones beside her mother, and put her arms around her.

For a moment Lady Anlan went rigid. She turned her head. She stared at her daughter with wide, wet, frightened eyes, as though she did not know her.

Then something moved in her face.

“*Yaoyao?*”

Xueyao made a small sound in her throat.

“Yaoyao,” said her mother again. Her hands came up, trembling, and touched Xueyao’s face — her cheeks, her hair, her brow — the way a blind woman touches a face to learn it. “Oh. Oh, look at you. You’re so thin. When did you get so—” She stopped. Her eyes went past Xueyao, to the house, the peach tree, the pavilion by the pond. “When did you come home? Why didn’t anyone tell me you were—”

And then her face changed again.

Esdel saw it happen. Saw the memory come up in her like a stone rising from the bottom of a pond — slow, and dark, and heavy — and break the surface.

“Where is your father?” said Lady Anlan.

Xueyao did not answer.

“Yaoyao. Where is your father?” Her voice was rising. “He went to the harbor. He went to see the ship. My brother’s ship, from Nanyue — I sent the jade with him — he said he’d be back for supper, he said—”

She stopped.

Esdel watched her remember.

He watched her remember all of it, there on her knees in the wet grey garden, in front of the orchids her husband had trimmed too short. The harbor. The crates. The guards. The tribunal. The decree with its blood-red seal. The morning of the execution, and the silence after, and the long weeks of silence after that. And a soft voice at the door. And a box of lacquered rosewood, and two vials of amber, and a warmth that spread through her chest like mercy.

She did not scream. She had gone past screaming. She only knelt there with her daughter’s face between her hands, and her mouth open, and no sound coming out of it at all.

Then she said, in a small, dead, perfectly lucid voice:

“I married him.”

“Mother—”

“I *married* him.” Her hands fell from Xueyao’s face. She stared down at them, as though they belonged to someone else. “Yaoyao. I married the man who— He stood on the docks. He *stood* there. He opened the crates. And I—” Her breath came short and ragged. “I let him sit in your father’s chair. I let him pour my tea. I let him—” Her whole body shuddered. “Why? Why would I do that? I can’t — I can’t remember *why*. I remember being so tired. And then he came, and I wasn’t tired anymore. I remember being so *grateful*—”

“It wasn’t you,” said Xueyao.

Her mother looked at her.

“It wasn’t you,” Xueyao said again, fiercely, taking her mother’s shaking hands in both of hers. “It was the tonic. He put something in it. Every day. To make you soft, and sleepy, and — it wasn’t you, Mother. You didn’t choose it. He did it *to* you.”

Esdel, in the shadow of the gallery, closed his eyes.

*To make you soft, and sleepy.* That was all she knew. That was all he had told her. She did not know about the gold oil, or the mouse that followed Pei’s hand. She did not know that her mother had not only been dulled but bound — that the gratitude, and the warmth, and the way she had turned to the Count like a flower, might have been poured into her one amber drop at a time.

And her mother would never know either. Not from him.

He did not know if that was mercy or cowardice. He was beginning to think there was not always a difference.

---

They stayed in the garden until the sun came up over the east wall and turned the dew to gold.

Lady Anlan did not weep again. She sat on the stone edge of the orchid bed with her daughter beside her and her hands folded in her lap, very straight-backed, very still, and she talked. Not about the Count. About small things. About Xueyao as a girl, and the mare in the stables, and the silk gloves. About the peach tree, and the year the iron bands had to be put on it after the storm split the trunk. About Lord Hengzhi, pruning his orchids every morning before breakfast, and how she used to stand at the window and scold him for trimming too much, and how he never once listened.

Her voice was hoarse and thin and exhausted. It was also, unmistakably, her own.

Esdel had never heard it before. He had only heard the soft, sweet, drifting voice of the pavilion. This one was different. It was dry, and quick, and a little sharp at the edges, and it made small precise jokes and then waited, with the faint impatience of a clever woman, for other people to catch up. He thought: *Changli was right. She would have frightened people.*

He thought: *Xueyao has her voice.*

As the light grew, Lady Anlan turned and looked at the house. At the gallery, and the east wing, and the long pale roof beyond it where the nursery was, with its window open to the spring air.

“Yueming,” she said.

“He’s well,” said Xueyao. “He’s asleep. I’ve seen him every day.”

Her mother nodded slowly. Then she turned and took Xueyao’s face between her hands again — gently this time, steadily, her fingers not trembling at all.

“Listen to me,” said Lady Wei Anlan. “I don’t know how long I have like this. Clear. I can feel it — something wanting to pull me back down. Like a tide.” Her eyes searched her daughter’s face. “So listen. Whatever happens to me, you keep him safe. Your brother. You keep him away from that man. Do you understand me, Yaoyao? Not the house. Not the name. Not the ships, not the gold. *Him.*”

“Mother—”

“Promise me.”

Xueyao looked at her mother for a long moment, in the golden light, beneath the falling petals.

“I promise,” she said.

Lady Anlan let out a breath. Some of the terrible straightness went out of her back. She leaned — slowly, heavily, like a woman who has carried something a very long way — against her daughter’s shoulder, and closed her eyes.

“Good,” she murmured. “Good girl. You always kept your promises. Even when you were small.” A faint, tired curve of the lips. “Your father used to say it was the most inconvenient thing about you.”

Xueyao put her arm around her, and held her, and looked out over her mother’s bowed head at the garden, and the peach tree, and the pale roof of the nursery, with an expression Esdel could not read.

Then her eyes moved, and found him in the shadow of the gallery. She had known he was there. Of course she had.

She did not look away. Neither did he. They stood like that — she on the stones with her mother in her arms, he in the shadows — while the sun climbed and the house began to wake around them, and somewhere at the front of the estate, faint and far, there came the clatter of hooves in the courtyard and the creak of the great gate opening.

The Count was home.
