# **Chapter 70**

In Edenridge, the river was running high with snowmelt, and the willows along its banks had come into leaf, and it had been a year.

Lyora knew the day exactly. She had not meant to count. But there was a morning, early in spring, when she went out to the garden and found the first green shoots of the mint coming up between the stones — and she stood there with the watering can in her hands and thought, *this is when the caravan came.* A year ago, almost to the hour. The shouting in the lane. The cart. The man on the stretcher with his lips cracked white and sand in his hair, whom she had thought was dead.

She had set the watering can down on the path, very carefully, and gone inside, and ground a whole morning’s worth of feverfew before she trusted her face again.

---

The winter had been long. That was what everyone said, and it was true. The snow had come early and stayed late, and the river had frozen at the edges for the first time in a decade, and old Richard had slipped on the ice by the shrine and cracked two ribs and complained about it, magnificently, until the thaw.

The bamboo channels had held. Reyn had gone out along them every week in the worst of the cold, knocking the ice from the joints with a mallet, muttering at them as though they were disobedient children. *He built them too well. He built them so well I can’t even complain properly.* In the spring, when the melt came down off the hills and the water rose, the channels had carried it up to the high fields without a single joint giving way. The farmers said it was the best early water in living memory.

No one said his name when they said it. But everyone knew whose hands had tied the knots.

The lamp still burned at the foot of the stairs.

She had not decided to keep lighting it. She had simply never stopped. Every night, all through the long dark winter, she had turned it low and set it on the second step and gone to bed. Arwen had never said a word. Once, in the depth of midwinter, when the oil ran short and the traders could not get up the road through the drifts, Lyora had found that her grandmother had quietly given up the lamp in her own room so there would be enough for the one on the stairs.

Neither of them spoke of that either.

---

Eldric’s father was walking without the cane now.

He came down to the apothecary in the first week of spring to prove it, stomping in through the door and doing a slow, triumphant circuit of the shop while Eldric stood in the doorway with his arms folded, trying not to grin.

“Look at that,” the old man said. “Like a young buck.”

“Like a young buck with a limp,” said Arwen.

“A *distinguished* limp.” He sat down heavily on the bench and beamed at Lyora. “It was your salves, girl. And your feverfew. Eldric told me you broke your ankle going up the ridge for it.”

“Twisted,” said Lyora. “Not broken.”

“Twisted, broken.” He waved his hand. “You did it for me. I don’t forget that.” He looked at her a little longer than he needed to, and then at his son, and then away, and Lyora pretended very hard to be busy with the jars.

When they left, Eldric lingered at the door. He did not say anything. He never did, anymore. He only looked at the lamp on the second stair — unlit now, in the daylight, its glass clean and its wick trimmed — and then at her.

“Still?” he said quietly.

She did not pretend not to understand. “Still.”

He nodded. He did not smile, exactly; but his face did something gentle and tired and kind. Then he went out after his father into the spring sunshine, and she heard the old man say *well?* and Eldric say *leave it, Da*, and then their voices went away up the lane.

---

No letters had come.

She had not really expected any. She did not even know if soldiers could send letters, from wherever they were — whether there were messengers, or post-riders, or whether a letter from the front simply vanished into the great grey machinery of the war and was never seen again. She did not know where he was. She did not know if he was alive.

She wrote to him anyway.

She had started in the autumn, on the night the first frost came and she could not sleep. She wrote at the kitchen table by the light of a single candle, and she did not put his name at the top, and she did not sign her own at the bottom. She wrote about small things. The harvest coming in. Old Richard’s ribs. The cat, who had taken to sleeping on the third stair — the silent one — as if waiting for someone to come down it.

She never sent them. She had nowhere to send them to. She folded each one in half, and in half again, and put it in the drawer of her worktable, beneath the ledger, where Grandmother would not look.

There were a great many of them now.

---

On a warm evening in the middle of spring, a soldier came through Edenridge.

He was alone, on foot, with a bandaged hand and a pack on his back, walking home from somewhere to somewhere else. He stopped at the apothecary to have the bandage changed. Lyora changed it. He was young and very tired and grateful, and when she asked — very casually, as though it were nothing — whether he had served in the supply companies, he said yes. The Third Logistic, under Captain Erven.

Her hands did not stop moving on the bandage. She was proud of that, afterward.

“There was a man,” she said. “From here. Last summer. He went with Captain Erven as a medic.” Her hands kept moving. “In my place. His name was Esdel.”

The soldier frowned, thinking. Then his face cleared.

“The apothecary,” he said. “Aye. Tall, quiet? I remember him. He wasn’t with us long. There was an ambush in the forest, and afterward — gods, the way he—” He stopped, and looked at her, and seemed to decide something. “He was with us to the front. Then they put him in the medical tents at the Eastern Post. I heard he did good work there. The old surgeon thought the world of him.” He shrugged. “Then he was transferred. North, I think. To one of the ridge posts.”

“And after that?”

The soldier shook his head. “I don’t know, miss. That was a long time ago. But—” He hesitated, and then, perhaps because she had been kind to him, or perhaps because of something he saw in her face, he said, “He was alive. Last I heard. Alive and working. That’s worth something, at the front.”

She finished the bandage. She tied it off. She told him to keep it dry, and he thanked her, and went on down the lane toward the river road with the sun setting red behind him.

She stood in the doorway until he was gone.

Then she went back inside, and sat down at the kitchen table, and put her face in her hands, and did not cry. She only sat there, very still, breathing, while the light went gold and then grey around her. *Alive. Alive and working.*

When it was dark, she got up. She trimmed the wick of the lamp on the stairs, and filled it, and lit it, and turned it low.

Then she took a fresh sheet of paper from the drawer, and sat down at the kitchen table again, and began — without a name at the top, without a name at the bottom — to write.
