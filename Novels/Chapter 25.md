# **Chapter 25**

For three days after the night of the dream, the house was careful with itself.

Nothing had changed, and everything had. Arwen still rose before the light and set the kettle on the hearth. The jars still stood in their long rows along the shelves, catching the morning like water caught in glass. The cat still pretended not to be waiting for him at the foot of the stairs.

But Lyora set his cup down a little closer to his hand than she used to. Close enough that he did not have to reach.

He noticed. He noticed everything now — the way she hummed, and then stopped when he came into the room, as if the song belonged to someone else; the way her eyes found his across the worktable and slid away, not quickly, as they used to, but slowly, reluctantly, like a hand leaving a warm stone.

Neither of them spoke of that night. Of his tears. Of the three of them holding one another in the lamplight until the flame went out. It sat between them, soft and unnamed, like a bruise that did not hurt unless you pressed it.

He did not press it.

---

On the fourth morning, Arwen sent him to the well.

The sky was already white with heat. Cicadas sawed in the trees along the lane, and the dust rose in small, lazy puffs around his boots. At the well, two women were drawing water — aunties from the lower street, sleeves tied back, their voices carrying the easy volume of people who had known each other forty years and saw no reason to whisper.

He waited his turn by the old stone rim. They nodded to him kindly and went on talking, as though he were a tree.

“—after the harvest, she says. She’s already spoken to the go-between.”

“Well, it’s time. That boy’s carried the whole farm on his back since his father’s leg. Since spring. You’ve seen his mother’s hands.”

“Poor woman. And Lyora’s been up there every week with salves, hasn’t she? Feverfew for the fever, comfrey for the bone.”

“Every week.” A satisfied cluck. “Arwen’s girl and Eldric. Such a good match. Grew up in each other’s pockets, those two. Everyone always said.”

The rope creaked. The bucket rose, dripping silver.

“Eldric’s a good boy.”

“The best of them.”

Esdel stepped forward when they were done. He lowered the bucket, and drew it up, and filled both pails to the brim, and thanked them. They smiled at him and said *such a helpful young man*, and he walked home along the dusty lane with the pails swinging steady at his sides.

He did not spill a drop.

It was the only way anyone could have known something was wrong — had they been watching closely. How carefully he walked. As though the water were something that might break.

No one had asked what Lyora wanted. He noticed that, too. In all that easy, affectionate talk, no one had said her name as if it belonged to someone with a heart of her own.

But then, he thought, who was he to ask it?

---

He began to be busy.

Never before the shop. Whatever else he had taken on, he was at the worktable at first light, as he had been every morning since spring — sweeping, grinding, turning the bundles on the drying lines, copying the day’s receipts into his notebook in his small, strange hand. Arwen set out the work and he did it. If he did it with fewer jokes than usual, she did not remark on it.

It was after the shop that the rest began.

It was not difficult. There was always work in Edenridge for a man who wanted it. Reyn had a sluice gate that stuck. The Chief wanted the bridge planks replaced before the heavy rains. The baker’s oven door had warped in the heat. Esdel went where he was needed and stayed until the light failed, and some days after.

He came home when the lamps were low. He ate what was left, alone, after the others had finished. He said *good night* from the stairs.

Arwen watched him with her quiet green eyes and said nothing.

Lyora said, once, “You’re never here anymore.”

“Reyn needs help with the gate.”

“Reyn has needed help with that gate for six years.”

He almost smiled. He didn’t. “Then it’s time someone fixed it.”

She looked at him a moment longer, and he saw the question rise in her face and then sink, unasked, like a stone settling into a riverbed. She turned back to her mortar.

“Your supper’s by the stairs,” she said.

---

Twice that week, Arwen sent him up to Eldric’s farm in Lyora’s place.

“Lyora has the counter, and I have the ledgers,” she said, handing him the basket. “Comfrey for the poultice. Check the bindings at the ankle. And don’t let him talk you out of the willow.”

Eldric’s father lay on a pallet by the kitchen window with his leg bound between two lengths of ash, glaring at the yard he could not walk in. He was a big man gone thin, with Eldric’s shoulders and none of Eldric’s ease.

Esdel knelt and undid the linen. The swelling had gone down since Arwen last looked; the toes were warm and pink when he pressed them. He said so. He warmed the comfrey in a little water on the hearth until the kitchen smelled green and rank, like a ditch after rain, and laid it along the shin, and bound it again, firm but not tight, the way Arwen had shown him.

Then he measured the willow bark into a cup. A level spoon, no more, as it was written in his notebook.

The old man eyed it. “Tastes like a fence.”

“It does,” Esdel agreed. “Arwen says the worse it tastes, the better it works. I’ve never once been able to prove her wrong.”

That got a grunt that might have been a laugh. The old man drank. Halfway down, his jaw went tight — the leg, shifting — and Esdel said nothing more, only steadied the cup until it was done and eased the pillow under the knee.

At the door, Eldric’s mother pressed his hand between her worn ones and thanked him twice. Eldric was out in the fields. He always was.

It was a good house. Warm, and tired, and honest. He walked home down the hedgerow path and did not let himself think about why that should hurt.

---

Eldric came to the workshop on a blazing afternoon, a week into Esdel’s busyness, with straw in his hair and his hat in his hands.

Reyn looked up from the sluice gate, raised one copper eyebrow, and went very deliberately back to his work.

“Esdel.” Eldric’s grin was the same as ever — wide, sunlit, a little sheepish at the edges. “You got a moment? I need your hands. Mine only know dirt.”

Esdel set down the plane. “What for?”

Eldric turned the hat around in his fingers. For a man so broad, he looked suddenly young. “Lyora’s drying rack. The old one out back of the apothecary — it came down last week. A whole summer’s worth of feverfew in the mud. She laughed about it, but I saw her face.” He cleared his throat. “I’d like to make her a new one. A good one. Only everything I build leans.”

The workshop was very quiet. Outside, the water wheel turned, and turned.

“She’d like that,” Esdel said.

“You think so?” The relief in Eldric’s face was so plain it hurt to look at. “Will you help?”

There was no reason to say no. None that he could say out loud.

“Yes,” said Esdel.

---

They worked in the long gold hours after Eldric’s fields were done — three evenings, then four. Esdel cut the bamboo and burned the joints clean, the way he had for the channels. Eldric held what needed holding and fetched what needed fetching, and talked, because Eldric always talked. It poured out of him as easily as water from the channel mouths.

He talked about his father.

“Cart went over on the hill road in spring. Axle snapped, and his leg was under it.” He shook his head. “The bone’s knitting, Arwen says. Slow. But he’s too proud to lie still. Tried to stand on it last week and near fainted in the yard. Ma cried half the night.” A pause. “She doesn’t cry, Ma. Not ever.”

He talked about the fields — the south plot flooding, the east plot not flooding enough, the mule that hated him personally.

“Sometimes,” he said, sitting back on his heels with a bamboo pole across his knees, “I think I’ll never see anything past those fields. Not the city. Not the sea. Just the same furrows till I’m my father’s age and something falls on me too.” He laughed, but it was a thin sound. “Don’t tell anyone I said that. I’m meant to be the cheerful one.”

Esdel tightened a binding. “I won’t.”

Eldric was quiet for a while. The sun slid lower, laying long stripes of light across the workshop floor.

“She’s the only girl who never looked at me like I was the prize at the fair,” he said at last, softly. “You know? The others — they’d look at me like I was something to be won. Lyora just… brought medicine. Scolded me for not wrapping my hands. Like I was a person.” He rubbed the back of his neck. “I was stupid at the festival. Grabbed her hand like a boy. I think I frightened her.”

Esdel’s hands went still on the knot.

“I’ll say sorry,” Eldric said. “Properly. When I give her this.” He looked up, and his eyes were so honest, so entirely without guile, that Esdel had to look away. “Thank you, Esdel. Truly. You’re a good friend.”

---

Esdel built it with a care he could not hide.

He knew he should make it plain — serviceable, the kind of thing anyone might have built. Instead he found himself measuring the height of the rails against the memory of her reach, so she would not have to rise onto her toes. Sanding every edge so the bamboo would not snag her sleeves. Spacing the rungs for feverfew and chamomile and the long stems of valerian she liked to hang upside down.

And binding every joint with the same knot he had used on the channels — the one that pulled tighter the harder the water pressed against it. He did not decide to. His hands simply did it, the way they did most things, from some place beneath memory.

Reyn watched from the doorway on the last evening, arms folded, a smear of grease across his cheek.

“Beautiful work,” he said.

“It’s Eldric’s.”

“Is it.” Reyn’s gaze moved from the rack to Esdel’s face, and stayed there. He was a practical man; he did not deal in poetry. So when he spoke, it was plainly, the way he would tell a man his beam was cracked.

“You’re building the door you mean to walk out of.”

Esdel said nothing. He pulled the last binding tight.

---

Eldric carried it to the apothecary the next morning, while Esdel was away at the bridge.

He heard about it afterward, from three different people. How Lyora had come out into the yard wiping her hands on her apron. How she had stopped, and put a hand to her mouth. How she had thanked Eldric so warmly that he went red to the ears — and how Eldric had apologized, for the festival, for the hand, and she had said *there’s nothing to forgive*, and meant it.

It was a good story. The village liked it very much.

---

That evening, when the house was quiet, Lyora went out into the yard alone.

The new rack stood against the south wall where the light was best, already hung with the first bundles of feverfew, their small white faces nodding in the dusk. She reached up — it was exactly the height of her reach — and touched the top rail.

Her thumb found a binding.

She stopped.

It was a small knot, tight and neat, the cord wound back through itself in a way no one in Edenridge wound cord. She had seen it a hundred times on the channels by the river, holding back the water. She had watched a pair of hands tie it, patient and precise, on a hot afternoon at the start of the summer.

She stood there a long time with her thumb resting on it, as if it were a pulse.

Then she went inside.

She did not say anything. Not to Arwen. Not to anyone.

But that night, when she set his supper at the foot of the stairs, she covered it with a cloth and laid a sprig of mint on top, the way she used to when he was sick.

In the morning it was still there. Cold. Untouched.

She took it away without a word, and scraped the plate, and washed it, and set it back on the shelf.
