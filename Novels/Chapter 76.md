# **Chapter 76**

The Count came to see him three days later.

He came in the evening, alone, without a servant — walking down the narrow corridor of the east wing with his black cane tapping softly on the boards — and he knocked on Esdel’s door, which no one in the Lián house had ever done. The servants opened doors. The General summoned. Changli simply walked in.

The Count knocked, and waited, and when Esdel opened the door, he smiled.

“Apothecary,” he said. “Forgive the hour. May I come in?”

It was not a question. Esdel stood aside.

The Count came in and looked around the narrow room with polite interest. At the bed. At the chests. At the table beneath the window, cleared now — the flasks washed and packed away, the tin sheet scoured, the baskets gone. The mice had been released in the kitchen garden, at dusk, the morning after the hearing. Pei had done it. He had not said anything about it, and neither had Esdel.

“Plain,” the Count said, approvingly. “I like a plain room. It tells you a man isn’t hiding anything.” He lowered himself onto the single chair, the cane across his knees, and looked up at Esdel with his clear grey eyes. “Please. Sit.”

Esdel sat on the edge of the bed.

---

“I wanted to tell you,” the Count said, “that I bear you no ill will.”

He said it simply, warmly, the way a man says a thing he has thought about and means. The lamplight softened the lines of his face. He looked, sitting there, like someone’s kindly uncle come to visit a sick nephew.

“You did what you were asked. You were brought here by the General, you serve the General, and the General asked you to find poison in a bottle.” He spread one hand, gently. “So you found it. I don’t blame a soldier for obeying his commander. Even when the commander is—” He paused, delicately. “Unwell.”

Esdel said nothing.

“I read your notes, you know,” said the Count. “Grand Physician Morrell was kind enough to send me a copy. Remarkable work. Truly. I understood perhaps a third of it.” He smiled. “Morrell understood perhaps half. He was very disturbed by it. He said he had never seen reasoning like it in fifty years. He said it was like reading a book written in a language that does not exist.”

A small silence.

“He is an old man and a fool,” the Count said lightly, “and I told him so. A mind like yours is wasted in a field tent. Wasted on mice.” He leaned forward a little. “I have a physician, Apothecary. Master Vale. You have met him. He is loyal, and competent, and old, and has no imagination whatever. I could use a man with imagination. A post in my household. Rooms of your own — real rooms, not a cupboard at the end of the servants’ corridor. A salary. A laboratory. Whatever instruments you want, from wherever in the world you want them.” He paused. “And gold, of course. More than you would see in ten years at the front.”

“I’m bound to the General’s service,” said Esdel. “Until my levy ends.”

“Levies can be bought out. I would buy it.”

“And I’ve given my word to her.”

“Ah.” The Count sat back. He regarded Esdel for a long moment with an expression of gentle, rueful amusement, the way one might regard a child who has refused a sweet out of stubbornness. “Your word. Yes. You strike me as a man who keeps it.” He tapped his fingers on the cane. “It is a rare quality. I admire it. I really do.”

He rose. Esdel rose with him, out of courtesy.

The Count went to the door. He put his hand on the latch. And then, as though it were an afterthought — as though it had only just drifted into his mind — he turned back.

“I had your service record brought up from the Ministry of War,” he said. “Forgive me. I like to know who is living under my roof. It was very short, your record. Almost nothing in it. No birthplace. No family. Just a levy, taken up by the Third Logistic, last summer, from a village somewhere near the desert’s edge.” He smiled. “*Edenridge.*”

Esdel did not move. He did not let one single muscle of his face move. He thought afterward that it was the hardest thing he had ever done.

“What a pretty name,” said the Count softly. “Edenridge. It sounds like a place where people are very kind. Where they would take in a stranger from the desert, and nurse him, and never ask where he came from.” He tilted his head. “I imagine they miss you there. I imagine someone is waiting.”

The lamp hissed on the table.

“Good night, Apothecary,” said Count Aldric Reng. “Think about my offer. There is no hurry. I am a patient man.”

He went out. The door closed. His cane tapped softly away down the corridor, unhurried, and was gone.

---

Esdel sat down on the bed.

He sat there for a long time, while the lamp burned down. He was not aware of time passing. He was aware only of his own heartbeat — that even, precise, borrowed heartbeat, which did not stumble even now — and of a cold that had begun somewhere in the center of his chest and was spreading outward, slowly, into his arms and hands.

*I imagine someone is waiting.*

He had never said her name. Not here. Not at the front. Not to Halden, not to Benn, not to the General, not to anyone. He had kept it inside him the whole year like a coal carried in a clay pot, and never once let anyone see the light of it.

And it did not matter. The Count did not need her name. He had a village, and a map, and time, and a patient mind. It would be very easy. A letter to the right man. A rider on the south road.

He thought of the apothecary. The ivy on the walls. The rows of jars catching the morning. The lamp at the foot of the stairs.

He thought of Lyora sitting on the second step in the dark, saying *people leave this house*.

---

He did not sleep. Near midnight he got up, and took the writing board, and sat down on the floor beneath the window with a stub of charcoal and a fresh sheet of paper, as he had done so many nights on the road.

He did not write. He sat with the charcoal in his hand and the paper blank on the board, and he understood — slowly, the way cold water rises — that he could not write to her anymore. Not even letters he did not send. Because a man who was patient, and had read his service record, might one day have his room searched. And might one day find a sheaf of folded papers, unaddressed, unsigned, that spoke of a river and a garden and a lamp on a stair.

He took the letters out of his coat. All of them. The whole thin sheaf, soft now at the folds from a winter of being carried against his chest.

He sat with them in his lap for a long time.

Then he fed them, one by one, into the flame of the lamp, and held each until it caught, and set it burning in the copper dish on the floor, and watched it curl and blacken and go to ash. He did not read them first. He knew what they said.

The last one he held longest. It was the first one he had written, in the ruined inn on the road, the night the General told him to send them before he was somewhere they couldn’t reach.

It had only one line.

He burned that too.

When they were gone, he sat on the floor with the copper dish of ash in front of him and his hand pressed flat against his chest, over the small silver flower on its chain, until the window went grey.

He did not take the necklace off. He thought about it. He thought about it for a long time. And then he did not, because there was a promise in it, and he had never broken that one, and he did not know how to begin.
