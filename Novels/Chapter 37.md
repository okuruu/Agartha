# **Chapter 37**

The rumor reached the Lián house with the morning bread. *The King is going to make the Count guardian of the Lián heir.*

“The Heavenly Dragon,” said Xueyao, in her sitting room, with her mother asleep behind the half-open door, “is bound to the Lián blood. Not to me. When I die, it will pass to the next who can bear it. I have no children. There is only one other.”

Han Ruo made a sound in his throat.

“He’s a *baby*,” said Pei Lun from the corridor, where he had been listening without permission.

“Yes. And the man who raises him will teach him what to love, and whom to obey.” She looked at her mother’s sleeping form. “I don’t think the Count wants my mother’s dowry, Captain. I think he never did.”

That night Esdel sat with a loop of cord in his lap and his fingers working, three knots, then three, and did not know what he was tying toward.

---

For six days, Lady Wei Anlan was herself.

Not every hour. In the afternoons, when the light went long and amber, her eyes would drift toward the pavilion, and her hands would begin to tremble. “It wants me to go to him,” she said, very precisely, the way she said everything now. “I hate him. I *know* I hate him. And still.”

Esdel, who had come in with the bitter bark tea he brewed to steady her heart, stood very still with the cup in his hand.

“Will it pass, apothecary?” Her eyes were sharp and grey and too clear.

“I don’t know. I hope so.”

She held out her hand for the cup. Her fingers closed over his, dry, cool, very steady, and she said, so low that her daughter could not hear: “You’re kind. I can tell. You have a kind man’s hands.” A pause. “Kind men should learn to lie better.”

She drank, and made a face. “Gods, it tastes like a boot.”

Xueyao laughed. It was the first time Esdel had ever heard her laugh.

They were good days. He would think that, afterward, when he let himself think of them at all. Xueyao slept on the floor by her mother’s bed, and in the long mornings they talked, the way people talk who have been parted a long time and know, without saying it, that time is short. In the afternoons Changli brought Yueming, and Lady Anlan held him up to the window until the sunlight made him blink and squirm and laugh. “He has your father’s ears. That little fold at the top. You grew out of it.” She kissed the top of the baby’s head. “I hope he doesn’t.”

On the fifth day a letter came sealed in red wax with a silver serpent. Lady Anlan read it by the window and folded it neatly, in half and in half again. “Burn it. He says he loves me. That he is patient. That he will be waiting in the pavilion, at the amber hour, as always.” She looked out at the empty pavilion. “He wrote it beautifully. I almost went.”

That night, near midnight, far off on the other side of the gardens, Esdel heard a door close softly. He listened for footsteps, and there were none, and after a while he told himself it was the wind.

---

Changli found her at dawn.

She had fallen asleep in the chair, not deeply, she would say afterward, over and over, *not deeply, I would have heard*. Lady Wei Anlan lay on her back with her hands folded on the coverlet, her face peaceful, and faintly, strangely smiling, as though she were having a pleasant dream.

She was not breathing.

Esdel knelt by the bed and did what he knew how to do. The lips were faintly blue. The muscles slack, entirely slack. He lifted an eyelid with his thumb. The pupil was a pinpoint. *It quiets the muscles,* Halden had said, over a boy in a tent. *All of them. In the end, the ones that breathe.*

On the table stood his own clay cup, the one he had brought her bitter bark tea in last night. It was empty. He held it to his face. Beneath the bark, very faint — sweet. Slow. Amber.

He had not put that there.

He understood it all, kneeling in the grey dawn, the way he understood the angle of a slope: clean, and certain, and terrible. The Count had felt her hand shake and understood that someone had taken his wife back from him. In another week she would be clear enough to speak, not in a garden before forty witnesses who could be made to forget, but before the court, with her own dry, quick, frightening voice. About the docks. About the crates.

A woman like that could not be allowed to wake.

*You didn’t choose it,* Xueyao had said in the garden. But Esdel had chosen. He had drawn her up out of the fog, and made her clear, and sharp, and dangerous, with his own hands, and loved the work. And the cure had made her a threat. And the threat had been removed.

Changli wept in the corner with her face in her hands. Han Ruo wept in the doorway, silently, his great shoulders shaking. Xueyao stood at the foot of the bed looking at the loose hair on the pillow, dark and silver, which she had brushed out last night while her mother scolded her for pulling, and made no sound at all.

“How?” she said at last.

He told her. He did not soften any of it. He told her it was because her mother had been getting better. That it was the waking that had killed her. He waited for her to understand whose hands had made the waking, and saw her understand, slow and cold, and braced himself for what would be in her face.

It was not blame. It was recognition. The look of one soldier to another, across a field, when both have given an order that got someone killed, and neither will ever be able to put it down.

“You gave her six days,” said Lián Xueyao. Her voice was perfectly steady. “He took a year from her. You gave her back six days. She knew me. She held my brother. She laughed.” She turned back to the bed. “Don’t you dare,” she said, very quietly, “ever, *ever* be sorry for that.”

She knelt on the far side and held one of the folded hands against her forehead. Esdel stayed where he was, with his hands open on his thighs. They were the hands she had called kind. He did not know what to do with them.

---

Master Vale found that the Lady’s heart, long weakened by grief, had failed in her sleep. Grand Physician Morrell countersigned it without reading it twice. No one asked the General’s apothecary what he had found.

On the third day of the funeral, with six hundred mourners kneeling in white, the King laid a single flower beside Lady Anlan’s folded hands. “The Lady Wei Anlan leaves behind a son. Count Aldric Reng, the Crown names you guardian of Lián Yueming, and steward of the Lián estate, until the boy is of age.”

When the Count straightened, there were tears in his grey eyes. “I will raise him as my own.” And then he looked across the bier at the General standing at its foot, with such sorrow, such gentleness, such understanding.

Esdel, at the wall, was the only one close enough to see her hands. Her fingers curled inward, one by one, until the nails pressed into the palms, and stayed there, perfectly still, while a thin bright line of red ran from her knuckles onto the white of her mourning robe. Across the hall, beside his father, the Crown Prince kept his eyes on the floor, and a muscle jumped in his jaw like something trying to get out.

They buried her on the fourth morning in the Lián tombs on the hill above the river. When the mourners had gone, the General stayed until noon, alone, her right hand flat against the cold stone of the door. Esdel waited under a pine, where he could see her. He did not try to hear what she said.

When she came down the hill she stopped beside him, and did not look at him.

“She made me promise,” said Xueyao. “In the garden. To keep him safe.”

“I know,” said Esdel. “I heard.”

She nodded once, and walked on to where Changli waited with the horses.

He stood a moment longer under the pine, with his hand over the silver flower. It was warm. It had been warm all day, as if it were trying to tell him that he was still someone who could be touched. He did not know what to say to it either.
