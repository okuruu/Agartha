# **Chapter 80**

For six days, Lady Wei Anlan was herself.

Not all at once. Not every hour. There were times — in the afternoons, mostly, when the light went long and amber across the garden — when something seemed to pull at her, and her eyes would drift toward the window, toward the pavilion, and a small puzzled crease would come between her brows, as though she were listening for a voice that was not there. Her hands would begin to tremble. Once, she stood up from her chair and walked halfway to the door before she stopped, and stood there, and did not know why she had risen.

“It wants me to go to him,” she said to Xueyao, afterward. Very quietly. Very precisely, the way she said everything now. “Something in me. It still wants to go and sit beside him and take his hand.” She looked down at her own hands. “I don’t understand it. I hate him. I *know* I hate him. And still.”

Esdel, who was in the room to bring her the bitter bark tea he had brewed to steady her heart, stood very still with the cup in his hand.

Xueyao said, “It’s the tonic. It will pass.”

“Will it?” Her mother looked up — not at Xueyao, but past her, at Esdel. Her eyes were sharp and grey and too clear. “Will it pass, apothecary?”

He did not know what his face was doing. He hoped it was nothing.

“I believe so,” he said. “In time.”

Lady Anlan looked at him for a long moment. Then she held out her hand for the cup. And when she took it from him, her fingers closed over his, briefly — dry, cool, very steady — and she said, so low that her daughter could not hear:

“You’re kind. I can tell. You have a kind man’s hands.” A pause. “Kind men should learn to lie better.”

She let go, and drank the tea, and made a face at the bitterness, and said, “Gods, it tastes like a boot.”

Xueyao laughed. It was the first time Esdel had ever heard her laugh.

---

They were good days. He would think that, afterward, when he let himself think about them at all.

Xueyao did not leave her mother’s side. She slept on the floor by her bed, and ate her meals at the little table by the window, and in the long mornings they talked — about nothing and everything, the way people talk who have been parted a long time and know, without saying it, that time is short. Esdel heard pieces of it, coming and going with his cups and his powders. Xueyao’s voice, lower and softer than he had ever heard it. Her mother’s, dry and quick. Once, both of them laughing at something, helplessly, the way Lyora and Arwen had laughed on the hill road, a world away.

In the afternoons, Changli brought Yueming.

Lady Anlan would hold him on her lap by the window, in the good light, and look at him — not the soft, drifting look of the pavilion, but a real one. A mother’s look. Counting his fingers. Smoothing his hair. Holding him up to the window so the sunlight fell on his face and made him blink and squirm and laugh.

“He has your father’s ears,” she told Xueyao. “Look. That little fold at the top. You had it too, when you were born. You grew out of it.” She kissed the top of the baby’s head. “I hope he doesn’t.”

The Count did not come.

He sent flowers every morning — white camellias, a great cool armful of them, which Xueyao had taken out to the kitchen middens every morning, unopened. He sent notes, in his beautiful hand, asking after his wife’s health, which Xueyao burned. Master Vale came to the door twice more, with his leather case. Twice more, the General met him there.

*The Count will be informed.*

On the fifth day, the steward came, in person, with a letter sealed in red wax with a silver serpent.

It was addressed to Lady Wei Anlan. Xueyao took it from him at the door.

She did not open it. She stood there with it in her hand, looking at the seal, for a long moment. Then she gave it to her mother.

Lady Anlan broke the seal. She read it by the window, in the good light, with her lips pressed together. When she was done, she folded it very neatly, in half and in half again, and held it out to her daughter.

“Burn it,” she said.

“What does it say?”

“That he loves me. That he is patient. That he understands I have been unwell, and that when I am recovered, he will be waiting in the pavilion, as always, at the amber hour. That he will always wait.” She looked out the window at the empty pavilion. “He wrote it beautifully. I almost went.”

Xueyao took the letter. She burned it in the brazier while her mother watched.

That night, for the first time, Xueyao did not sleep on the floor. Her mother sent her away — *go and sleep in a bed, you look like a corpse, you’re frightening the baby* — and she went, protesting, to her own room across the corridor. Changli stayed in the chair. Han Ruo sat in the corridor with his sword across his knees.

Esdel, at the end of the wing, lay awake a long time and listened to the house.

It was very quiet. The quietest night since they had come.

Near midnight, he heard a door, far off, on the other side of the gardens. He heard it close softly. He listened for footsteps, and there were none, and after a while he told himself it was the wind.

---

Changli found her at dawn.

She had fallen asleep in the chair — not deeply, she would say afterward, over and over, *not deeply, I would have heard, I would have heard* — and woke in the grey light with a crick in her neck, and got up to open the shutters, and looked at the bed.

Lady Wei Anlan lay on her back with her hands folded on the coverlet. Her eyes were closed. Her face was peaceful, and very pale, and faintly, strangely smiling, as though she were having a pleasant dream.

She was not breathing.

---

Esdel knelt by the bed.

He did not remember how he had come there. He remembered Changli’s voice in the corridor — not a scream, Changli did not scream, but a sound he had never heard from her, a high thin broken sound — and then running. And then the room, and the grey light, and the General standing at the foot of the bed with her hands at her sides. Not moving. Not speaking. Only looking.

He knelt. He did what he knew how to do.

The body was cool. Not cold. Some hours, then. Four, perhaps five. Midnight. The lips were faintly blue. The fingernails too. The muscles of the face, the jaw, the hands — slack, entirely slack, without any of the stiffness that should already have begun to come.

He lifted one of her eyelids with his thumb.

The pupil was very small. A pinpoint.

He knew what it was. He had seen it once before, in the tent at the Eastern Post — a young soldier, in agony from a belly wound, who had got hold of the surgeon’s greyveil and taken far, far too much. Halden had stood over him, afterward, with a face like stone. *It quiets the muscles,* he had said. *All of them. In the end, the ones that breathe.*

Esdel sat back on his heels.

He looked at the cup on the table beside the bed. It was his own cup, the plain clay one he had brought her bitter bark tea in last night, before she sent them all away. It was empty. He picked it up and held it to his face.

Beneath the bark, beneath the bitterness, very faint — sweet. Slow. Amber.

He had not put that there.

He set the cup down.

*Near midnight, a door. On the other side of the gardens. No footsteps.*

He understood it all, then. Kneeling on the floor of the dead woman’s room, in the grey dawn, with the smell of amber in his nose, he understood it the way he understood the angle of a slope — all at once, clean and certain and terrible.

The Count had noticed. Of course he had noticed. He had held her hand in the pavilion and felt it shake, and watched the tonic stop working, day by day, and understood that someone had reached into his house and taken his wife back from him. That she was waking. That in another week, or two, she would be clear enough to speak — not in a garden, before forty witnesses who could be made to forget, but before the court, before the King, with her own dry, quick, frightening voice, the voice that had once won an argument with the Minister of Rites. About the docks. About the crates. About the man who had stood there and opened them.

A woman like that could not be allowed to wake.

And so, while she slept, someone had come — someone who knew the house, who knew the doors, whom the maids would not question — and had poured into the cup at her bedside, the cup Esdel had left there, a dose of greyveil a hundred times what Vale had ever given her. And she had woken in the night, thirsty, as the sick do, and reached for the cup, and drunk it. And lain back. And her muscles had quieted, one by one, very gently. Until the ones that breathed.

*He did it to her,* Xueyao had said in the garden. *You didn’t choose it.*

But Esdel had.

He had chosen to draw her up out of the fog. Had chosen to make her clear, and sharp, and dangerous. He had brewed it with his own hands, night after night, in the old stillroom, and loved the work, and been ashamed of loving it. He had done it to save her.

And the cure had made her a threat. And the threat had been removed.

He knelt on the floor beside the bed, with his hands on his knees, and did not move.

---

Behind him, the General had not moved either.

She stood at the foot of her mother’s bed and looked at her mother’s face. At the faint strange smile on it. The folded hands. The loose hair on the pillow, dark and silver, which Xueyao herself had brushed out last night, in the lamplight, while her mother scolded her for pulling.

She did not weep. She did not speak. Changli was weeping — in the corner, with her face in her hands — and Han Ruo in the doorway was weeping, silently, his great shoulders shaking. Pei Lun had turned and walked out into the corridor and was hitting the wall, over and over, with the flat of his hand.

Xueyao stood at the foot of the bed like a pillar of ivory, and did not make a sound.

After a long time, she said, “How?”

Esdel told her. He did not soften any of it. He told her about the pinpoint pupils and the slack muscles and the blue lips and the sweetness in the cup. He told her about the door at midnight.

He told her that it was because she had been getting better. That it was the waking that had killed her.

He waited for her to understand what that meant. Whose hands had made the waking.

She understood. He saw her understand. He saw it go through her, slow and cold, and settle.

And then she turned her head and looked at him, kneeling on the floor beside her mother’s body — and he braced himself for what would be in her face.

It was not blame.

It was something much worse. It was recognition. The look of one soldier to another, across a field, when both of them have given an order that got someone killed, and both of them know it, and neither of them will ever be able to put it down.

“You gave her six days,” said Lián Xueyao.

Her voice was perfectly steady.

“He took a year from her. You gave her back six days. She knew me. She held my brother. She laughed.” She turned back to the bed. “Don’t you dare,” she said, very quietly, “ever, *ever* be sorry for that.”

Then she went and knelt beside her mother, on the other side of the bed from Esdel, and took one of the folded hands in both of hers, and held it against her forehead, and stayed like that — while the sun came up over the garden, and the peach blossoms fell, and somewhere across the house, on the other side of the gardens, a door opened, and a cane began to tap, slowly, unhurriedly, along the gallery toward them.
