# **Chapter 95**

The herald came to Edenridge on the hottest day of the summer, when the wheat in the high fields had just begun to turn.

He came up the river road at noon on a tall bay horse, with two soldiers riding behind him in the Crown’s blue and a leather case slung across his saddle, and the village watched him come the way villages watch anything strange — from doorways, and over fences, and through the gaps in the shutters. No herald had come to Edenridge in living memory. The Chief could not remember one. Old Richard, who remembered everything and complained about most of it, said there had been one in his grandfather’s time, to announce the old King’s coronation, and that it had rained.

It did not rain now. The sun stood white and fierce over the square, and the cicadas sawed in the willows, and the herald dismounted beneath the great tree where the wishing ribbons had hung the summer before, and took a scroll from his leather case, and nailed it to the trunk with four iron nails.

Then he stood beside it and waited, while the village gathered.

---

Lyora was in the apothecary when she heard the bell.

It was the shrine bell, down by the river — the small bronze one that the keeper rang for births and deaths and the Devotional Gathering. It rang now in a way she had never heard it rung, quick and uneven, the way a child rings a bell to call people in from the fields. She put down the pestle.

Arwen was at the window. She had been sitting there all morning, in the good light, with a basket of mending in her lap that she had not touched. Her hands lay folded on the basket. They were trembling.

“Someone’s come,” said Arwen.

They went down together. Lyora gave her grandmother her arm on the lane, the way she did now, without either of them mentioning it. The whole village was going the same way — farmers in from the fields with the chaff still in their hair, women with babies on their hips, children running ahead. Reyn came out of his workshop wiping his hands on a rag and fell in beside them without a word. Eldric came up from the southern fields with his father limping behind him.

The square was full by the time they reached it. The herald stood beneath the great tree with the two soldiers on either side of him, in the Crown’s blue, sweating in the heat. The scroll was nailed to the trunk behind him. It was very long.

He waited until the bell stopped. Then he read it.

---

He read it in a clear, bored, carrying voice, the voice of a man who had read the same words in forty market squares in forty days and would read them in forty more. *By the will of the Crown. In the name of the safety of the realm.* The words went out over the square in the white heat, and the village stood and listened, and did not understand most of them.

A general. A traitor. A dragon, and a stolen child, and a gate forced in a storm. It was a story from very far away — from the capital, from the war, from the great world beyond the forest that Edenridge had never had much to do with and did not much want to. People shifted in the heat. A baby cried and was hushed. Somewhere at the back, someone whispered *what’s a Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon* and someone else whispered *shh*.

Then the herald began to read the names.

Lyora was not really listening. She was watching Arwen, beside her, whose face had gone very pale in the heat. She was thinking she should take her grandmother home, out of the sun. She was thinking about the feverfew that needed hanging, and the jars that needed labeling, and whether there would be rain this week for the wheat.

“*—and the apothecary Esdel,*” read the herald, “*of no family, formerly of the Third Logistic Company and the medical division of the North Ridge post, late of the village of Edenridge.*”

---

The square went silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The kind of silence that has weight — that falls over a crowd all at once, the way snow falls off a roof, and does not lift. The cicadas were still singing in the willows. The river was still running beyond the shrine. But in the square beneath the great tree, where the wishing ribbons had hung the summer before, no one made a sound.

The herald did not seem to notice. He went on reading. *A reward of five thousand gold crowns. One thousand for any of her accomplices. Living or dead.* He rolled up his copy of the scroll, and put it back in his leather case, and looked around at the silent square with mild, sweating impatience, as though waiting for someone to ask him a question so he could leave.

No one asked him anything.

No one looked at Lyora. That was the thing she would remember. The whole village of Edenridge stood in the white heat of the square, and every one of them — every farmer and every mother and every child — very carefully did not look at the apothecary’s granddaughter. They looked at the herald, and the scroll, and the dust, and the great tree. They looked anywhere but at her.

She stood very still, with her grandmother’s arm through hers.

She did not faint. She did not cry out. She only stood there, in the silence, with the herald’s words lying on the dust of the square in front of her like something spilled — *living or dead, living or dead* — and felt her grandmother’s arm, against hers, begin very slightly to shake.

*Alive,* she thought.

It was the only word she could hold on to. It came up through her out of nowhere, clear and steady, like a lamp being lit in a dark room.

*Living or dead.* They did not know which. They did not know where he was. They were looking for him. Which meant he was not in a grave, or a ditch, or a burned field at the front. Which meant he was somewhere. Running, or hiding, or—

*Alive.*

---

It was Reyn who moved first.

He stepped forward out of the crowd — a big dusty man with a copper beard and a rag in his hand — and walked up to the great tree, and stood in front of the scroll, and read it. His lips moved. He read it slowly, all the way down, the way he read a plan for a bridge.

Then he turned around and looked at the herald.

“That’s a lie,” he said.

The herald blinked at him.

“That man,” said Reyn Challon, and his voice was not loud, but it carried in the silent square the way it had once carried over the fields on the day the water ran uphill through the bamboo. “That man lived in this village one spring and one summer. He fixed our fences. He carried our water. He made the river climb that hill when every enchanter in the valley said it couldn’t be done.” He pointed up the slope, toward the high fields, where the channels caught the white sunlight like threads of glass. “He never raised his voice. He never took a coin he didn’t earn. He’d sooner cut his own hand off than hurt a child.” His jaw was working under the copper beard. “Whatever that paper says he did — it’s a lie.”

The herald looked at him with polite, sweating disinterest.

“I only read them, friend,” he said. “I don’t write them.”

He turned to mount his horse.

“Then take a message back to whoever does,” said Reyn.

The herald paused, one foot in the stirrup.

Reyn looked around the square. At the silent faces. At the Chief, standing near the front with his silver hair tied back and his hands shaking at his sides. At old Richard, and the baker, and the women from the lower street who drew water at the well. At Eldric, standing very still at the edge of the crowd with his father beside him. At the apothecary’s granddaughter, pale as paper in the white heat, with her grandmother’s arm through hers.

“Tell them,” said Reyn, “that Edenridge doesn’t know anything about any traitor.”

He looked back at the herald.

“We only knew an apothecary,” he said. “And he was a good man.”

---

No one else spoke. No one needed to. But the silence in the square changed. Lyora felt it change. It was not the heavy, falling silence anymore. It was something else — something that stood up straight, the way a field of wheat stands up after the wind has passed.

The herald looked at Reyn a moment longer. Then he shrugged, and mounted his horse, and rode out of the square with his two soldiers behind him, and down the river road, and did not look back.

The scroll stayed nailed to the great tree.

No one took it down. No one touched it. It stayed there in the white heat of the afternoon, and the village went slowly back to its fields and its houses and its work, and no one looked at it as they passed.

---

That evening, two men came up the river road on foot and took a room at the inn.

They were not soldiers. They wore plain grey clothes and carried plain packs and said they were wool buyers from the eastern valleys, come to look at the summer clip. They paid in good silver. They were polite. They drank a cup of wine each in the common room and went up early to bed.

In the morning, they did not go to look at any sheep.

They sat on the bench outside the inn, in the shade of the eaves, and watched the lane. The lane that ran up from the square, past the well, past the shrine, toward the old house at the edge of the village with ivy on its walls.

They sat there all day. They were there the next day, too. And the next.

---

Lyora walked home from the square that afternoon with her grandmother on her arm, very slowly, up the lane in the heat. Neither of them spoke. Arwen’s steps were uneven, and Lyora matched them, and the cicadas sang in the ivy, and the old house waited for them at the top of the lane with its door standing open and its jars catching the light.

She put Arwen in her chair by the window, in the good light. She made tea. She put the cup into her grandmother’s trembling hands and folded them around it, the way Arwen had once folded hers.

Then she went into the kitchen, and sat down at the table, and did not move until it was dark.

When it was dark, she got up. She took the lamp from the shelf. She filled it, and trimmed the wick, and struck the flint — once, steadily — and lit it. She turned it low.

She carried it to the foot of the stairs, and set it on the second step, where it belonged.

Then she stood looking at it for a long time. At the small gold flame. At the third stair above it, silent now, that had not creaked in a year.

*Late of the village of Edenridge.*

He was alive. She held on to that. She sat down on the second step beside the lamp and held on to it with both hands, the way she had held on to the edge of the worktable in the spring when the soldier said *alive and working*.

And then, because the house was very quiet, and the night was very long, the other thing came.

She did not want it. She could feel it coming the way you feel a fever coming — a coldness first, at the back of the neck. And then the herald’s bored voice again, in her head, reading it out under the great tree. *The woman Lián Xueyao. Formerly General of the Northern Front. Formerly styled Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon.* And after it, in a list with fifteen soldiers, his name.

Not hers. *Hers.*

She had heard them whispering in the square, afterward, as the crowd broke up. The Maiden. They said she rode a dragon made of gold. They said she had golden eyes. They said she was the most beautiful woman in the Kingdom, and the most terrible, and that she had fled into the night in a storm with a handful of her father’s soldiers — and with him.

He had stood beside her in a storm. He had forced a gate for her. He had drawn his sword for her, the whisperers said, at some river, against a Count. He had given up his name, and his levy, and his whole life in the Kingdom, and gone with her into the dark, and the Crown said he would be hanged for it.

For her.

It was not jealousy. Lyora knew what jealousy was. She had been jealous once, of a woman with blue hands who had only asked about a wheel, and it had been hot and small and shameful and it had burned the tea.

This was not that. This was cold. This was much worse.

*A year,* she thought. *More than a year.*

What was a spring and a summer in a valley, against a year at the war? Against a storm, and a gate, and a river, and a woman with golden eyes who needed him? He had carried her down off the ridge once, in the rain. How many people had he carried since? He had held her on the floor at the foot of these stairs until she could breathe again. How many people had he held since, in how many tents, in how many storms?

What was one girl with an apothecary’s apron and a lamp on a step, to a man who had forced the gates of the capital?

*He’ll remember us,* she told herself. *Of course he will.*

And a small cold voice answered: *He doesn’t remember anything. That’s the whole of him. He forgot everyone he ever loved once already. What makes you think you’re any different?*

She put her hands over her ears. As if that would help. As if it had come from outside.

She did not cry. She sat on the second step beside the lamp with her hands over her ears and her eyes wide open in the dark and did not cry, and the hours went by, and the lamp burned down, and she did not turn it up.

She had turned it up every night for a year, when it burned low. Every night. She did not do it now. She sat and watched the small flame shrink, and shrink, down toward the brass, and thought: *who am I keeping it for? For a man who went with her. For a man who’s gone.*

She let it gutter.

It went down to a blue bead on the wick. It flickered. It almost went out.

Almost.

---

It was the watchers that saved it.

She did not think of them until nearly dawn — until the window over the shop went grey, and she heard, very faintly, a door at the inn at the bottom of the lane. Footsteps. The creak of the bench under the eaves as two men sat down on it, in the grey light, to begin their first day of watching the apothecary’s door.

She lifted her head.

*Late of the village of Edenridge.*

They had not written *late of the capital.* They had not written *late of the Northern Front*, or *of the General’s household*, or of any of the places where he had stood in storms beside a woman with golden eyes. Out of a whole year at the war, out of every place he had ever been, they had chosen to write the name of one small village at the edge of the desert. And then they had sent two men to sit on a bench and watch one door.

You do not set a snare where the rabbit will never come.

They thought he would come here. The Crown of the whole Kingdom, and whoever stood behind it, with all their spies and all their riders — they thought that of every road in the world, the one he would try to take was the road to this door.

They knew him better than she had, tonight.

And he would know it too. Wherever he was. He would hear the herald in some other square, and hear the name of the village read out after his own, and understand — the way he understood everything, all at once, like the slope of a field — that there were men waiting at the bottom of her lane. And he would not come. Not because he had forgotten. Because he had not.

He would stay away. For her. For as long as it took. He would keep his promise to come back by never coming.

That was the thing that finally broke her. Not the decree. Not *living or dead*. Not the golden-eyed woman in the storm. That she had sat all night on the stairs and let the lamp die down, doubting him — while somewhere out in the dark he was running *away* from this door with everything in him, so that nothing would ever follow him to it.

She bent over her knees and pressed her face into her apron, and made no sound at all.

Arwen, in her chair by the window, did not come. But after a long time, Lyora heard the old woman get up — slowly, painfully — and cross the room, and stop in the doorway behind her. And stand there. Just stand there, in the grey, with her trembling hands at her sides, the way she had stood in the doorway of the attic a year ago, on the night of the dream.

Neither of them said anything.

After a long time, Lyora lifted her head. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, briskly.

The flame on the wick was a blue bead, barely alive.

She reached out and turned the lamp up. Slowly. Carefully. The bead caught, and grew, and steadied, and turned gold — and she kept turning, past where she had ever turned it before, until the small flame stood up bright and tall on the second stair and threw its light all the way across the dark shop to the door, and under it, and out into the lane.

Where two men in grey on a bench outside the inn could see it, if they liked. Let them.

It stayed lit.

It never went that low again.
