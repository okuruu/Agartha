# **Chapter 94**

The decree reached the Eastern Post on a wet morning, nailed to the post of the command tent by a courier who did not dismount.

Halden read it standing in the mud in his shirtsleeves, with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand and a bloodstained apron over his belly. He read it the way he read a wound — from the top, carefully, all the way to the bottom, without skipping. The traitor Lián Xueyao. The Heavenly Dragon. The infant, the guardian, the north gate. The list of names.

He got to the last one, and stopped.

He stood there a long time. Rain dripped off the edge of the tent roof onto his bald head. He did not seem to notice.

Benn found him there a quarter of an hour later, still standing in the mud, still holding the cold mug.

“Sir? The fever patients—”

Halden did not answer. He only lifted the mug, very slightly, and pointed with it at the decree.

Benn read it. He read it all the way to the end, as Halden had. Then he stood beside the old surgeon in the rain and did not say anything at all.

“*Of the village of Edenridge,*” said Halden at last. His voice was flat. “*Formerly of the Third Logistic Company and the medical division of the North Ridge post.*” He took a long breath through his nose. “They didn’t even put us in. The Eastern Post. A month he was here. A whole month, and it’s *North Ridge* on his traitor’s warrant.” He shook his head. “Typical.”

“Sir—”

“*Idiot,*” said Halden.

He said it very quietly. He did not say it the way he said it to Benn, forty times a day — sharp and weary and fond. He said it the way a man says a word he has been saving for a long time for exactly the right occasion, and has found it, and wishes he had not.

“I gave him that vial,” he said. “The blue one. For emergencies. I told him not to use it unless he had to. I told him to come back in one piece so I could give him a proper lecture.” He looked down at the cold coffee in his mug. “I never gave him the lecture.”

Benn swallowed. “Maybe he’s—”

“Oh, he’s alive.” Halden’s voice was very dry. “Of course he’s alive. They wouldn’t be offering a thousand crowns for him if he weren’t. Idiot. Backwater genius. Went off to the capital with a *general* and a *dragon* and got himself put on a *decree*.” He turned his head and spat in the mud. “Should have stayed here. Should have stayed in my tent boiling knives. I’d have kept him out of trouble.”

He was silent a moment. The rain dripped.

“No,” he said. “No, I wouldn’t. He’d have found some. He always did.”

He drank the cold coffee in one long swallow, and made a face, and handed the empty mug to Benn. Then he took the decree off the post. He did not tear it. He folded it, very carefully, along the creases, and put it into the pocket of his apron, over his heart, where he kept the small things he did not want to lose — a lucky coin, a letter from his sister, a stub of pencil.

“The fever patients,” he said. “Right.”

He went back into the tent. Benn stood alone in the rain for a while after he had gone, looking at the empty post where the decree had been. Then he went in after him.

That night, at the fire outside the medical tent, Garrick and Darius and the others sat without laughing, and no one told any ghost stories. And when a new recruit asked who the apothecary on the decree had been — the one with the funny name, from the desert village — no one answered him for a long time.

Then Darius said, “He was the one who nearly killed Garrick,” and everyone laughed, too loud, and then stopped, and looked at the fire.

---

At North Ridge, the dragon had not slept in a week.

The soldiers did not understand it. They had lived beneath it for three years — a vast golden shape coiled on the high rock of the eyrie above the post, like a mountain that breathed — and they had learned its moods the way sailors learn the sea. It slept for days after a battle, heavy and sated, and the whole post slept easier with it. It woke before a storm and lifted its great head and tasted the wind. It stirred, sometimes, in the night, and made a sound like distant thunder rolling in its throat, and the horses screamed in their lines and the new recruits wet their bedrolls, and the old soldiers turned over and said, *it’s dreaming. Go back to sleep.*

It had never been like this.

It had begun on a night in early summer — the soldiers did not know which, afterward; they argued about it. A night with no moon. Some said there had been a storm in the south; they had seen lightning flicker on the horizon, very far away. That night, near midnight, the dragon had lifted its head from the rock of the eyrie, all at once, as though someone had called its name.

And it had looked south.

It had not stopped looking since.

It did not eat. It did not sleep. It lay on the high rock with its great head raised and its gold eyes fixed on the southern horizon, and it did not move, for days — except, sometimes, at night, when it would rise up suddenly to its full terrible height against the stars and stand there, swaying, with its vast wings half-spread, making a sound none of the soldiers had ever heard it make before. Not the thunder. Something higher. Longer. A long, rising, keening note that went on and on until it hurt the teeth, and echoed off the rocks of the ridge, and made grown men in the trenches below put their hands over their ears.

The old soldiers did not say *it’s dreaming* anymore. They did not say anything at all.

The enemy heard it too. Across the no-man’s-land, in their own trenches, the enemy soldiers heard the dragon keening in the night — and for the first time in three years, they did not fall silent and afraid. They listened. And their officers listened. And on the fifth night, the enemy came over the lines in the dark, the way they had not dared to come since the Titan fell.

The dragon did not rise to meet them.

It lay on its rock and looked south, and keened, and the soldiers of North Ridge fought alone in the dark for the first time in three years, and lost a mile of ground, and three hundred men.

The next morning, the decree arrived.

---

Loryn read it in the medical tent.

He was lying on a cot with a bandage round his thigh where an enemy spear had gone in during the night fighting — not deep, the surgeon said, he’d walk again — and someone had brought the decree in to read aloud, because the wounded liked news and there was never enough of it. A young clerk stood at the end of the tent and read it in a high, nervous voice, all the way through.

Loryn was not really listening. His leg hurt. He was thinking about his mother, and whether she would hear about the night fighting, and whether she would worry. Then the clerk read out the last name.

He sat up.

“*The apothecary Esdel,*” the clerk was saying. “*Of no family. Formerly of the Third Logistic Company and the medical division of the North Ridge post—*”

Loryn did not hear the rest.

He was remembering a morning, almost a year ago. A road from the Eastern Post, and a red sunrise, and a tall quiet man with a medical satchel riding behind him on his horse. *Esdel, right? Name’s Loryn. I’ll be your escort.* And then the plain, and the fire, and the Titan falling out of the sky toward them, and the man flying off the horse into the smoke. And Loryn searching for him afterward, for hours, through the burning field, with his arm broken, calling his name. He had not found him. He had been sure he was dead.

And then, weeks later, back at North Ridge with his arm in a splint, he had seen him. Walking across the camp toward the medical tents, with his satchel over his shoulder. Alive.

Loryn had never gone up to him. He had meant to. He had thought about it for seven months — every time he saw the tall quiet man crossing the camp, or bent over a stretcher, or sitting alone outside the tents in the evening turning something silver over in his fingers. He had meant to go up and say, *I looked for you. I’m glad you’re alive. I’m sorry I lost you.* He had never done it. There had always been a reason not to.

And now there was a price on his head.

Loryn lay back down on the cot, slowly, and looked up at the canvas.

Above the post, on the high rock of the eyrie, the dragon was keening again. It had started up at noon, in the full light of day, for the first time. The sound came down over the camp and through the canvas of the tent and into Loryn’s bones, long and high and rising, like the cry of something that has lost the one thing in the world it was bound to, and does not understand where it has gone.

He closed his eyes, and listened to it, and did not know why — for the first time since his arm had been broken on the burning plain — he wanted to cry.
