# **Chapter 73**

The King gave a banquet in honor of the victory at North Ridge, and the General of the Northern Front was commanded to attend.

It was held in the Hall of Clear Waters, on the second terrace of the palace, where the floor was black marble polished to a mirror and the pillars were wrapped in gold. Three hundred guests. Musicians on a raised platform behind a screen of silk. Lanterns hung so thick from the carved ceiling that the whole hall seemed to float in a warm, trembling haze, and every surface — every goblet, every hairpin, every buckle — caught the light and threw it back, until the eye ached with glitter.

Esdel stood against the wall behind the General’s table, where the attendants stood, in the plain grey coat of a military apothecary. No one looked at him. That was the only mercy of the evening.

He had not wanted to come. Xueyao had not asked him if he wanted to.

“You will stand behind me,” she had said that afternoon, fastening her ceremonial armor with quick, sure hands — the ivory and gold she had worn on the day of the Titan, polished now until it shone like bone. “You will watch his cup. You will watch my mother’s. You will tell me afterward everything you see.”

So he watched.

---

Lady Anlan sat beside the Count at the high table, two places down from the King. She wore pale gold silk, and her hair was dressed with pearls, and she was beautiful — the kind of fragile, softened beauty that made people lower their voices when they spoke to her. She smiled often. She did not speak much. When the Count leaned to murmur in her ear, she laughed — a small, delighted sound, like a girl’s — and her hand went to his sleeve and stayed there.

Esdel watched the cups. The Count drank wine. Lady Anlan drank tea, pale and faintly gold, from a cup that no servant poured.

At the General’s table, Xueyao sat with a face like carved ivory and ate nothing at all.

The courtiers came to her in waves, as the evening went on. Generals, ministers, the wives of ministers. They bowed and praised her — the Titan, the dragon, the victory, the glory of the realm — and she answered each of them with the same few perfect sentences, in the same perfect tone. *You honor me. The victory belongs to many. The soldiers of the north deserve the realm’s thanks.* Esdel watched their faces as they turned away from her. Admiration. Awe. And beneath it, in almost all of them, something else. A small, wary relief, like people who have just stepped back from the edge of a cliff.

*They’re afraid of her,* he thought. *All of them.*

And then someone came who was not.

---

He was young — younger than Esdel, perhaps, or of an age — with dark hair tied back simply and eyes of a clear, startling blue. He wore dark green, very plain, with a single gold ring on his hand. He did not come in a wave with the others. He came alone, in a lull between them, and when the courtiers around the General’s table saw who it was, they fell back as though a wind had moved them.

“General Lián,” said the Crown Prince of the realm, and bowed.

Not deeply. Not the court bow, all sweeping sleeves and bent knees. A plain bow, soldier to soldier, the kind men gave each other at the front.

Xueyao rose. She bowed back — correctly, precisely, exactly as low as protocol required.

“Your Highness.”

“May I?” He gestured at the empty chair across from her. She inclined her head. He sat.

For a while he said nothing. He looked at her untouched plate. Then at her face.

“You hate this,” he said.

Esdel, against the wall, saw her go very slightly still.

“Your Highness is mistaken. It is an honor to—”

“I hate it too,” said Prince Ardbert. He picked up a small round fruit from the bowl between them and turned it in his fingers. “Three hundred people telling me what I already know, in words that mean nothing, while the food goes cold.” He glanced up. “I spent last spring in the famine villages in the south. We ate millet and turnip, and everyone said exactly what they meant, and I have never in my life been so happy.” He set the fruit down. “I thought you might understand that. Having come from the front.”

Xueyao looked at him for a long moment.

“The front,” she said, “is not happy.”

“No.” His face sobered. “I don’t suppose it is. Forgive me. That was a stupid thing to say to someone who has been there three years.”

It was the apology, Esdel thought, that did it. Not the words — the plainness of them. Something in Xueyao’s shoulders eased, by the width of a hair.

“It wasn’t stupid,” she said, quieter. “It was — honest. There isn’t much of that here.”

“No.” Ardbert smiled faintly. “There isn’t.” He was quiet a moment. Then: “We met, you know. Years ago. At my seventeenth birthday. Your father brought you. You were very correct, and you bowed exactly right, and you looked at me as if I were a person and not a crown.” He shrugged, a little embarrassed. “I don’t suppose you remember.”

“I remember,” said Xueyao.

He looked up, surprised.

“You were the only one in the room,” she said, “who didn’t look relieved when I walked away.”

The music swelled behind its silk screen. For a moment the two of them simply looked at each other across the table, in the warm, trembling lantern haze — two people who had spent their lives being looked at, finding, for the length of a breath, someone who was looking back.

Then Xueyao’s eyes moved past him, to the high table. And Esdel saw her face close again, like a door.

---

The Count had risen from his seat. He was coming down the steps from the high table, leaning lightly on his black cane, his smile warm and easy, nodding to the guests who bowed as he passed. He was coming toward them.

No — Esdel realized, a moment later. Not toward them. Past them. Toward a tall man in charcoal grey who stood alone by one of the gilded pillars with a cup of wine he had not drunk, watching the hall with pale, glacial eyes.

Grand Duke Rowan Hale.

The Count stopped beside him. The two men did not bow. They stood shoulder to shoulder, looking out at the banquet, like two old friends admiring a view — and Esdel, who had learned in a year of silences how to read the space between people, saw that it was nothing of the kind.

He was too far to hear. But he could see their mouths. And he could see the Duke’s face, which did not move at all, except that his jaw went very slightly tight, the way a man’s jaw goes tight when he is told something he already suspected and did not like.

The Count said something, smiling. The Duke replied — three or four words, clipped and cold.

The Count laughed. He patted the Duke’s arm, lightly, with two fingers, the way one might pat a dog. Then he turned and went back up the steps to his wife, who lit up at the sight of him like a lamp.

The Duke stood alone by the pillar. He looked, for a long moment, across the hall — directly at the General’s table. At Xueyao.

Then he set down his untouched wine and walked out.

---

“The leave,” Xueyao said, very low, in the carriage going home.

Esdel sat across from her. Changli sat beside her. Outside, the capital rolled past in the dark — lanterns and shuttered shops and the smell of the river.

“My three petitions,” Xueyao said. “Refused. Refused. Refused. Always the same words. *The war needs you.* Always the Grand Duke’s men in the Supreme Commander’s ear.” She was looking out the window. Her voice was flat. “And then the fourth. Granted. No explanation. I wondered why.”

“The Count,” said Changli quietly.

“The Count.” Xueyao’s reflection in the dark glass did not move. “Did you see the Duke’s face? He didn’t know. He wanted me at the front — far away, busy, useful, fighting his war. And the Count went around him. Brought me home.” She was silent a moment. “The Duke is not a man who likes to be gone around.”

“Why would the Count want you here?” Esdel asked.

Xueyao turned from the window and looked at him. In the swaying lantern light of the carriage, her golden eyes were very dark.

“I don’t know yet,” she said. “But I know he wants something. And I know it isn’t my mother anymore. He already has her.”

The carriage turned into the street that led to the river, and the Lián gate, and the red banner with its silver serpent, snapping in the dark.
