# **Chapter 81**

The Count’s grief was the most beautiful thing the capital had seen in a generation.

It began at the door of his wife’s chamber, that first morning, where he stopped — leaning on his cane, in his dove-grey coat, the light behind him — and looked at the bed, and at the General kneeling beside it, and at the apothecary on the floor. And his face broke. Slowly, like ice on a spring river. He took one step into the room, and then another, and then his cane slipped from his fingers and clattered on the boards, and he went down on his knees at the foot of the bed with a sound that was not a word.

The maids who had followed him wept to see it. The steward wept. Even Master Vale, standing behind him in the corridor with his leather case, took off his black cap and bowed his head.

Xueyao did not look up. She did not let go of her mother’s hand. She knelt on the far side of the bed and held it against her forehead, and did not move, and did not make a sound — and the Count knelt at the foot and wept for both of them, beautifully, until the maids had to help him to his feet and lead him away.

Esdel, on the floor, watched his face as he went.

At the door, just for a moment, the Count glanced back. Not at his wife. Not at the General. At the plain clay cup on the table by the bed.

Then he was gone, leaning on the steward’s arm, his shoulders shaking.

---

Master Vale examined the body within the hour. He found that the Lady’s heart, long weakened by grief and nervous exhaustion, had failed in her sleep. It was, he said, very sad. It was also, he said, not unexpected. He had feared it for some weeks. He had told the Count so.

He wrote it down in his small neat hand, and signed it, and sent it to the royal college, where Grand Physician Morrell countersigned it without reading it twice.

No one asked the General’s apothecary what he had found. It was not his place.

---

The funeral was held on the ninth day.

The Count spared nothing. The Lián house was hung in white from the gate to the peach tree — white silk on every pillar, white lanterns on every eave, white paper flowers heaped along the galleries until the whole estate seemed to have been buried in a soft, silent snow. Monks came from three temples to chant. Musicians played the old laments on the long flutes, day and night, so that the thin high sound drifted out over the walls and along the river and the whole eastern quarter of the city could not sleep for it.

Six hundred mourners came. Nobles and ministers and merchants, and the wives of all of them, in their white robes and their pale cosmetics, filing slowly through the great gate beneath the red banner — which had been taken down, for the funeral, and replaced with white. They filed past the bier in the hall, where Lady Wei Anlan lay in white silk and pearls with her hands folded and her face made beautiful by the embalmers. They bowed. They laid their white flowers at her feet. And then they turned — every one of them — to the Count.

He stood at the head of the bier, in white, leaning on his cane. He did not sit, though a chair had been brought for him. He stood for nine hours on the first day, and nine on the second, and received every mourner with his own hands, and thanked each of them for coming. He did not weep before them. He had wept enough, people whispered, in private — the servants had heard him, night after night, in the empty pavilion by the pond. Before the mourners, he was grave and gentle and steady, and when an old noblewoman broke down at the bier, he held her hands and comforted her.

*He loved her so,* the mourners said to one another, in the galleries, over the thin music of the flutes. *They say he sat beside her every day. Fed her with his own hands, when she could not eat.*

*And after what her daughter said to him. In his own garden.*

*He never once spoke against the girl. Not once. Can you imagine?*

---

The General stood at the foot of the bier.

She stood there for nine hours on the first day and nine on the second, in plain white, with her hair unbound in the old way of mourning daughters, and her hands at her sides. She did not receive the mourners. The mourners did not look at her. They filed past her as though she were a pillar, and bowed to the Count, and whispered.

Esdel stood behind her, at the wall, in the place of attendants. He had put on white as well. It felt like wearing someone else’s skin.

He watched her. He had nothing else to do. He watched her stand there hour after hour without moving, while six hundred people looked through her, and he thought of a thing she had said to him once, by a fire in a ruined inn. *I have never felt anything hate the way it hated them.* Her dragon. Speaking of the Kālākṣa.

He thought: *she’s holding it. Something like that. She’s holding it the way you hold a coal in a closed fist, so no one will see the light.*

And he thought of her dragon, far away at North Ridge, that could feel what she felt across any distance.

He wondered what it was feeling now.

---

On the third day, the King came.

He came at noon, with the Crown Prince at his side and a small train of ministers behind, and the whole hall knelt as he passed. He went to the bier. He looked at Lady Wei Anlan’s made-up face for a long moment, with no expression at all. Then he laid a single white flower on the silk beside her folded hands, and turned to the Count.

The Count went down on one knee, painfully, on his cane.

“Rise, Count Reng,” said the King. His voice carried through the hall — quiet, and tired, and very clear. “You have borne a great deal. The Crown has seen it.”

The Count rose. He bowed his head.

“The Lady Wei Anlan leaves behind her a son,” said the King. “The last heir of the Lián name. An infant, without a father, and now without a mother.” He paused. “Such a child cannot be left without a guardian. And there is no one in the realm who has shown more devotion to this house, in its misfortune, than the man who stood beside its lady to the end.”

There was a murmur through the hall. Soft, approving, like wind through wheat.

“Count Aldric Reng,” said the King, “the Crown names you guardian of Lián Yueming, and steward of the Lián estate, until the boy is of age. May you raise him to honor his mother’s memory.”

The Count bowed very low. When he straightened, there were tears standing in his clear grey eyes.

“I will raise him,” he said, “as my own.”

And then, for the first time in three days, he turned his head and looked across the bier — down the length of the white silk, past the folded hands and the pearls and the painted face — at the General standing at its foot.

He looked at her with such sorrow. Such gentleness. Such understanding.

Xueyao looked back.

Esdel, at the wall, was the only one close enough to see her hands. They were at her sides, as they had been for three days. And very slowly, as he watched, they closed. Not into fists. She did not make fists. Her fingers simply curled inward, one by one, until the nails were pressed into the palms — and then they stayed like that, perfectly still, while a thin bright line of red welled up between the knuckles of her right hand and ran down, drop by drop, onto the white silk of her mourning robe.

No one else saw. No one else was looking at her.

Across the hall, beside his father, the Crown Prince stood with his head bowed and his eyes on the floor. He did not look up once. But his jaw was clenched so hard that a muscle jumped in it, over and over, like something trying to get out.

---

They buried Lady Wei Anlan on the fourth morning, in the Lián family tombs on the hill above the river, beside an empty place where her husband should have lain. Lord Hengzhi had no grave. The Crown did not give graves to traitors.

The Count wept at the tomb, leaning on the arm of the Grand Duke, who had come in white with his pale face perfectly composed and did not once look at the man he was holding up.

When it was over, and the mourners had gone down the hill in their long white procession, the General stayed.

She stood before her mother’s tomb until noon, alone. Changli waited at the foot of the hill with the horses. Han Ruo and the soldiers of House Lián waited behind her, in a silent line. Esdel waited a little apart from them, beneath a pine tree, where he could see her.

At noon, she knelt. She put her right hand — the one that had bled — flat against the cold stone of the tomb door. She stayed like that for a long moment, with her head bowed.

Esdel was too far to hear what she said. He did not try to hear it. He looked away, down the hill, at the river shining in the sun, and the city beyond it, and the long pale roof of the Lián house, where somewhere a baby was waking in a nursery with a new guardian’s name on the paper beside his bed.

When he looked back, she was standing. Walking down the hill toward them, in her white robe, with her hair unbound and her face like carved ivory.

She stopped beside him. She did not look at him.

“She made me promise,” said Xueyao. “In the garden. To keep him safe.”

“I know,” said Esdel. “I heard.”

The General nodded once. Then she walked on, down the hill, to where Changli waited with the horses.

She did not say anything else. She did not need to. He had heard that too.
