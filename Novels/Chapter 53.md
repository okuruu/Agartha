# **Chapter 53**

Prince **Ardbert** leaned back against the cushioned seat, letting the hum of the carriage lull him. A mirror of fine silverwork hung opposite, its edges carved with delicate motifs of lilies and cranes. His reflection stared back — dark hair tied neatly at the nape, features refined but softened by the golden light, and eyes that, despite their calmness, carried something restless beneath.

For a while, he simply watched his reflection. Then, a thought surfaced, unbidden.

**House Lián.**

The name alone carried a weight few others could. The Liáns were merchants first, nobles second — but in a kingdom whose veins ran on trade, that distinction hardly mattered. Lord **Lián Hengzhi**, head of the house, controlled nearly half of the eastern ports. Ships under his banners crossed every known sea, and his word could bend the flow of commerce itself.

Ardbert had met the man countless times during court sessions and trade councils — a shrewd figure of unshakable composure, who spoke with the confidence of one accustomed to winning. But the prince had never minded him; the Liáns, unlike most nobles, earned their influence, rather than inherited it.

It was through Lord Lián that Ardbert first met **Lián Xueyao**.

He hadn’t thought much of it then — the introduction was formal, brief. It was during his **seventeenth birthday**, the great hall radiant with candlelight and laughter, where noble houses gathered to offer blessings that meant little and promises that meant even less. Amid the sea of painted smiles and glittering gowns, she had stood out not because she tried to — but because she *didn’t*.

Her bow had been graceful, her words measured. Yet it was her **eyes** — gold like sunlight reflected on still water — that caught him off guard.

Most faces that looked upon him did so with intent: ambition, envy, admiration, fear. Hers held none. There was no calculation in that gaze, no expectation. It was a look that acknowledged him as a person before a prince — and that, he realized later, was what made it unforgettable.

He saw her a few times after that. Always from a distance — the balcony of a royal banquet, the far end of a council corridor — never long enough to speak, never close enough to matter. But her image lingered, like a name one remembers without meaning to.

And sometimes, when the court grew too suffocating, or the voices of advisors too loud, his thoughts would drift — not toward her beauty, nor her title, nor her strange moniker as the *Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon* — but toward that quiet poise.

That stillness, unbothered by crowns or gold.

The carriage hit a small bump, and the prince blinked, the sound of the countryside returning to him. The air outside was changing — the green giving way to stone roads, the gentle expanse of farmland yielding to watchtowers and banners.

He turned his gaze outward once more. Farmers bowed as the royal insignia came into view; soldiers stood straighter at the roadside. The smell of rain lingered faintly, mixed with the scent of wheat and smoke.

There were times, Ardbert thought, when the weight of the crown felt lighter here, away from marble halls and whispered schemes. Here, where life grew simple and real. He could almost imagine a world where titles didn’t exist — where he could walk among them without the burden of name or expectation.

Almost.

The reflection in the mirror caught his eye again — the prince’s face returning his stare. Dark hair, the same shade as the King’s. Blue eyes, clear yet weary, reflecting both the lineage and the loneliness that came with it.

“Your Highness,” came a calm voice.

Ardbert turned. His attendant sat across from him — a tall man with a neatly trimmed beard, dressed in the modest black of royal service. His name was **Khalid**, a man of few words but sharp mind, the one person Ardbert trusted without reservation.

“We are nearing the palace gates,” Khalid said, bowing his head slightly. “Shall I signal the guard to prepare the entrance?”

Ardbert looked past him, toward the nearing stone arches rising in the distance — the first signs of the palace emerging through the mist.

For a moment, he didn’t answer. His gaze lingered on the fields fading behind the carriage — the children still running, the laughter growing faint, the ripples in the water catching the last of the morning sun.

“...Yes,” he said softly at last. “Let’s return.”

Khalid inclined his head. “As you wish, my prince.”

The carriage slowed as it reached the cobbled road, the sound of hooves echoing beneath the grand archway. The golden fields disappeared from view, swallowed by walls of white stone and banners that fluttered against the wind.

Ardbert glanced once more at his reflection — and in the mirrored eyes, he saw not a prince, but a man caught between the warmth of a world he admired and the weight of one he could not escape.

Then, as the gates closed behind him, the laughter of the fields faded entirely, and the air of duty returned like the slow closing of a door.
