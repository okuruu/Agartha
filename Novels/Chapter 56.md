# **Chapter 56**

Morning unfolded gently over the Lián estate.

Mist clung to the tiled roofs, drifting in pale ribbons over the sprawling courtyards. The gardens were awake with dew, the air tinged with jasmine and the faint rustle of bamboo leaves brushing against one another like whispered prayers. Servants moved quietly through the halls, their steps muted by years of discipline. In this house, even silence carried refinement.

Lord **Lián Hengzhi** sat beneath the peach trees, one hand resting over the head of his infant son, **Yueming**, who slept in a cradle beside him. The Lord’s robe, dyed in the muted gold of sunrise, fluttered faintly with the breeze. His face was that of a man who had won many things in life — wealth, power, and the respect of kings — and yet carried in his gaze the subtle weariness of one who had lost something dearer.

A teapot steamed softly before him. The scent of roasted oolong rose, curling upward like a sigh.

Behind him, the soft steps of **Lady Wei Anlan** drew near. She moved with the grace of a woman accustomed to calm — her silver hairpins gleamed like frost in her dark hair, her pale robe embroidered with pale cranes gliding through clouds. In her hands, she carried a folded cloth — the unfinished embroidery of a dragon winding through blossoms, gold threads trailing loose.

“You’ve been sitting here since dawn,” she said gently, taking a seat beside him. “If you keep that up, the servants will start believing you’ve turned into a peach tree yourself.”

Hengzhi’s lips curved faintly. “If I do, perhaps she’ll finally come home to see me bloom.”

A soft chuckle left Anlan. “You still wait for her every morning.”

He turned the teacup slowly in his hand, watching the steam twist and vanish. “I can’t help it,” he murmured. “Every sunrise feels like the one she might return with.”

The breeze stirred again. A single leaf, sun-yellowed at its edge, detached from the branch above and drifted down, landing on the cradle. The infant stirred, his tiny fingers curling in the air.

“Even Yueming seems to know her absence,” Anlan said quietly, brushing the leaf from his blanket. “He smiles when I tell him stories of her — as if he knows her voice already, though he has never heard it.”

“She used to hum by that window every night, before she left,” Hengzhi replied. “The same tune you sing to him now. She thought I didn’t notice.”

He exhaled through his nose, a sigh heavy with pride and ache. “Our daughter has grown into someone even I cannot protect.”

“Nor should you,” said Anlan softly, her eyes turned to the distant koi pond. “She carries more than our name now. She carries the people’s faith.”

For a long while, the only sound was the wind combing through the bamboo, and the soft murmur of water as koi rippled the pond surface in circles.

Hengzhi poured tea into two cups. The quiet act seemed sacred here — not ceremony, but affection disguised in habit. He handed one to his wife.

“When she returns,” he said at last, “no guards, no attendants. Just us. The four of us.”

Anlan smiled faintly. “A private dinner?”

“Yes,” he said, his eyes softening. “A family table. No gold plates, no court speeches. Only rice, soup, and warmth.”

She nodded, gaze distant and wistful. “She would like that. She never cared for the noise of our halls.”

Hengzhi chuckled under his breath. “No, she didn’t. Remember when she vanished from her debut banquet? I found her in the stables, feeding the mare with her silk gloves still on.”

Anlan’s laughter, low and warm, drifted between the peach trees. “And when you scolded her, she only said—”

“‘Even the horse was hungry, Father.’”  
They said it together, and both fell silent afterward — smiling faintly at the memory.

The moment lingered, fragile and golden.

The sun rose higher, pushing back the mist, revealing the wide courtyards and flowing banners of the **Lián estate** — the fortress of a family whose influence stretched across the eastern ports, whose name was uttered in the same breath as kings and princes. The servants continued their silent routines: sweeping, trimming, tending. The koi glimmered beneath the water’s surface, flashing orange and white.

But above it all, the Lord and Lady sat in stillness, two hearts woven by time, waiting for the return of the one thread that had gone missing.

A bell tolled faintly from the western gate. A messenger, perhaps. Hengzhi did not turn. He closed his eyes, listening to the rustle of the garden — the hush before change.

Anlan reached out, brushing her fingers against his hand.

“She’ll come home soon,” she whispered.

He nodded, though his throat tightened. “And when she does… we’ll make her favorite dish. Steamed fish with ginger.”

“Mm. And osmanthus wine,” she added. “Just one cup.”

“For all four of us,” he murmured.

They sat together under the pale gold of the summer morning, while the wind swept through the branches and the light fell through the leaves in slow, shifting coins — two parents and their sleeping child, unaware that the tide of fate was already shifting far beyond the garden walls.

For now, the Lián estate was peaceful.

Too peaceful.
