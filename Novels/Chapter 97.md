# **Chapter 97**

The Kingdom told stories about her. It could not help it.

By the end of the summer, they were in every tavern and every market and every barracks from the western harbor to the ridges of the north. The Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon, who had gone mad at the front and come home and stolen her brother out of his cradle, and cut down the brave Count on the banks of the Greenwater, and vanished into the night with a handful of her father’s old soldiers and an apothecary with bleeding eyes.

No one knew where she had gone. That was what made the stories grow.

*She’s gone east,* said the traders in the river towns, over their wine. *Into the borderlands. There’s nothing out there but rock and wind. She’ll die out there, and the crows’ll have her.*

*She’s gone north,* said the soldiers in the camps. *Back to the ridges. Back to it. She’ll call it down one night and burn the whole front — ours and theirs both. You’ll see.*

*She’s gone over the mountains,* said the old women at the wells. *To the lands beyond, where the dragons come from. To make some bargain. You mark me.*

*She has the boy,* they all said. *She has the boy still, and no one knows why. They say she means to use him. They say the dragon wants him.*

*They say,* the soldiers whispered in the camps, very late, when the fires were low, *she wanders the borderlands in white. With her hair cut off. Like a ghost. And her heart’s gone cold and hard as iron, and she’s only waiting. Waiting for the day. And on that day, she’ll come back.*

*And the dragon?*

*The dragon’s sleeping.*

That was the strangest story of all, and the soldiers at North Ridge swore it was true. The dragon had keened for nine days after the new moon. Nine days and nine nights, until the men in the trenches thought they would go mad from it. And on the tenth night, all at once, it had stopped.

It had not flown. It had not fed. It had not moved from the high rock of the eyrie. It had simply lowered its great golden head onto its coils, and closed its eyes, and gone still.

It had not woken since.

The enemy had come over the lines three times since then, in the dark, and the dragon had not woken. The front had fallen back two miles. The generals sent rider after rider to the capital, begging for another way to wake it, another hand to bind it — and the King’s own court magicians had come north and stood beneath the eyrie with their incantations and their chains of silver, and the dragon had not so much as stirred.

*It’s waiting,* the soldiers said. *It’s waiting for her.*

*It won’t wake for anyone else. Not ever. Not till she calls.*

*And when she calls—*

No one ever finished that sentence. They would look at the fire instead, and pull their cloaks tighter around them, and not say anything for a while.

---

In the palace, the King sat alone with a map.

It was not the old map. It was a new one — large, and precise, and very expensive, drawn by the royal surveyors in fine black ink on white vellum, showing every road and river and town in the Kingdom as it was now. It covered the whole of the table in the small private chamber with windows over the river.

It had pins in it. Many small pins, with colored heads. Red for the places where the traitor Lián Xueyao had been seen — or said to have been seen — by some shepherd or trader or frightened child. They were scattered all across the northern and eastern borderlands, in no pattern at all, like drops of blood flung from a blade.

Blue for the companies of the Crown’s soldiers riding out to look for her. There were a great many blue pins.

There was one other pin. It was white. It stood by itself, very small, far to the south and west of all the others, at the edge of the desert, in a green valley between low hills where a river ran. There was no name written beside it on the map; the place was too small. But the King knew what it was called. He had read the decree.

He looked at it for a long time.

He did not move it. He did not take it out. He only sat there in the lamplight with his hands folded on the edge of the map, looking at the small white pin in the green valley at the edge of the desert, while the river sounded beneath the windows.

After a while, he reached out and touched it, very lightly, with one fingertip.

Then he took his hand away, and turned down the lamp, and sat on in the dark.

---

In the Lián house — which was the Reng house now, by every document in the Ministry of Rites, though the old characters of the Lián name were still carved in the stone over the great gate — the Count sat in the nursery.

He sat there every evening. The servants whispered about it. At the amber hour, when the light went long across the garden and the maids used to carry the white tea things down to the pavilion by the pond, the Count would climb the stairs to the south gallery, alone, and go into the small warm room at the end of it, and sit down in the chair by the window beside the empty cot.

He did not do anything. He only sat.

His right hand lay in his lap, in its black silk sling. It had healed badly. The cords that the apothecary’s blade had cut across the back of his wrist had knit together short and hard, and the fingers had curled inward, and would not open again. He could not hold a pen with it. He could not hold a sword. He had learned to write with his left hand in his beautiful script, and it was almost as beautiful, and the court said it was a marvel.

In the first week after the decree, a clerk had come from the Ministry of Justice with a limner at his elbow — a nervous young man with a board and a box of charcoal — to take down the likenesses of the traitors for the bills. The General’s face they had already; half the capital had painted it. The soldiers of House Lián they had pieced together from the grooms and the kitchen maids. There remained only the apothecary.

*You saw him closer than anyone, my lord,* the clerk had said. *At the ford.*

The Count had considered the question with great courtesy.

*I’m afraid I barely remember him,* he said. *Brown eyes, I think. Neither tall nor short. The sort of man one passes in a corridor and forgets.* He smiled, apologetic, and lifted his bandaged hand a little in its sling. *It was dark, you understand. And I was occupied.*

The limner had drawn nothing. The clerk had written *no particular features* and gone away. And the Count had sat on alone, afterward, looking at the garden, and had not stopped smiling for some time.

A man with a face on every tree hides in the hills and never comes down. A man with no face begins, in time, to believe he is safe. He starts to go down to markets. He starts to think of roads.

And sooner or later, every road a man like that thinks of runs home.

He sat by the empty cot with his ruined hand in his lap and his clear grey eyes on the pillow, where the small shape of a child’s head was still pressed into the linen. The maids had not changed it. He had told them not to.

Sometimes, when he sat there long enough, in the silence, in the fading light, the servants passing in the gallery outside thought they heard him speaking. Softly. As though to someone in the room with him.

*Patience,* he would say. Or they thought he said. *Patience. I know. I know. She will come back. They always come back to the blood. And when she does—*

And then something else, too low to hear. And sometimes — the maids swore it, afterward, in the kitchen, with their voices dropped — sometimes he would stop, and tilt his head, and *listen*. As though someone had answered him.

There was no one else in the room.

The maids did not like to pass the nursery after the amber hour anymore. The steward had let two of them go for refusing. Master Vale, in the west wing, had begun to lock his door at night.

---

In his high study on the western hill, Grand Duke Rowan Hale did his arithmetic.

He had a new sheet of paper. There were four names on it now.

*Reng. Lián. The King.*

And beneath them, very small, a fourth, which he had added only that evening, after a long conversation with a bookkeeper’s cousin about a gold-threaded something that a maid had seen in a glass bottle on the Count’s nightstand, and a small grey man in the west wing who had begun, very quietly, to look for a ship.

*Vale.*

He looked at the four names for a long time. Then he drew a line from the fourth to the first, and a question mark beside it, and sat back in his chair, with the lamp burning low, and did not burn the paper.

It was the first time in forty years he had not known how a column would come out.

---

And at the north gate of the capital, at the top of the Hundred Steps, on a warm night at the end of the summer, the watch saw a man in a dark hooded cloak come walking up the slope alone.

He did not come to the gate. He stopped in the middle of the paved street, ten paces off, in the place where the lantern light from the gatehouse did not quite reach. He stood there for a long time, looking at the great doors, barred for the night, and the black iron portcullis above them.

The watch did not challenge him. There were twelve of them again now, since the new moon; the Grand Duke’s economies had been reversed by order of the Crown. But the captain of the watch had seen the face beneath the hood once, by accident, on the first night the man came — and had quietly told his men that the gentleman was to be left alone.

He came often. Not every night. But often.

He never did anything. He only stood there, in the dark, at the top of the Hundred Steps, looking north. Toward the road. The open country. The hills, and the borderlands beyond them, and whatever was out there in the wind and the dark.

After a while, he would pull his hood forward, and turn, and walk back down the long slope toward the palace. And an older man in plain black, who had been waiting in the shadows at the foot of the Hundred Steps all the while, would fall into step a pace behind him without a word.

The watch never learned his name. The captain would not tell them.

But the youngest of them — a boy of seventeen, new to the watch that summer — said once, at the end of a long night, that he had seen the man’s face in the lantern light as he turned away. And that his eyes were a very clear, very startling blue.

And that he had looked like someone who was waiting, the boy said, for a door to open that he had already closed with his own hands.
