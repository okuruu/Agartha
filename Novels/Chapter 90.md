# **Chapter 90**

The north gate of the capital stood at the top of a long, steep street called the Hundred Steps, though there were no steps anymore — only a paved slope, worn smooth by centuries of carts and feet and the hooves of armies going out to war. At the top of it, the walls rose sheer and pale out of the dark, and in them was the gate: two towers, a vaulted passage, a portcullis of black iron, and beyond it the great doors, barred for the night.

Ten riders came up the Hundred Steps in the last hour before dawn, in the rain.

Esdel counted them without meaning to. The General. Himself. Pei Lun, with a split lip and a bruise already darkening down one side of his face. Seven of the old soldiers of House Lián — eight of the fifteen left, counting Pei. Four in the gateway. Suyin gone down the river. And two more, somewhere back in the Street of Lanterns, when the Count’s men had come out of the dark a second time, and the two old soldiers had turned their horses without a word, as Han Ruo had, and not come back.

Ten. He kept counting it, over and over, as though the number might change.

The storm was passing. The rain had thinned to a cold steady drizzle, and in the east, behind them, over the river, the sky was beginning — very faintly — to go grey.

The gate was closed.

---

There were lanterns in the gatehouse. Esdel could see them through the arrow-slits — warm yellow squares in the pale stone. He could see figures moving behind them. Not many.

“Four,” said Xueyao, very low, reining in at the foot of the last slope. She was looking up at the towers with her soldier’s eyes, measuring. “Han Ruo said twelve.”

“Maybe the rest are sleeping,” said Pei Lun.

“Maybe.”

She did not sound as though she believed it. Neither did Esdel. The Count was a patient man. The Count thought of everything. There would be men in the passage. There would be men behind the doors, with bows. There would be a trap, and they would ride into it, and—

A door opened at the foot of the right-hand tower.

Two men came out into the drizzle. One of them carried a lantern. They walked down the slope toward the eleven riders — not fast, not slow — and stopped ten paces off, in the middle of the paved street, in the grey light.

The one with the lantern was older, in plain black, with a neat grey beard. The other was young, and tall, and wore a dark hooded cloak, and when he pushed the hood back, the lantern light fell on dark hair tied simply at the nape, and a pair of clear, startling blue eyes.

Xueyao went very still in her saddle.

“Your Highness,” she said.

---

Prince Ardbert looked up at her.

He looked at all of them — the ten riders, wet and filthy and bloodied, on steaming horses, at the top of the Hundred Steps in the dawn. At Pei Lun’s split lip. At Esdel, and the faint red still staining the whites of his eyes. At the drawn sword across the General’s saddle. At the place beside her where a captain should have been riding, and was not.

His face did not change. But Esdel, who had learned in a year to read the space between people, saw his hands, at his sides. They were shaking, very slightly.

“I heard the bell,” said Prince Ardbert. “From the palace. Everyone heard it.” He paused. “And then I heard that the Grand Duke’s nephew had cut the north watch to four men, for the new moon, to save the Crown’s money.”

Xueyao said nothing.

“I thought,” said the Prince, “that it was a strange night to save money.” A faint, humorless curve of his lips. “So I came to see who else might think so.”

Behind him, the older man — Khalid, the attendant — stood very still with the lantern, his eyes going constantly up and down the empty street.

“The Count will be here soon,” said Xueyao. Her voice was flat. “He’ll have men on the Hundred Steps within the hour. He knows the north road is the only one out.”

“Yes,” said Ardbert. “I know.”

“Then why are you here, Your Highness?”

He did not answer at once. He looked at her a long time in the grey light — at her face, white and set and streaming with rain, at her golden eyes.

“At the Hall of Remedies,” he said at last, “I didn’t speak.”

Xueyao was silent.

“My father looked at me, and I stopped. I have been stopping all my life, when my father looks at me. I stopped at the funeral. I stood beside him while he gave your brother to that man, and I looked at the floor, and I didn’t say one word.” His jaw tightened. “I told myself it was duty. That a prince doesn’t speak against his King in public. That there would be a better time.” He shook his head. “There isn’t a better time. There’s only this one.”

He turned.

“Khalid,” he said. “Open the gate.”

The older man hesitated — only for a heartbeat — and then bowed his head, and turned, and went back up the slope toward the tower with the lantern swinging.

---

They heard him inside. A voice raised in the passage — calm, clear, carrying authority. *Crown’s orders. By the hand of His Highness the Crown Prince. A courier party for the northern front, delayed by the storm. Open the gate.* Another voice, a guard’s, uncertain. A pause. Then the groan of the windlass, and the rattle of chains, and slowly, slowly, the black iron teeth of the portcullis began to rise.

The General did not move.

She sat on her horse in the drizzle, looking down at the Prince standing alone in the middle of the paved street.

“They’ll know,” she said. “The guards. They’ll tell him. Your father.”

“Yes.”

“He’ll know you let me through.”

“Yes.” Ardbert looked up at her. “I expect he’ll be very disappointed in me.” Something moved in his face that was almost a smile. “I find I don’t mind as much as I thought I would.”

The portcullis was halfway up. Beyond it, in the dark of the passage, they could hear the heavy scrape of the bar being drawn from the great doors.

Xueyao looked at him a moment longer. Then she urged her horse forward — three steps, four — until she was beside him, looking down.

“Why?” she said quietly.

He was silent a moment.

“At my seventeenth birthday,” he said, “three hundred people looked at me. And every one of them was thinking about the crown on my father’s head, and when it would be on mine, and what they could get from me when it was.” He looked up at her. “And one girl looked at me as if I were a person. That was all. Just a person.” He shrugged, a little helplessly. “I’ve never forgotten how that felt. I suppose I wanted to give it back. Once. Before it’s too late.”

The great doors groaned. Grey light spilled into the passage beyond the portcullis. The road. The open country. The north.

Xueyao reached down.

She did not take his hand. She only touched his shoulder, briefly, with two fingers — the way a soldier touches another soldier, in passing, on the field. Then she took her hand back.

“I remember how you looked at people too,” she said. “You looked at them the same way.”

Then she turned her horse, and rode through the gate.

The others followed her. One by one, under the rising iron teeth, into the passage, and through the great doors, and out onto the north road in the grey drizzling dawn. Pei Lun. The seven old soldiers. Esdel last of all.

As he passed the Prince, standing alone in the street with the rain on his dark hair, Ardbert looked up at him.

It was a brief look. A curious one. The look of a man seeing a stranger for the first time and wondering, idly, who he might be.

Esdel inclined his head, very slightly.

The Prince inclined his back.

Then Esdel was through the passage, and through the doors, and out onto the wet road, and the great grey open country of the north was spread before him under the paling sky — fields, and hedgerows, and far off, dark against the dawn, the first low line of hills.

Behind him, he heard the portcullis begin to come down.

He looked back once, before the road turned. Through the closing gap beneath the black iron teeth, he saw the Prince still standing in the middle of the street at the top of the Hundred Steps, alone, watching them go.

And far down below him, at the foot of the long slope, in the grey of the city, a line of torches coming up.
