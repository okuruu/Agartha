# **Chapter 69**

The banner over the gate was red.

That was the first thing. Before the walls, before the gatehouse, before the grooms running out into the street — the banner, deep red, snapping in the evening wind above the old gate of the Lián estate, and on it, stitched in silver thread, a serpent devouring its own tail.

Xueyao reined in beneath it.

Esdel, at the back of the column, could not see her face. He saw only her back — very straight, very still — and the way her hand had closed on the reins. Around him the fifteen soldiers of House Lián had gone silent. Han Ruo was staring up at the banner. His jaw was working, as though he were chewing something he could not swallow.

Beneath the serpent, carved into the stone lintel, the old characters of the Lián name were still there. Someone had not bothered to chisel them away. Somehow, that was worse.

“General,” said Changli, very softly.

Xueyao lifted her chin. She rode through the gate.

---

The courtyard had been swept. Lanterns had been lit along the covered walk, though it was not yet dark. Servants stood in two neat lines — new faces, most of them, in new livery the color of wine — and bowed low as the column clattered in over the stones.

And at the foot of the steps, waiting, stood Count Aldric Reng.

He was taller than Esdel had imagined. Silver-haired, straight-backed, dressed in soft dove grey with no ornament but a small silver clasp at his throat. He leaned lightly on a black cane, and when he smiled up at the General, it was the most perfect smile Esdel had ever seen on a human face. Warm. Welcoming. A little rueful. Exactly as much grief in it as the occasion required, and not one drop more.

“General Lián,” he said, and bowed — deeply, beautifully, the cane tucked beneath his arm. “Welcome home. We have counted the days.”

Xueyao dismounted. She did not bow back.

“Count Reng,” she said.

If the omission stung, he did not show it. His smile only deepened, as though she had done something charming.

“Your mother is resting,” he said. “The journey of her nerves has been — long. I thought it best she not stand in the cold. But she knows you are here. She has spoken of nothing else all week.” He turned his head, taking in the column, the soldiers, the mule with the medicine chests; his gaze passed over Esdel without pause, the way it might pass over furniture. “Your rooms are prepared, and your people’s. The east wing, as it always was. I had the orchids moved to your sitting room. I remembered you planted them.”

Xueyao’s hand, at her side, closed very slowly into a fist.

“You remembered,” she said.

“Of course.” Aldric’s eyes were grey and clear and kind. “Your father spoke of them often. He was proud of you, you know. Even at the end.”

For one moment the courtyard was so quiet Esdel could hear the lantern flames.

Then Xueyao said, “Take me to my mother,” and walked past him up the steps without another word.

---

Esdel did not see the meeting. The General’s apothecary had no place there. He was shown with the soldiers to the east wing, to a narrow room at the end of a narrow corridor that looked out on a kitchen garden and a wall. He set down his chests. He sat on the bed.

He heard it anyway.

Not the words. The walls were thick, the house was large. But an hour later, as the light was going, he heard the General’s footsteps come down the corridor — the brisk, even tread he had learned to know on the road — and pass his door.

They did not sound brisk.

They sounded like someone walking very carefully across ice.

A door closed further down. Then nothing.

---

It was Changli who told him, later, when she came to fetch him for supper. She stood in his doorway with her hands folded in her sleeves and her beautiful face perfectly composed, and only her voice was wrong.

“She didn’t know her,” Changli said.

Esdel looked up.

“No — that’s not true. She knew her. She said *Xueyao*, and held out her arms, and she was glad.” Changli’s mouth tightened. “She was *so* glad. She held her, and patted her hair, and said she was too thin, and asked if the war was very cold.” A pause. “And then the Count came in behind us. And she let go.”

Esdel waited.

“She let go of her daughter,” said Changli, “to turn and look at him. Like a flower turns to the light. And she said, *my lord, look, she’s home, isn’t it wonderful*, and she took his hand.” Her voice had gone very quiet. “She held his hand for the rest of the visit. She didn’t hold Xueyao’s again.”

Esdel said nothing.

“Lady Anlan,” said Changli, “argued with the Minister of Rites at her own wedding banquet over the correct order of the toasts. And won. She read the ledgers of forty ships every month and caught the errors before the clerks did. She *frightened* people.” She looked down at her folded hands. “Tonight she asked the Count three times whether she ought to have the red tea or the white. And each time he told her, she smiled at him like he had given her a jewel.”

“How did the General take it?”

Changli was silent.

“She said, *I’m glad you’re well, Mother*,” she said finally. “And then she said she was tired from the road. And she walked out.” She lifted her eyes to his. “She has not cried since she was fourteen, Apothecary. I was there. I would know.”

---

He was not called to supper after all. The General sent word that she would take it in her rooms, alone, and that her people were to eat in the kitchen hall with the soldiers. So Esdel ate rice and river fish among the fifteen veterans, who were quieter than he had ever heard them, and afterward he could not sleep.

He went out.

The east wing opened onto a long gallery, and the gallery onto the gardens, and at the heart of the gardens, silver in the rising moon, stood a peach tree in full flower.

He had never seen one so old. Its trunk was thick and gnarled and split in two places, bound with iron bands. Its branches spread wide over a paved circle and a small pond, and every one of them was heavy with blossom — so thick and pale that the tree seemed to glow, as though it had drawn in all the light of the day and was now, quietly, giving it back. Petals drifted down onto the black water of the pond and floated there like small boats.

Beneath it, on a stone bench, someone was sitting with a bundle in her arms.

He stopped.

It was the General. She had not heard him. She was sitting beneath the peach tree in a plain robe with her hair loose down her back, and in her arms, wrapped in pale blue, was a child.

A very small child. Not yet a year old, perhaps. Fat-cheeked and fast asleep, with one fist curled against his mouth.

Esdel should have gone. It was not his place. But he found he could not move, and so he stood in the shadow of the gallery and watched the General of the Northern Front — who had burned a Titan from the inside out, who fed a dragon on the souls of men — hold her brother for the first time in her life.

She held him as though he were made of something that might shatter. Her arms were rigid with care. She was not rocking him; she did not seem to know how. She was only looking at him — at his face, his fist, the soft dark down of his hair — with an expression Esdel had never seen on her, and did not have a name for.

A petal fell onto the child’s cheek. She lifted it away with one fingertip, so gently that he did not stir.

“Yueming,” she whispered. It was barely a sound. “I’m your sister.”

The child slept on.

“I’m sorry I didn’t come sooner,” said Lián Xueyao, to the brother who could not hear her, beneath the tree her father had loved. “I’m sorry. I’m sorry. I’m here now.”

And then her shoulders began, very slowly, to shake.

She made no sound. Not one. She sat beneath the peach tree with her brother in her arms and her face bent over him, and wept in perfect silence, the way soldiers learn to — so that no one in the camp will hear.

Esdel stepped back into the dark of the gallery.

He went back to his narrow room, and closed the door, and sat on the bed until the moon went down. He did not write anything that night. He only sat with his hand pressed flat against his chest, where the small silver flower lay against his heart, and thought of another house, and another girl, and another tree in another garden, and all the people who had once stood in a doorway and said *we’ll be back*.
