# **Chapter 67**

The road south from North Ridge ran through country that had forgotten the war.

On the first morning, it did not feel that way. The column rode out beneath a sky the color of old iron, past burned fields and the black ribs of farmhouses, past a ditch where crows lifted from something no one looked at twice. The fifteen soldiers of House Lián rode in two lines, silent, their armor wrapped in dark cloth against the dust. The General rode at their head. Her attendant rode beside her. The apothecary rode at the back, with the pack mule and the medicine chests, where the dust was thickest.

No one spoke to him. He did not expect them to.

By the second day, the ash thinned. By the third, there were orchards.

They came over a low rise at noon, and the land below simply changed — as though someone had drawn a line across the country and said *here, the war stops.* Plum trees stood in white rows on the slopes. A stream ran beside the road, clear as glass, and children were wading in it, and they stopped and stared at the riders with round, unafraid eyes. A woman beating rugs over a fence lifted her hand, and after a moment, one of the soldiers lifted his back.

Esdel watched the blossoms drift across the road, and something in his chest turned over, slow and painful, like a key in a stiff lock.

It was spring again.

He had lost count, at the front. There had been mud, and then snow, and then mud again, and he had stopped marking the seasons by anything but the kind of wounds that came into the tent — frostbite, then fever, then rot. But here was spring, unmistakable, blowing white petals into his horse's mane. Which meant that somewhere far to the south and west, beyond all these orchards, beyond the forest and the low hills, there was a village where the river would be running high with snowmelt.

Where a year ago, almost to the day, a caravan had carried him in out of the sand.

He looked down at his hands on the reins. He did not let himself think any further than that.

---

On the third evening, they made camp in a stand of poplars beside the stream, and he did what he always did. He unpacked the chests, and laid out his cloths and salves on a flat stone, and waited.

The soldiers of House Lián were veterans — hard, weathered men, most of them past forty, who had followed the General's father before they followed her. They did not complain. They had not complained on the first day, when the cold made their old wounds ache, nor on the second, when the long gallop across the burned plain rubbed their legs raw inside their greaves.

But on the third evening, the youngest of them — a boy of nineteen, with a sunburned nose and a gap in his teeth — came limping over to the flat stone and sat down with an air of great dignity.

“Apothecary,” he said. “I have a small problem.”

Esdel looked at the way he was sitting. “Your boots.”

“My boots,” the boy agreed gravely, “are trying to kill me.”

He had blisters the size of coins on both heels, burst and angry. Esdel cleaned them, and packed them with salve, and bound them with soft linen, and the boy watched with the frank curiosity of the young.

“You’re quick,” he said.

“I’ve done a great many feet.”

“Pei Lun,” said the boy, and stuck out his hand, and then remembered his hands were filthy and wiped them on his knees and stuck it out again. “Everyone calls me Pei. Except Captain Han. He calls me *that boy*.”

Esdel shook his hand. “Esdel.”

“I know.” Pei grinned, showing the gap. “Everyone knows. You’re the one who told Changli no.”

Esdel’s hands paused on the linen.

“The whole escort knows,” Pei went on cheerfully. “Suyin saw her go to your tent. And then she saw her come out again, *fast*. Captain Han laughed so hard he pulled something in his back.” He leaned closer, lowering his voice. “Is it true you said *my focus is solely on my commission*?”

“Hold still,” said Esdel.

“It *is* true.” Pei looked delighted. “Gods. You’re either a monk or a madman.”

“Neither.” Esdel tied off the bandage. “Keep these dry. Change them tomorrow.”

Pei limped away, and within the hour three more soldiers had drifted over to the flat stone with various complaints — a split knuckle, a cough, an old shoulder that ached in the damp. The last to come was the captain himself: Han Ruo, grey-bearded and broad as a door, who sat down heavily and held out a wrist swollen from an old break that had never set right.

“Lord Hengzhi’s stables,” Han Ruo said, when Esdel asked. “Thirty years ago. A horse the Lord loved and I didn’t.” He watched Esdel’s hands work. “He paid the physician himself. For a stableboy.” A pause. “You don’t forget that kind of man.”

Esdel said nothing. He knew by now that the veterans did not want answers when they spoke of the Lord. They only wanted someone to hear it.

When he was done, Han Ruo flexed the wrist, grunted, and looked at him for a long moment from under his heavy brows.

“You’ll do,” he said, and got up, and went back to the fire.

After that, someone saved Esdel a place there.

---

Xueyao watched it happen from the edge of the firelight.

She had not meant to watch. She had come out of her tent to check the horse lines, the way she always did, and had stopped beneath the poplars — and there he was, bent over the captain’s wrist in the orange light, and there were her soldiers, who had not warmed to anyone new in three years, making room for him on the log.

Changli came to stand beside her. For a while, neither of them spoke.

“He’s very good with them,” Changli said at last.

“He’s an apothecary. It’s his work.”

“Mm.” Changli tilted her head. “He doesn’t do it like work, though. Have you noticed? He does it like—” She searched for the word and gave up with a small shrug. “Like he’s paying something back.”

Xueyao did not answer.

She was thinking of the night the Titan fell. A man on a crate by the roadside, blood-smeared and hollow-eyed, and the flash of red she had seen in his gaze before it went brown and ordinary again. She had asked him who he was. He had not answered.

Seven months. He had stitched her soldiers’ wounds for seven months, not two hundred paces from her tent, and she had not asked again. She had told herself there was a war. That it did not matter.

It mattered now. She was taking him into her mother’s house.

---

Late that night, when the fire was embers and the soldiers were snoring in their bedrolls, she walked the camp one last time and found him still awake.

He was sitting apart, beneath a poplar, with a writing board across his knees and a stub of charcoal in his fingers. He was writing by the light of the dying fire — slowly, carefully, his head bent close to the page.

She stopped a few paces off. He did not hear her. He was very still, the way people are still when they are listening to something far away.

He wrote a line. Stopped. Looked at it a long time.

Then he folded the paper in half, and in half again, and slid it into the inside of his coat, where — she saw now — there were other papers, a thin sheaf of them, all folded the same way. None of them sealed. None of them addressed.

Something glinted at his throat as he bent: a fine silver chain, and on it a small charm the shape of a flower. He touched it once, absently, the way a man touches a wound that has closed but not stopped aching, and then tucked it back inside his collar.

She understood, then, without needing to be told. Letters that would never be sent. A charm that was not his.

Someone was waiting for this man.

She stepped back, quietly, before he could look up, and returned to her tent. She lay down in the dark in her clothes, as she had slept for three years, and looked at the canvas above her.

*No past,* Changli had said. *No family. No roots.*

Changli, she thought, was wrong about something. It was the first time in seventeen years she could remember that happening.

She was not sure why the thought unsettled her so much.
