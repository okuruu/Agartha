# **Chapter 92**

The decree was read in the Hall of Clear Waters on the third morning after the new moon, before the whole assembled court.

It was the same hall where, not three months before, three hundred guests had drunk to the victory at North Ridge beneath a haze of lanterns. The lanterns were gone now. The silk screens were gone. The black marble floor was bare, and cold, and reflected the grey light from the high windows like still water. The courtiers stood in their ranks along either side of it, in dark robes, with their hands folded in their sleeves, and did not speak.

At the far end, on the dais, the King sat on his throne.

He did not move. He sat as he always sat — very still, one hand resting on the carved arm, his face tired and grey and without expression. Beside him, one step down, stood the Crown Prince, in dark green, with his eyes on the floor. Beside the Prince stood the Prime Minister. And beside the Prime Minister, a little apart, in charcoal grey, his pale eyes on nothing at all, stood Grand Duke Rowan Hale.

And before the dais, alone in the middle of the bare black floor, stood Count Aldric Reng.

---

He had come without his cane.

That was the first thing the court noticed, and the thing they whispered about most afterward. He had come walking into the Hall of Clear Waters on his own two feet, straight-backed, in a plain black coat — not the white of mourning; he had put that away — and he had no cane at all. Instead, his right arm hung in a sling of black silk, and the hand at the end of it was wrapped in white linen from the fingers to the elbow.

And his face was bruised. A long dark bruise down one side, from the temple to the jaw, as though he had struck it on stone.

He did not speak of it. He did not need to. The court had heard the story already, the way the capital always heard stories — in the kitchens and the stables and the taverns by the bridge, faster than any herald.

*She fled in the night. The mad General. She drugged the whole household and took the boy — the baby, the Lián heir, his own ward — and fled.*

*The Count rode after her himself. With his own men. Through the storm.*

*At the ford of the Greenwater, her people turned on him. Her apothecary — the nameless one, the one from the college hearing — some kind of demon, they say, his eyes bled — he cut the Count’s hand to the bone and threw him into the river. His own men had to pull him out. Half-drowned.*

*And the boy. They say she has the boy still. No one knows where.*

*They say she’s gone mad with that dragon. They say it’s in her head, telling her things.*

*They say she means to use the child.*

---

The Prime Minister stepped forward with a scroll. He unrolled it. He read it aloud in a clear, flat, carrying voice, and every word of it fell into the silent hall like a stone into still water.

“*By the will of the Crown, and in the name of the safety of the realm—*

“*Let it be known that the woman Lián Xueyao, formerly General of the Northern Front, formerly styled Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon, has on the night of the new moon abandoned her command, deserted the service of the Crown, and taken by force and by stealth from the house of his lawful guardian the infant Lián Yueming, ward of the Crown—*

“*That in her flight she did assault the person of the said guardian, Count Aldric Reng, a peer of the realm, with intent to kill—*

“*That she did force the north gate of the capital in defiance of the Crown’s watch—*”

The Crown Prince’s eyes did not lift from the floor. But Esdel, had he been there, would have seen his hands. They were clasped behind his back, and the knuckles were white.

The King did not look at his son. He did not look at anyone.

“*—and that she has thereby shown herself a traitor to the Crown, as her father was before her.*

“*Let it be known that all titles, ranks, honors, and properties formerly held by the said Lián Xueyao are forfeit to the Crown. That her name is struck from the rolls of the army and the registers of the nobility. That any who give her shelter, food, or aid shall be held guilty of the same treason, and shall suffer the same penalty.*

“*And let it be known that the following persons, having knowingly aided the said traitor in her crimes, are likewise declared traitors to the Crown, stripped of all rank and protection, and condemned to death wherever they may be found—*”

He read the names.

There were fifteen of them. Every soldier of House Lián who had ridden south with her in the spring, the living and the dead alike — the four who had fallen in the gateway, the two in the Street of Lanterns, the two whose bodies had been pulled from the Greenwater and known by their armor, and the ones who had ridden on. Their names, their ranks, the villages they had come from.

Then the Prime Minister paused, very slightly, as a man pauses before a word he does not know how to pronounce.

“*—and the apothecary Esdel, of no family, formerly of the Third Logistic Company and the medical division of the North Ridge post, late of the village of Edenridge.*”

The name fell into the hall and lay there on the cold black floor.

Aldric Reng, standing alone before the dais with his arm in its black silk sling, did not move. But the corner of his mouth — the unbruised side — lifted. Very slightly. So slightly that no one saw it but the Grand Duke, who happened at that moment to be looking at him, and who looked away again at once, as a man looks away from something unclean.

---

“*Further,*” read the Prime Minister, “*let it be known that the soldiers of the Lián household who remain in the service of the Northern Front, having served under the said traitor, are stripped of rank and reassigned to the penal companies. That the family tombs of the Lián on the hill above the river are closed by the Crown’s seal. That the name of Lián shall not be spoken in any temple of the Kingdom, nor any offering made in it.*

“*And let it be known that a reward of five thousand gold crowns is offered for the capture of the traitor Lián Xueyao, living. And one thousand for any of her accomplices. Living or dead.*”

He rolled up the scroll.

“*So it is decreed. So let it be done.*”

The silence in the Hall of Clear Waters went on for a long time.

Then the King stirred.

He lifted his hand from the arm of the throne — only a little — and the Prime Minister stepped forward and bowed and laid the scroll across the King’s knees, with a brush already wet with ink beside it on a small lacquered tray. And the King took up the brush, and signed his name at the foot of the scroll in a single long unhurried stroke, and set the brush down.

His hand did not tremble at all.

---

Afterward, when the court had gone — when the courtiers had filed out through the great doors in their dark robes, murmuring, and the Count had gone out among them, accepting their sympathy, their outrage, their soft hands on his good arm — the King sat alone on the dais in the empty hall.

Not quite alone. The Crown Prince had not left.

He stood where he had stood all morning, one step down from the throne, with his hands clasped behind his back. He had not moved. His father had not dismissed him.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The grey light lay on the black floor.

“The guards at the north gate,” said the King at last, very quietly, “say that the gate was forced.”

Ardbert did not answer.

“That is what they say,” said the King. “That is what they told the captain of the watch, and what the captain of the watch told the Prime Minister, and what the Prime Minister wrote in the decree. The gate was forced, by the traitor and her people, in the storm.” He looked down at his own hands, folded on the scroll in his lap. “It will be what history says. There will be no other account.”

Ardbert stood very still.

“I made certain of it,” said the King.

The Prince lifted his head. For the first time that morning, he looked at his father.

The King did not look back. He was looking at the long bare hall, the black floor, the high grey windows.

“You are my only son,” he said. “And you are going to be King. And a King does not open gates in the night for traitors, whatever he remembers of how they looked at him when he was seventeen.” A pause. “I have made it so that you did not. You were never there. Khalid was never there. It never happened.”

“Father—”

“It never happened,” said the King again. Quietly. Without anger. Without anything at all. “And you will never do it again.”

The Prince looked at him for a long moment.

Then he bowed — the full court bow, deep, with sweeping sleeves — and turned, and walked the length of the black hall alone, and went out through the great doors. They closed behind him with a sound that went on echoing for a long time in the empty room.

The King sat on his throne with the signed decree across his knees.

He did not move for a long time. Then, slowly, he took from his sleeve a small folded paper. It was very old, and soft at the creases. He unfolded it on top of the decree.

It was a map. A small one — a corner torn from a larger sheet, long ago. It showed the far north of the Kingdom, beyond the ridges. And in the corner, in faded red ink, a coiled golden shape, with old characters beside it.

*Heaven’s Beast. Bound to the blood of Lián.*

The King looked at it for a long time. Then he took up the brush again, and dipped it in the ink, and very carefully, beside the old characters, he wrote two new ones.

*For now.*
