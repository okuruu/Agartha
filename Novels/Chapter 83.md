# **Chapter 83**

He asked for her on the twentieth day after the funeral.

He did it properly. He did everything properly. He sent a letter first, in his beautiful hand, requesting the honor of the General’s company in the east pavilion at the hour of her choosing, on a matter concerning the future of her house. The letter was sealed in white wax — the mourning wax; he still wore white — with the silver serpent pressed into it.

Xueyao read it standing by the window in her sitting room, with the orchids on the sill behind her. She read it twice. Then she held it out to Changli without a word.

Changli read it. Her face did not change, but her hand holding the paper went very still.

“Don’t go,” she said.

“I’ll go.” Xueyao took the letter back and looked at it. “I want to hear him say it.”

“Say what?”

Xueyao did not answer. But Esdel, standing by the door with the tray of bark tea he still brought her every morning out of a habit neither of them had broken, saw her face. And understood, with a slow cold drop in his stomach, that she already knew.

---

The east pavilion stood at the far end of the garden, beyond the peach tree, on a little rise above the river wall. It was older than the house — a single room of dark wood open on three sides, with a floor of worn stone and a view down over the water. Lord Hengzhi had used it, the steward said, for reading. The Count had hung it with white silk.

He was waiting there when she came. Standing at the rail, looking at the river, in his white mourning coat with his cane in his hand. He turned when he heard her step, and smiled, and bowed.

“General. Thank you for coming.”

Xueyao stopped at the top of the steps. She did not come in. She did not sit. Esdel, who had been told to wait at the foot of the rise with Han Ruo, could see her from where he stood — straight-backed, in her plain dark coat, her white mourning ribbon tied at her wrist.

“Say what you want to say, Count Reng.”

He did not seem troubled by her tone. He never did.

“I’ll be plain, then,” he said. “You prefer it. I’ve noticed.” He leaned on his cane, looking at her with his clear grey eyes. “You are in a very difficult position, Xueyao. I think you know that. You accused a peer of the realm before forty witnesses, and the college found no cause. You have been seen — forgive me — to be unwell. Your mother, may she rest, begged you publicly to stop. And now your mother is gone, and your brother is my ward by the King’s own decree, and you—” He spread one hand. “You have three months’ leave, a handful of your father’s old soldiers, and a name that half the court still thinks of as a traitor’s.”

Xueyao said nothing.

“When your leave ends,” said the Count gently, “you will go back to the front. And the court will go on whispering. The Maiden who lost her mind. The traitor’s daughter. The madwoman of North Ridge.” He shook his head. “It will follow you. It will follow you for the rest of your life, unless someone puts a stop to it.”

“And you’d put a stop to it.”

“I would give you a name,” said Aldric Reng, “that no one would dare whisper about.”

The river went by below the wall. Somewhere in the garden, a bird was singing.

“Marry me,” he said.

---

Esdel did not hear it. He was too far. But he saw the General’s face, from the foot of the rise — saw it not change at all, not by a hair — and knew.

Beside him, Han Ruo said something under his breath that was not a word in any language Esdel knew.

---

“You were married to my mother,” said Xueyao.

Her voice was perfectly even.

“I was.” The Count bowed his head. “And I grieve her. You must believe that. But she is gone, and you and I are still here, and the house she loved is still standing — just — and there is a child in the nursery who needs a father and a mother both.” He looked up. “I have spoken to the King. He has no objection. There are old precedents for it, in the great houses, when a line is in danger. It would be — unusual. But it would be seen as a kindness. A healing.” A faint smile. “A man redeemed by devotion, raising up the broken house of the woman he loved. People would weep at the wedding. You know they would.”

“The King,” Xueyao said, “has no objection.”

“None.”

“Of course he hasn’t.”

The Count tilted his head, as if puzzled by her tone.

“Say the rest,” said Xueyao.

“The rest?”

“You have my brother. You have the house. You have the gold, what’s left of it. You don’t need me for any of that.” She took one step forward, into the pavilion, onto the worn stone, and the white silk stirred around her in the river wind. “So say the rest. Say what you actually want. I want to hear you say it.”

The Count regarded her for a long moment.

And then, very slowly, his smile changed.

It did not vanish. It did not harden. It simply — shifted. Deepened. Became, for the first time since she had known him, entirely honest. And that honesty was the most frightening thing Esdel had ever seen, even from the foot of the rise, even without hearing a word.

“You know what I want,” said Aldric Reng softly. “You’ve always known. You’ve known since the night you came through the gate and looked up at my banner.” He took a step toward her. “You are the Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon, Xueyao. The blood of Heaven runs in you. Your children would carry it. My children would carry it.” His grey eyes were very bright. “Half-divine. A line that commands the beast that won the war. Do you know what that is? It is not a house. It is a *throne*. Waiting for someone with the patience to build the stairs.”

The river wind moved the white silk.

“And if I have no children,” said Xueyao, “there is always my brother.”

The Count smiled.

“There is always your brother,” he agreed.

---

She did not strike him. Afterward, that was what Han Ruo could not stop saying — in the corridor, in the kitchens, to anyone who would listen, his voice hoarse and shaking with something like awe. *She didn’t strike him. She didn’t draw. She didn’t even raise her voice. I’d have killed him. Thirty years I served her father, and I would have killed him where he stood, and she just—*

She just looked at him.

She stood in the east pavilion where her father had read in the evenings, with the white silk moving around her, and looked at Count Aldric Reng for a long, long moment. Esdel, at the foot of the rise, could see her face. It had gone perfectly calm. Calmer than he had ever seen it — calmer than beneath the peach tree, calmer than at the funeral, calmer than the night of the Titan when she had stepped down from the dragon’s harness with the ash still falling. It was the calm of deep water, at night, when there is no wind at all.

“No,” she said.

Just that.

The Count inclined his head, as though she had declined a second cup of tea.

“I thought you might say that,” he said. “I don’t ask you to answer today. Think on it. There is no hurry.” He smiled. “I’m a patient man.”

“I know,” said Xueyao. “I’ve seen your patience. I buried it on the hill above the river.”

She turned and walked down the steps.

---

She walked past Esdel and Han Ruo at the foot of the rise without stopping. She walked through the garden, past the peach tree, past the empty pavilion by the pond where her mother had drunk her tea every day for a year. She walked along the gallery and into the east wing and down the corridor to her rooms. Her steps were even. Her back was straight. She did not hurry.

She went into her sitting room and closed the door behind her.

Changli, who had been waiting inside, came out a moment later. She shut the door behind her very quietly, and stood with her back against it, and looked at Esdel coming down the corridor.

“She wants to be alone,” Changli said.

He stopped.

Changli’s face was white. Her hands were folded in her sleeves the way they always were. But he could see, now, that they were not folded. They were holding each other, hard, the knuckles pale.

“She told me,” said Changli. “What he wants.” Her voice was very low. “She said it the way she reads casualty lists. *He wants to marry me. He wants my children for the dragon. And if I won’t give him children, he has Yueming.*” She drew a breath. “And then she said: *Go out, Changli. I need to be alone for a while.* And she sat down by the window with the orchids. And I came out.”

The corridor was quiet. Somewhere, far off, a flute was still playing the old laments, though the funeral had been three weeks ago. The Count had kept the musicians on.

“I have known her since we were six,” said Changli. “I have seen her angry, and I have seen her grieving, and I have seen her afraid. Once. When she was fourteen, and the dragon spoke to her the first time.” She looked at the closed door. “I have never seen her like this. I don’t know what this is.”

Esdel looked at the door too.

He thought of deep water at night, with no wind.

He had seen that calm once before, he realized. Not on her. On the soldiers in the field tents at the Eastern Post, in the last hour — the ones who had understood, finally, that nothing more could be done, and had stopped fighting it. Who lay very still, and very quiet, and looked at the tent roof with clear, peaceful eyes.

Not despair. Something past despair. The quiet of a thing that has broken so completely there is nothing left in it to break.

“Leave her,” he said. “For now.”

Changli looked at him.

“For now,” he said again. “Not for long.”
