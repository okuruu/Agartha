# **Chapter 65**

Lián Xueyao sat beneath the dim canvas of her tent, a structure meant for temporary deployment yet one that had felt like her permanent, suffocating cage for years. The **oil lamp** on the campaign chest flickered violently, less like a heart beating and more like a dying star fighting against the endless draft that snuck through the crude seams of the tent flaps. The scent of stale oil, leather, and woodsmoke clung to the fabric, a harsh, constant reminder of the life she was forced to lead.

Three years. Three agonizingly long years had passed since she last saw her home—the palatial, sun-drenched grand corridors of the **Lián estate**, where light always seemed to fall just right on the polished wood; the ancient **peach tree** outside her mother’s chamber that bloomed violently pink every spring; the deep, resonant calm of her **father’s voice** echoing across the central courtyard during evening lessons. Now, those cherished memories were brittle, beautiful ghosts, buried deep beneath the relentless mud of war and the cruel certainty of betrayal.

Her father, Lord Hengzhi—her one and only father—had been a pillar of the Kingdom. Yet he had died under the weight of a meticulously woven lie, a traitor to the very crown he spent his life defending, his honor sacrificed to **Count Aldric Reng’s** carefully laid plan.

Xueyao ran a thumb over the crease in the dark, heavy message—a message that had arrived like a dagger, delivered by one of Lord Hengzhi's most loyal, and now most desperately dead, vassals. It confirmed the unthinkable: the charge of rebellion was a sophisticated fraud. Her father, the man who had instilled in her the core value of unwavering loyalty, was now officially deemed a betrayer, allied with the rebel factions. He was denied a proper burial, his bones were fed to the dogs.

The same Aldric Reng, the architect of this ruin, now occupied the most sacred seats of her life. He sat in her father’s place at the high table, he wore her father’s name, not by right, but by virtue of her mother’s new vows, and he drank her family’s prized, decades-aged plum wine as though it were his birthright. This same Aldric, she thought with a tightening in her chest that felt like physical pain, smiled the serene, reassuring smile of a savior before the court, his hands outwardly clean, while he inwardly celebrated the destruction of the Lián clan.

When the news finally arrived—carried not by official messenger but by quiet, horrified rumor—that **Lady Wei Anlan** had married the very man who orchestrated her husband’s demise, Xueyao’s heart didn’t just break. It clenched with such a cold, terrible force it almost tore her in half. The pain was so sharp it eclipsed the grief she felt for her father. This was desecration. This was the final blow to her family's dignity.

She had begged her commander for leave, once, twice, a third time. First, to attend the sham of her father’s funeral, then again, just to see her mother, to look into her eyes and discover the terrifying truth for herself. Each time, the response was the same icy refusal, masked in the necessity of duty: *The war needs you.*

And behind those cold words lay the true conspiracy: **Grand Duke Rowan Hale** was pulling the strings. His influence stretched across the map, a complex, sticky web ensnaring every aspect of her life, from the lowliest courier to the highest command. She, the "Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon," the fiery symbol of morale for the entire Imperial Army, was merely a piece on the board—a distraction, an asset to be controlled, and most importantly, a dangerous disturbance to the plans of Count Aldric Reng and Grand Duke Rowan Hale. Outwardly, she was the epitome of Imperial loyalty. Inside, she was just a daughter, grieving and trapped, torn between her solemn oath to the empire and the raw, desperate ache to return home and confront the ghosts that wore the faces of her loved ones.

But the night the late Lord Hengzhi’s most trusted vassal, in his final, guilt-ridden message, revealed the devastating truth about the **"tonic,"** something in Xueyao finally shattered—and from that rubble, something dark and unyielding was born.

The draught Aldric brought her mother wasn’t the restorative medicine it was claimed to be. It was a chain. A subtle, invisible, and insidious poison designed not to kill, but to **subdue**. It worked by softening her mother's brilliant mind, dampening her proud spirit, and binding her emotions, making her pliant, suggestible, and utterly unable to resist the smooth, destructive charm of the man who had methodically dismantled her life.

Xueyao’s hands had trembled, not with weakness, but with a terrifying, contained fury, as she read the confession. Her mother—proud, gentle, and one of the most brilliant political minds of her generation—was reduced to a puppet, manipulated into a false marriage through chemical deceit.

The apothecaries the vassal had secretly consulted confirmed the presence of an unknown substance, a thing alien to any known healer, described in the message as being both chemical and unnatural—a **"whisper of control"** in every seemingly benign drop.

“It’s too late,” the vassal's shaky script concluded, heavy with failure. “She won’t listen to reason, not from anyone. The bonds are too deep.” But Lián Xueyao, the Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon, refused to accept that finality. If her mother was trapped behind an enchanted wall of false affection, then she—her eldest child, her true blood—would be the one to break it down and free her, no matter the unforgivable cost to her career, her honor, or her very life.

Her golden eyes, usually calm and reflective as frost under the dawn sun, now burned with a light that was colder, harder, and infinitely deadlier than any winter. She began to plan, the strategy taking shape in the depths of the night like a dark constellation. She could not go herself—that would be an immediate death sentence and ruin all chances of discovering proof. She would send someone in her place—someone unnoticed, sharp-witted, and utterly expendable. Someone who could blend into the background of the Lián estate, observe the poisonous reality, and, crucially, survive.

She turned to **Changli**, her most trusted lady’s maid, who was more than an attendant—she was a childhood friend, a shadow who had followed her loyally since girlhood, even into the grime and danger of the military camps.

“Is there anyone we can trust for this?” Xueyao asked quietly, the question stripped of her usual commanding authority, revealing the vulnerability beneath.

Changli, whose loyalty was a known quantity but whose caution was well-earned, hesitated. A long, charged silence settled between them, broken only by the hungry lick of the oil lamp flame. Then, with a subtle but firm nod, she spoke. “There’s one,” she said, her voice barely a murmur. “**Esdel**. The apothecary who transferred to our post seven months ago.”

Changli's tone carried a curious blend of assessment and slight, weary amusement. “He’s strange. He cracks unexpected jokes when everyone is grim, and he keeps rigidly to himself when the rest of the company is drinking away the horrors. The soldiers like him well enough, but he never lets anyone truly get close. He’s clever, my Lady… maybe too clever. But most importantly—he has no past. No family. No visible roots to anchor him here, or anywhere else. If things go wrong, if Aldric’s network catches him, we can claim no knowledge of him. We can, politely, **‘take care’** of him.”

Xueyao listened, her gaze distant. *Esdel.* She had not forgotten him — a pair of tired brown eyes by the roadside the night the Titan fell, and the flicker of red she had seen in them. Seven months, and she had never gotten her answer. When Changli finished, a faint, chilling smile touched the corner of the Lady’s mouth—a smile devoid of warmth, fueled only by the steel of her new resolve.

“Perfect,” she murmured, the single word sealing Esdel’s fate. Her fingers brushed the hastily drawn map of the Kingdom on her campaign table, tracing the long, impossible path back to her lost home. “Then he’ll go.”

Outside the tent, the relentless wind of the north howled through the camp, carrying the stark scent of iron, ash, and distant fear. The muffled, heavy rhythm of war drums echoed faintly, a constant reminder of the external conflict. But for the first time in three years, Lián Xueyao’s thoughts were no longer consumed by the battles. They were fixed entirely on her mother—and on the man who had taken everything from the Lián family.

And as the small lamp flame trembled, reflected in her golden eyes like two twin shards of cold, deadly light, Lián Xueyao swore quietly to the night wind—that even if she had to burn the empire she served to the ground, she would walk through the ashes to save her mother and reclaim her family’s name.
