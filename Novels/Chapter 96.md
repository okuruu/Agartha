# **Chapter 96**

They went north and east, into the borderlands, where the Kingdom frayed out into nothing.

It was country none of them knew. Not the General, who had spent three years at the front but always behind the lines, in the camps and the eyries and the command tents. Not the old soldiers, who had been born in the river towns and would die there if they could. Not Esdel, who did not know anywhere at all. It was a high, bare, empty country of brown hills and grey rock and wind — always wind, blowing out of the east across the long slopes, bending the thin grass flat, whistling in the stones. There were no roads. There were goat tracks, and dry streambeds, and sometimes, on the ridges, the tumbled ruins of old watchtowers from some war no one remembered.

There were no villages. That was why they had come.

---

They traveled at night, mostly, and slept in the day, in the lee of rocks or in the ruins of the old towers. They ate what they could find. Pei Lun turned out to be very good at snaring rabbits, and Gao knew which of the grey-green plants on the hillsides could be eaten and which would kill a man. Esdel knew that too. He did not know how. He knelt beside a low thorny bush on the first morning and knew, the way he knew the angle of a slope, that its berries would stop a heart in an hour, and that its roots, boiled twice, would ease a fever.

He did not tell anyone how he knew. They did not ask.

The General did not speak much. She rode at the head of the little column, in the dark, with her face turned always north and east, and in the day she slept apart, wrapped in her cloak, with her sword beside her. She ate what she was given. She checked the horses. She took her turn at the watch, and would not let anyone take it for her.

She did not mention her brother. Or her mother. Or the old man in the gateway beneath the carved arch.

She did not mention the dragon, either. But sometimes, on the high ridges, in the dark, Esdel saw her stop her horse and turn in the saddle and look back — not south, toward the capital, but north and west, toward something none of them could see. And she would sit there, very still, for a long time, with her face turned toward it.

He did not ask what she was listening to. He thought he knew.

---

On the twelfth night, they came up onto a long high ridge above a valley none of them had names for, and stopped there in the ruins of a watchtower to wait for the dawn.

It was the highest place they had been. From the ruined wall, you could see a very long way — back the way they had come, over rolling brown hills silver-grey in the starlight, fading into the dark. And ahead, north and east, more hills, and more beyond them, and at the very edge of sight, where the stars met the land, a thin pale line that might have been mountains, or might have been cloud.

The others slept. Pei Lun curled against the wall with his too-big bow in his arms, like a child with a doll. Gao sat against a fallen stone with his chin on his chest. The four old soldiers lay in a row in the lee of the wall, snoring softly.

Esdel could not sleep. He sat on a stone at the edge of the ruin, where the wall had fallen away and the ridge dropped off into the dark, and watched the east go slowly grey.

He heard her before he saw her. The soft scrape of a boot on stone. She did not come to him. She went past him, along the crest of the ridge, to where it rose to a small rocky point above the drop, and stood there, with her back to him, looking east.

He watched her for a while without meaning to.

Then he saw what she was holding.

It was a knife. Her own belt knife, plain and worn, the kind every soldier carried. She was holding it in her right hand. With her left, she reached up behind her head and gathered her hair — all of it, the long black hair she had worn in a soldier’s knot for three years, that had hung loose down her back at her mother’s funeral in the old way of mourning daughters — and drew it forward over her shoulder, and held it there in her fist.

Esdel did not move.

She cut it. One long stroke, close to the nape. And then she stood there, on the rocky point above the drop, in the grey before dawn, holding a long black rope of hair in her left hand, with the wind blowing the short ragged ends of what was left around her face.

She looked at it for a long moment.

Then she opened her hand, and let the wind take it.

It went out over the drop, and unraveled as it went, and scattered — a long dark ribbon coming apart into a hundred threads, drifting out over the valley in the grey light, falling slowly toward the invisible ground far below, until he could not see it anymore.

---

The sun was coming.

It came up over the far pale line at the edge of the world — mountains, after all — and the first light of it came across the empty hills in a long low wave of gold, and reached the ridge, and touched her.

She had taken off her dark cloak. Beneath it she was wearing white. A plain white robe, rough and unhemmed — the mourning robe she had worn at the funeral, that she had carried rolled at the bottom of her saddlebag all the way from the capital without anyone knowing. She had put it on in the dark. She was standing now on the rocky point above the drop, in white, with her short hair blowing, with her back to him, facing east, into the dawn.

And the light came up behind the far mountains and poured across the valley and struck her, and she was a figure of white standing at the edge of the horizon, unmoving, serene, with the wind bending around her.

Esdel stopped breathing.

He knew her. He had seen her before. Not here — not like this — but *her*, this, the white figure at the edge of the world with the light behind her and her back turned. He had seen her in the desert, beneath the lightning, in the ruined temple. He had seen her at the edge of the red horizon, in the storm, with her hair drifting in the wind, before the sand rose up to meet him. He had seen her in dreams, walking against the storm. *A woman in white. She’s always standing far away, looking toward the horizon. I can’t see her face.*

*You were never meant to forget.*

His heart — his even, precise, borrowed heart, that had not stumbled in a year — stumbled.

He was on his feet. He did not know when he had stood. He took a step toward the rocky point, and another, over the broken stones, and the wind was loud in his ears and the light was in his eyes and the white figure stood there at the edge of the world with her back to him, and he opened his mouth to say—

He did not know what. A name. A name he did not have.

She turned around.

---

It was Xueyao.

Only Xueyao. Her face pale and tired in the dawn light, her golden eyes red-rimmed, her short hair ragged around her face where she had hacked it off with a belt knife. A tired young woman in a rough white robe on a ridge in the borderlands, who had just thrown her hair to the wind, and turned, and found the apothecary standing on the stones behind her with a look on his face she had never seen.

The white figure from the desert was gone. It had never been there. There was only the wind, and the dawn, and the ridge.

Esdel stood very still.

His heart settled back, slowly, into its even rhythm. The light was in his eyes. He could not tell, for a moment, whether he was dizzy or whether the world was.

“Esdel?” said Xueyao.

She had never called him by his name before. Always *Apothecary*. He noticed that, even now. Especially now.

“I’m sorry,” he said. His voice was strange in his own ears. “I thought— for a moment, I thought you were someone else.”

She looked at him for a long time.

She had seen it, he thought. Whatever had been in his face. She had seen it the way she had seen the red flash in his eyes on the night of the Titan, by the roadside, before it went brown and ordinary again.

“The woman in white,” she said slowly.

He had never told her that. He had never told anyone but Lyora, in a garden, in the moonlight, a year ago.

“I talked,” he said. “In the fever. In the hut.”

“You talked.” She looked down at herself — at the rough white robe, the bare feet on the cold stone. Then back up at him. “You said she was always standing far away. Looking at the horizon. You said you could never see her face.” She paused. “You said you didn’t know if she was waiting for you, or if you were supposed to find her, or if she was only something the desert put in your head so you wouldn’t die alone.”

The wind blew across the ridge. Behind them, in the ruined tower, Pei Lun muttered in his sleep and turned over.

“It isn’t you,” said Esdel. It came out quietly. He was not sure if it was a question.

Xueyao was silent for a long time.

“No,” she said. “I don’t think it is.”

She came down off the rocky point. She walked back along the crest of the ridge, over the broken stones, in her white robe with the dawn behind her, until she was standing in front of him. Not close. The width of a person between them, as it always was.

She looked up at him with her red-rimmed golden eyes.

“Who are you?” she said.

It was the third time. On the night of the Titan, by the roadside. In the ruined inn, in the rain. And now, here, on a ridge at the edge of the world, with nothing behind either of them but a decree nailed to every magistrate’s door in the Kingdom, and nothing ahead but hills.

He thought about it for a long time.

He thought about the desert. The cave, and the pool, and the creature, and the arm that had been gone and was not. The mural of the man who stood with the beast. The young man in silver armor on the cliff above the sea, who had said *fight* and pushed him. The soldier in the tent at Edenridge who had said *you lifted the fallen banner, everyone thought you died that day*. The gold thread in the Count’s heart. The dragon that hated the Kālākṣa. The eye of mercy.

The woman in white, who was not Xueyao, and was not anyone he knew, and was waiting somewhere at the edge of a horizon he could not reach.

He did not know who he was. He had never known. Perhaps he never would.

But he knew some things. He knew the weight of a water pail on a dusty lane, and the angle of a slope, and the knot that pulls tighter the harder the water presses. He knew the smell of rosemary, and burnt tea, and a lamp on a stair. He knew the sound a village makes when it laughs. He knew a promise he had never broken.

He knew his name. The one word that had come up out of the blank white sand, a year ago, to a stranger with green eyes.

“Esdel,” he said.

Xueyao waited.

“That’s all I have,” he said. “It’s the only thing I’ve ever been sure of.” He looked past her, east, at the far pale mountains and the light pouring over them. “For now, I think it’s enough.”

She looked at him a moment longer.

Then something happened to her face that he had never seen before, in all the months he had known her. Not a smile. Not quite. Something smaller and quieter and much rarer than a smile, that began in her eyes and went no further.

“Esdel,” she said. As though testing the sound of it. As though putting it somewhere safe.

Then she turned, and stood beside him on the ridge, in her white robe, with her short hair blowing — not touching, the width of a person between them — and together they watched the sun come up over the borderlands.

Behind them, one by one, the others began to wake.
