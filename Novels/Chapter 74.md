# **Chapter 74**

She did it in the garden, in daylight, in front of everyone.

Afterward, Esdel would wonder whether she had planned it that way or whether it had simply broken out of her. He thought, on balance, that she had planned it. She was a general. She did not charge without choosing her ground. But he also thought that when the moment came, the planning had not mattered at all — that something older and hotter than strategy had carried her forward, and she had let it.

---

It was the Count’s spring reception. He held one every year, the steward said — had done so even in his own house, before. Now he held it in the Lián gardens, beneath the peach tree, because the peach tree was famous and the blossoms were at their height, and forty guests of rank came to walk the paths and drink plum wine and admire the flowers that Lord Hengzhi had tended for thirty years.

Esdel was there because the General required it. He stood at the edge of the paved circle beneath the tree, with the other attendants, and watched the guests move among the blossoms in their silks, laughing softly. Ministers. Their wives. Two judges of the royal court. A physician of the royal college in his black cap. A handful of lesser nobles who had once, the steward whispered to another servant, dined at Lord Hengzhi’s table every week, and now dined at the Count’s.

Lady Anlan sat in the pavilion by the pond, in pale green, receiving the guests one by one. She smiled at all of them. She did not seem to know several of their names. When she faltered, the Count, standing at her shoulder, would murmur the name into her ear, and she would smile brighter and say it, and the guest would pretend not to notice.

And the General stood beneath the peach tree in her ivory and gold, with her hands clasped behind her back, and waited.

Esdel saw her waiting. He saw her watching the light. It was late afternoon, and the sun was sliding down toward the western wall, and the long amber hour was coming.

When the maid came with the tea things for Lady Anlan — the white porcelain, the pale tea, the single cup — Xueyao moved.

---

She walked across the paved circle to the pavilion. Her steps were not fast. They rang on the stones, even and measured, and the guests nearest her stopped talking, one by one, and turned to watch, the way people turn to watch a storm coming across a field.

The Count had already taken the small dark bottle from his coat. He was holding it in his fingers, unstoppered, over the cup.

Xueyao stopped at the edge of the pavilion.

“Put that down,” she said.

The garden went quiet.

The Count looked up at her. For a single breath — so brief that Esdel, watching with every sense he had, almost missed it — something moved behind his grey eyes. Not fear. Calculation. A man seeing a card turned over, and counting.

Then he smiled.

“General,” he said, pleasantly. “Your mother’s tonic. You know she has it every afternoon.”

“I know what it is.” Xueyao’s voice was not loud. It did not need to be. In the silence beneath the peach tree, it carried to every corner of the garden. “I know it is greyveil root. I know that given every day, for months, it softens the will and weakens the heart. I know you have been giving it to her since the week my father died.” She did not blink. “You are poisoning my mother, Count Reng. And you will put that bottle down.”

Someone gasped. Someone else dropped a cup; it rang on the stones.

The Count did not put the bottle down.

He looked at Xueyao for a long moment, with an expression Esdel had not seen on his face before — a slow, dawning, wounded disbelief. His hand, holding the bottle, began very slightly to tremble. When he spoke, his voice had changed. It was rougher. Hurt.

“Poison,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You think—” He laughed — a short, breathless, broken sound, as though the word had knocked the air out of him. He turned to the guests. His voice rose. “She thinks I am *poisoning* her. My wife. Her *mother*.” He turned back to Xueyao, and now there were tears standing in his eyes — real tears, bright in the amber light. “General. *Xueyao.* I have sat beside her bed every night for a year. When she could not eat, I fed her. When she woke screaming for your father, I held her until she slept. Master Vale brews this for her nerves, with the royal college’s own receipt—”

“Then let the royal college test it,” said Xueyao. “Here. Now. Give the bottle to that man.” She pointed at the physician in the black cap, who went pale.

And the Count’s voice broke.

“*Enough.*”

It was not a shout. It was worse than a shout. It was the voice of a good man pushed past what he could bear, and it rang off the garden walls, and forty people flinched.

“I have borne a great deal,” said Count Aldric Reng, very quietly now, into the silence. “I have borne the whispers. I have borne your coldness since the hour you came home — your refusal to bow, to take my hand, to eat at my table. I understood it. You had lost your father. You came home to find your mother remarried to a man you did not choose. I told myself: she is grieving. Give her time.” His voice shook. “But I will not bear *this*. Not in front of her.”

And he turned — and knelt, painfully, leaning on his cane — at Lady Anlan’s side.

Lady Anlan was staring at her daughter.

Esdel would remember her face for the rest of his life. It was not angry. That would have been easier. It was frightened — bewildered and frightened, like a child who has woken in the night in a strange room — and her hands, which had gone to the Count’s shoulders the moment he knelt, were clutching at the dove-grey silk of his coat as though he were the only solid thing in the world.

“Xueyao,” she said. Her voice was small and high and trembling. “Xueyao, how could you?”

The General did not move.

“He has been so good to me,” said Lady Anlan. Tears were running down her face now. “When your father— when everything— there was no one. No one came. And he came. Every day. How can you say such a thing? How can you *say* it?” She pressed her face into the Count’s shoulder, sobbing. “Make her stop. Aldric, please. Make her stop.”

The Count put his arms around his wife. He held her, gently, rocking her, murmuring into her hair — and over her shoulder, for one instant, his eyes met Xueyao’s.

They were perfectly dry.

---

It did not take long, after that.

The guests found reasons to leave. They found them quickly, quietly, with lowered eyes and murmured farewells, and none of them looked at the General as they went. The physician in the black cap was the first gone. The two judges were not far behind. By the time the sun touched the western wall, the garden was empty but for the household, the petals drifting down onto abandoned cups of plum wine, and the Count, in the pavilion, pouring the amber drops into his wife’s tea with a steady hand, while she clung to his sleeve and wept.

Xueyao stood beneath the peach tree and watched him do it.

She did not try to stop him again. She did not say another word. She only stood there, in her ivory and gold, with her hands at her sides, until her mother had drunk the cup to the bottom and gone quiet and soft in the Count’s arms — and then she turned and walked back across the circle, past Esdel, past Changli, past Han Ruo and his soldiers standing rigid at the gallery’s edge, and into the house.

No one followed her. No one dared.

---

By nightfall, it was all over the capital.

Esdel heard it himself, the next morning, when he went down to the river market for supplies. He heard it at the herb stalls and the fish stalls and in the tea house by the bridge, from people who had not been there and could not have known and told it anyway, with relish, as if they had.

*The Maiden accused him in front of everyone. In his own garden. Said he was poisoning the Lady.*

*The poor man wept. They say he wept.*

*Well, and she’s been three years at the front. With that — creature. Three years of blood. It does things to the mind.*

*And her father a traitor, don’t forget. Bad blood.*

*They say her own mother begged her to stop.*

He paid for his herbs. He walked back along the river with the basket on his arm and the voices following him like gulls. On the bridge, a group of young clerks were laughing, and as he passed he heard one of them say it — lightly, cheerfully, as if it were already an old joke, as if everyone had always known —

“*The general has lost her mind.*”
