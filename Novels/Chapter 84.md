# **Chapter 84**

She was not in her rooms at midnight.

Changli came to Esdel’s door, white-faced, with a lamp. The sitting room was empty. The orchids stood on the sill. The window was open onto the garden, and the white silk curtain was blowing in the warm night wind, in and out, like breath.

“She didn’t pass Han Ruo,” Changli said. “He’s been in the corridor all night. She went out the *window*.”

He was already pulling on his coat.

---

He found her beneath the peach tree.

He did not know how he knew she would be there. He simply went, the way his feet had gone up the ridge path in the storm, a year ago and a world away — down the gallery, across the dark garden, over the wet grass silver with moonlight — and there she was.

She was sitting on the stone bench where she had held her brother for the first time. The tree was almost bare now. Its blossoms had all fallen in the weeks of the funeral, and what remained on the branches was the small hard green of new fruit, and dark young leaves, rustling. The petals lay drifted in the corners of the paved circle like old snow, brown at the edges. The pond was black and still.

She was not weeping. She was sitting very straight, with her hands in her lap, looking up.

Not at the tree. Past it. North.

He stopped at the edge of the paved circle. She did not turn her head.

“I can feel it,” she said.

Her voice was quiet and conversational, the way she had spoken to him by the fire in the ruined inn.

“It’s awake. It’s been awake since the morning she died.” She was still looking north, over the garden wall, over the roofs of the city, toward something he could not see. “It doesn’t sleep, when I’m like this. It paces. Round and round the eyrie at North Ridge. The soldiers there will be frightened; they won’t know why.” A pause. “It’s asking me what I want.”

Esdel did not move.

“It’s five days’ ride from here,” said Xueyao. “For a horse. For the dragon, it’s a night. Less. If I called it now, it would be over the walls before dawn.” Her voice did not change. “It would do whatever I asked. That’s the pact. I command. It obeys.”

“And it feeds,” said Esdel.

Now she turned her head.

In the moonlight her face was very pale, and her golden eyes were very dark, and there was nothing in them at all. No anger. No grief. Only that terrible stillness, like deep water with no wind.

“Yes,” she said. “It feeds.”

She looked back up at the sky.

“I have fed it men,” she said. “Did I tell you that? On the road, I told you it hated the Kālākṣa. I didn’t tell you the rest. When I was fourteen, it told me its price. Souls. The souls of men. Or it would take what it pleased.” Her hands, in her lap, were perfectly still. “So they bring it prisoners. After battles. Enemy soldiers, deserters, criminals from the prisons. They chain them to the altar at the eyrie, and I stand there, because the pact requires that I stand there, and I watch it—” She stopped. “I have done that for nine years, Apothecary. I know every sound it makes when it eats.”

The wind moved the young leaves over their heads.

“I have fed it men,” said Lián Xueyao softly, “who did nothing worse than run away from a war they didn’t choose. Boys, some of them. Younger than Pei.” She looked at him. “I can feed it him.”

---

He had known she would say it. He had known it from the moment he saw her sitting there, looking north. Still, it went through him like cold water.

He came across the paved circle, slowly, through the drifted petals, and sat down on the other end of the stone bench. Not close. The width of the bench between them. The way he had sat once on a stair, a few steps above someone, in the rain.

“You could,” he said.

She said nothing.

“You could call it. It would come. It would burn this house, and him in it.” He looked at the pale roof of the east wing, the long gallery, the nursery beyond with its window open to the warm night. “And the servants. The cook who gave me the brazier. The maid who sleeps outside your mother’s — who slept outside your mother’s door. The steward, who is a Reng man and deserves it.” He paused. “And the nursery.”

Her hands moved, very slightly, in her lap.

“It wouldn’t stop at the house,” he said. “You know it wouldn’t. You said it yourself. It takes what it pleases. And tomorrow the King would send the army. And you would have to call it again. And again.” He was quiet a moment. “And every time, it would be hungrier. And every time, you would have to stand there and listen to the sounds it makes.”

“I know what I would have to do.”

“Yes,” he said. “I know you know.”

The pond was black and still. A single late petal fell from somewhere high in the tree, turned slowly in the moonlight, and landed on the water without a sound.

“Your mother didn’t ask you for vengeance,” said Esdel.

Xueyao went very still.

“In the garden,” he said. “At dawn. I heard her. She could have asked you for anything. She could have asked you to kill him. She was lucid enough. She knew what he’d done.” He kept his eyes on the pond. “She didn’t ask for the house, or the name, or the ships, or the gold. She said *him*. Your brother. Keep him safe. Keep him away from that man.” He turned his head and looked at her. “That’s all she asked. That’s the promise you made.”

For a long moment, nothing happened.

And then something in her face moved.

It was very small. A tremor at the corner of the mouth. A tightening around the eyes. But it was the first thing that had moved in her face since the pavilion — since the funeral, since the dawn when Changli screamed — and he watched it spread, slowly, the way a crack spreads through ice on a river in spring. Down from her eyes. Into her jaw. Into her shoulders, which began, very slightly, to shake.

She put her hands over her face.

She did not make a sound. She never did. But she bent forward on the stone bench beneath her father’s peach tree, with her face in her hands, and her whole body shook — and Esdel sat at the other end of the bench, and did not touch her, and did not speak, and did not leave.

Far away to the north, beyond the walls and the orchards and the burned plains, something vast and golden stopped its pacing in the dark, and lay down, and was still.

---

It was a long time before she spoke again. The moon had moved behind the tree.

“The blood is in him too,” she said. Her voice was hoarse. “The dragon’s blood. If he stays in this house, the Count will raise him. And when I die — at the front, in a year, in ten — it will pass to him. And the Count will be standing beside him when it speaks for the first time.” She lowered her hands. Her face was wet and blotched and entirely without dignity, and it was, Esdel thought, the first time he had ever seen her look her age. “A child. Fourteen. With that thing in his head, asking for souls. And that man beside him, telling him what to feed it.”

“Then he can’t stay in this house.”

“No.”

“And you can’t stay either.”

She looked at him.

“If you stay,” he said, “you’ll marry him, or you’ll kill him, or he’ll find a way to make you disappear the way he made your mother disappear. There isn’t another road.” He met her eyes. “So you leave. You take your brother, and you go.”

“Where?”

“Somewhere he can’t follow.”

“There is nowhere he can’t follow. There is nowhere the *Crown* can’t follow. I would be a deserter. A kidnapper. A traitor.” Her voice cracked on the word. “My father’s daughter.”

“Yes,” said Esdel.

She stared at him.

And then — to his astonishment, and he thought to hers — she laughed. It was a small, cracked, exhausted sound, barely a laugh at all. But it was one.

“You’re a terrible comfort, Apothecary,” she said.

“I know.” He looked at the pond. “Someone told me once I should learn to lie better.”

“Who?”

He was quiet for a moment.

“Your mother,” he said. “And before that—” He stopped.

Xueyao was watching him. In the dim light under the tree, with her wet face and her loosened hair, she did not look like the Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon at all. She looked like a tired young woman on a garden bench at midnight, looking at someone she had just realized she did not know anything about.

“And before that?” she said.

He looked down at his hands.

“There’s a village,” he said slowly. “Near the desert. A long way south and west of here. Where the caravan brought me, when I woke. There was an apothecary there. An old woman, and her granddaughter.” He did not say the name. He found he could not. “They took me in. They didn’t ask me where I came from. I lived in their attic for a spring and a summer.” A breath. “When the soldiers came, they wanted her. I went instead. And in the morning she — the granddaughter — she gave me this.”

He touched the chain at his throat. The small silver flower.

“It was her mother’s. Her mother went to the war, and didn’t come back. She said if she gave it away, it would mean the person wearing it would come home.” He was quiet. “I promised I would.” He was quiet a moment. “I’d already broken one promise to her that night. I promised her I’d stay, and less than an hour later I stood in the square and let them take me instead of her. She cried until she couldn’t breathe.” He looked at his hands. “I won’t break the other one. I don’t think I would survive it.”

Xueyao said nothing for a long time.

“The letters,” she said finally.

“I burned them.” He looked at the pond. “The Count had my service record brought up from the Ministry. He knows the name of the village. He said it to me in my room. *What a pretty name.*”

He heard her breath catch.

“So you see,” said Esdel quietly, “I can’t go home either. Not while he’s alive and watching. If I go back, I lead him to her door.”

The young leaves rustled overhead. The moon slid out from behind the branches and laid its pale light across the paved circle, and the drifted petals, and the two of them sitting at either end of the stone bench with the width of it between them.

“Then come with me,” said Lián Xueyao.

He turned his head.

She was not looking at him. She was looking at the pale roof of the nursery.

“Not for my sake,” she said. “I know you wouldn’t. You made your promise already, to someone else.” Her voice was very steady now. “Come because you can’t go home. Come because I will need someone who can keep a child alive on the road, and knows what’s in a cup before anyone drinks it, and doesn’t lie well enough to be a danger to me. And because—” She stopped. Something moved in her face, and was put away, very carefully, the way a soldier puts away a letter from home. “Because if you’re with me, the Count won’t be looking south. He’ll be looking for *us*. For as long as it takes.”

Esdel sat very still.

He understood what she was offering him. Not a place. Not safety. Only a direction for the hunt to run in — away from the river and the willows and the ivy-covered house at the edge of the desert. Away from a lamp on a stair.

It was the only gift in the world he could have accepted.

“Yes,” he said.

She nodded once. She did not thank him. He would not have wanted her to.

They sat there together beneath the peach tree for a while longer, in the moonlight, not speaking. Then she rose, and smoothed her robe, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand — once, briskly, like a soldier — and was the General again.

“Tomorrow,” she said. “We plan.”

She walked back across the garden toward the east wing. At the edge of the paved circle she paused, without turning.

“Apothecary.”

“General.”

“What was her name?”

He was silent for so long that she began to walk on.

“Lyora,” he said.

It was the first time he had said it aloud in a year. It came out very quietly. It hung in the warm night air beneath the peach tree for a moment, like a lantern on dark water, and then the wind took it.

Xueyao stood still.

“Lyora,” she repeated softly. As though testing the sound of it. As though putting it somewhere safe.

Then she went in.
