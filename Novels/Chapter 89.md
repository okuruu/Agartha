# **Chapter 89**

They were in the stable yard when the bell began to ring.

It was the alarm bell on the gatehouse — a great bronze thing, green with age, that had not been rung since Lord Hengzhi’s grandfather’s time. It had a voice like the end of the world. It came down through the storm over the roofs of the house, *clang, clang, clang*, flat and brazen and terrible, and every horse in the yard screamed and reared at once.

Han Ruo swore. Pei Lun, who had been holding the General’s horse, was nearly dragged off his feet.

“He didn’t drink,” said Xueyao.

She said it calmly. She was already swinging up into the saddle, her cloak streaming. “He didn’t sleep. He’s ringing it himself.”

“Who’ll come?” Pei gasped, fighting the reins. “Everyone’s asleep—”

“The city watch,” said Han Ruo grimly. “The street guard. The Count’s own men from his town house across the river. Anyone within a mile who hears that bell.” He was mounting too. “And they’ll come to the front gate. The great gate. Where we have to go out.”

Esdel was on his horse. He did not remember getting there. His medicine satchel was over his shoulder and his hands were steady on the reins and his heart was beating — even, precise, unhurried. Borrowed. It did not seem to know that anything was wrong.

“Then we go now,” said Xueyao. “Before they get here.” She drew her sword. The sound of it cut through the bell like a thread of silver. “Captain. Open the gate.”

---

The great gate of the Lián estate was a double door of iron-banded oak, twice the height of a man, beneath the stone arch where the old characters of the Lián name were still carved, and the red banner with its silver serpent had been hung again after the funeral. It was barred on the inside with a beam of timber as thick as a man’s body, in two iron brackets.

It took four of them to lift the beam. They lifted it. They threw it down on the cobbles with a crash that was lost in the bell. They hauled the great doors open, groaning on their hinges, and the storm came roaring in through the gap — and beyond it, in the street, in the rain, there were lanterns.

A great many lanterns.

They were coming up the street from the river. Twenty. Thirty. Men running, with swords and staves and torches that hissed in the rain. The street guard, in their leather caps. Men in the wine-colored livery of the Count’s household. And at the front of them, on a tall grey horse, with a sword in his hand and his white mourning coat blazing in the torchlight like a flag, rode Count Aldric Reng.

He was not leaning on his cane.

He had never needed it. Esdel understood that in a single cold instant, watching him ride up the street through the rain — straight-backed, easy in the saddle, the sword held loose and familiar in his right hand, the scar on the back of it pale in the torchlight. The cane had been a costume. Like the grief. Like everything.

The Count reined in at the foot of the street, fifty paces off. His men spread out behind him, across the width of it, a wall of torches and blades.

And he smiled.

“General,” he called. His voice carried easily over the rain, over the bell, pleasant and warm and faintly regretful. “Where are you going, at this hour? In this weather?” He tilted his head. “And where is my ward?”

---

Xueyao did not answer him.

She turned in the saddle, instead, and looked at her soldiers — fifteen old men and a boy, on their horses, in the gateway of her father’s house, with the storm behind them and the torches in front.

“There’s another way out,” said Han Ruo, low. “The river gate. We could go down the stair, along the wall—”

“On foot,” said Xueyao. “Without the horses. With every man in the city between us and the north gate by dawn.” She shook her head. “No. It’s this way, or not at all.”

Han Ruo looked at the street. At the torches. At the Count on his grey horse, smiling.

Then he looked at the gate. The great doors, standing open. The arch above them, with the Lián name carved in the stone.

Something moved in his old face. Esdel saw it — the same thing he had seen on the road, on the first night, when the captain had held out his broken wrist and said *he paid the physician himself, for a stableboy.* A kind of quiet, settling certainty.

“General,” said Han Ruo. “A word.”

She looked at him.

“Thirty years,” he said. “I served your father thirty years. I was there the day you were born. I held you, you know. Before your mother did, even — the midwife was busy, and your father was pacing, and they put you in my arms because I was standing closest.” He smiled a little through his grey beard. “Screamed like a gull. Bit my thumb. No teeth, but you tried.”

“Captain—”

“That man,” said Han Ruo, and nodded down the street at the Count, “stood on the docks. Opened the crates. Took the Lord’s chair. Took the Lady. Took the boy, or tried.” His voice did not rise. “I didn’t stop him. I was at the front, with you. I wasn’t here.” He turned his horse, slowly, until it stood in the center of the gateway, beneath the arch, facing the street. “I’m here now.”

Xueyao stared at him.

“The street narrows past the fountain,” said Han Ruo. “You know it does. You used to race your mare down it and your mother would shout from the gallery. Four men can hold it for as long as it takes. Longer, if they’re stubborn.” He drew his sword. “I’m very stubborn. So are these three.”

Three of the old soldiers had turned their horses too. They moved up quietly on either side of him, in the gateway, without being asked. They did not look at the General. They looked at the street.

“Go out the side lane,” said Han Ruo. “Behind the stables, past the old wine store, into the Street of Lanterns. It comes out below the Temple of the Moon. From there you can reach the north road. They won’t think of it; they’ll be too busy with us.” He settled his helmet on his head. “Go now, girl. Before he thinks of it himself.”

“*Han Ruo.*”

It was the only time Esdel ever heard her voice break.

The old captain looked at her from under the rim of his helmet. His eyes were very kind.

“You were always going to keep your promises,” he said. “Even the inconvenient ones. Your father said so.” He smiled. “Let an old man keep one of his.”

Then he turned his back on her, and raised his sword, and shouted down the street in a voice that had once carried across the parade ground of the Lián estate, forty years before — a voice that cut clean through the bell and the rain and the roaring of the wind.

“*For Lord Hengzhi!*”

And the four of them spurred their horses out of the gate, into the torchlight, straight at the Count.

---

Esdel did not remember the side lane.

He remembered only pieces of it, afterward. The General’s horse wheeling away from the gate, and her face in the flicker of the torches — white, set, streaming wet, her mouth a hard line. Pei Lun beside him, sobbing openly, his too-big bow bouncing against his back. The narrow dark lane behind the stables, and the old wine store, and the smell of wet straw and old barrels. And behind them, from the street, the sound.

He did not look back. None of them did. But the sound followed them — the clash of steel, and the scream of horses, and men shouting. And above it, once, very clear, an old man’s voice, laughing.

Then the lane turned, and the Street of Lanterns opened before them, empty and black and streaming with rain, its paper lanterns torn and dark on their strings. And the sound fell away behind them. And there was only the bell, still ringing, and the hooves of their horses on the wet stones, and the rain.

They rode.

---

They were almost to the Temple of the Moon when the Count’s men found them.

Six of them, on foot, coming out of a side alley at a run with torches and swords — men who had been sent around the back of the estate, Esdel thought afterward, in case of exactly this. Someone had thought of it after all. The Count was a patient man, and patient men think of everything.

The first of them caught Pei Lun’s horse by the bridle and dragged its head down. Pei went over its neck with a cry and hit the cobbles hard.

Esdel was off his horse before he knew he had moved.

He did not think. That was the thing he would remember — that there was no thought at all, only the old cold certainty rising up through him from somewhere beneath memory, the thing that had moved his hands in the tent at Edenridge, in the forest ambush, on the burning plain before the Titan. His hand was on the hilt of the short sword at his belt. The world was very clear.

And then, deliberately — slowly — he blinked.

*Sound dulled.*

The rain slowed. He could see it falling — each separate drop, hanging, turning in the torchlight like a bead of glass. The men’s faces slowed. The torches slowed, their flames stretching and curling like silk in water. The man standing over Pei Lun slowed, his sword coming up — up — with the terrible drowsy leisure of a thing in a dream.

Esdel could see his heart. He could see all their hearts — six small dark red lights beating in the dark, each at its own pace. He could see which ones were afraid. He could see which one was about to strike, and where.

He could see the rest, too. The weak knee. The wrist that would open too wide. The half-breath each man took before he committed, so that every blow was already written in the body before it came.

*The eye of mercy,* she had said, by the fire. *Whatever mercy there was in it, they had to bring themselves.*

He moved.

He did not kill them. Afterward, he would hold on to that, in the dark, the way a drowning man holds on to a spar. He moved through the slowed world between the drops of rain, and his short sword came down flat — on a wrist, and a sword went spinning; on the side of a head, behind the ear, and a man folded; on a knee, and another went down screaming, slowly, his mouth opening like a flower. He caught the man over Pei by the collar and threw him against the wall, and felt the impact go through his own arm like a bell.

Four. Five.

The sixth was running. Esdel let him run.

He blinked again, and the world crashed back in around him — noise, and rain, and the bell, and the torches roaring, and pain. Pain like needles driven through both eyes at once. He staggered. Something warm was running down his face that was not rain. He put up his hand and it came away red.

“*Esdel.*”

The General was beside him. On foot. She had her hand on his arm. Behind her, Pei Lun was scrambling up off the cobbles, white-faced, staring at the five men lying around them in the rain. At Esdel.

“Your eyes,” Xueyao said.

“I know.”

“They’re bleeding.”

“I know.” He blinked, hard. The world swam, and steadied. “It passes. It always passes.” He looked at the men on the cobbles. They were breathing. All of them. He counted, to be sure. “They’ll live.”

Xueyao looked at him. Blood on his face, and the rain washing it down. The red slowly fading from the whites of his eyes.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. He saw it in her face — the same thing he had seen by the fire in the ruined inn, when she had told him about the Kālākṣa, and the dragon that hated them. A careful, wary, old look, like someone stepping around ground she knew was mined.

And something else, beneath it. Something he did not have a name for.

“Can you ride?” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then ride.”

He rode.

Behind them, far back across the eastern quarter, the great bronze bell on the gatehouse of the Lián estate rang on, and on, into the storm. And then, all at once, in the middle of a stroke, it stopped.

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Esdel had ever heard.

None of them looked back. None of them said a word. They rode on up through the empty streets toward the Temple of the Moon and the north road, in the rain, in the dark, with the silence following them.

Behind them, in the gateway of the House of Lián, beneath the old carved name, an old man who had once held a screaming baby because he was standing closest had kept the last of his promises.
