# **Chapter 98**

At the edge of the borderlands, where the brown hills came down at last to a river and a ford and a scatter of stone houses, there was a market.

It was not much of a market. It happened once a month, on the flat ground beside the ford, when the herders came down out of the hills with their goats and their wool and the traders came up from the south with their salt and their needles and their cheap tin pots. There was no magistrate. There was no door to nail a decree to. There was a tree, an old twisted thornbush by the water, and someone had once hung a copy of the decree on it — but the goats had eaten half of it, and the rain had washed the rest to grey pulp, and no one in the borderlands could read anyway.

Esdel went down to it alone, in the grey of the morning, with his hood up.

The General had not wanted him to. None of them had. But they needed salt, and flour, and cloth for bandages, and thread, and he was the only one among them whose face had not been drawn on any wanted bill. The decree had named him. It had not described him. *The apothecary Esdel, of no family.* A man of middle height and no particular features, with brown eyes, in a worn grey coat. There were a thousand such men in the borderlands.

He had wondered about that, once, in the hut. Half the capital had looked at his face — the masters of the college, the whole Lián household, the Count across a small table in the east wing. Someone could have drawn it. No one had. He had turned the question over for a night, the way he turned over anything that did not add up, and then he had been too tired, and too grateful, to turn it any more.

“One hour,” Xueyao had said. “If you’re not back in one hour, we come down.”

“I’ll be back.”

She had looked at him — with her short ragged hair, and the white robe beneath her dark cloak, which she had not taken off since the ridge — and said nothing. Then she had nodded.

---

He bought the salt and the flour and the cloth. He bought thread from an old woman with no teeth who tried to sell him a goat as well. He bought a small bag of dried apricots for Pei Lun, who had been talking about apricots for three days with a longing that was painful to hear.

And then, at the far end of the market, where the traders from the south had set up their stalls beside the ford, he saw a man with a string of mules.

He was a peddler. A thin, weathered, cheerful man with a patched coat and a long grey beard, sitting on an upturned crate beside his mules and whittling a stick. His packs were half-empty; he had sold most of what he had carried up from the south. He was going home. Esdel could see it in the way he sat — easy, idle, with his face turned toward the ford and the long road beyond it, running down out of the hills toward the plains.

Esdel stopped.

He stood there in the middle of the little market, in the grey morning, with his arms full of salt and flour, and looked at the peddler for a long time.

Then he went over.

---

“Going south?” he said.

The peddler looked up. He had very pale blue eyes, faded with sun and wind, and a face so creased it was hard to tell where the smile lines ended and the rest began.

“South and west,” he said. “All the way down. Back to the plains, and then along the river road to the forest country. I’ve a sister there. I winter with her.” He tilted his head. “Why? You want something carried?”

Esdel did not answer at once.

He set down the salt and the flour on the peddler’s crate. He reached into the pocket of his coat, and took out a coil of cord.

It was nothing. Plain brown cord, the kind that tied up bundles of herbs or bound the joints of bamboo channels. He had bought it that morning from the old woman with no teeth. He had been carrying it in his pocket all through the market, not knowing why. Not letting himself know.

He sat down on the ground beside the peddler’s crate, with his back to the market, and began to tie a knot.

His hands knew how. They had always known. He did not have to think. The cord went over, and under, and back through itself — a small tight neat knot, wound in a way no one in the borderlands wound cord, or in the capital, or at the front. In a way no one anywhere wound cord, except once, a long time ago, in a village at the edge of the desert, beside a river, on a hot afternoon at the start of the summer. The knot that pulled tighter the harder the water pressed against it.

He tied it once. Then again, a hand’s width further down. Then a third time.

Three knots. On a length of plain brown cord.

He sat looking at them for a long time.

He thought of all the letters he had burned. The thin sheaf of them, soft at the folds, going to ash in a copper dish in a narrow room at the end of the east wing. The last one, in the stillroom, torn into pieces too small to hold a word. He thought of the decree. *Late of the village of Edenridge.* He thought of the men who would be watching, in the village, on the river road, at the inn. Waiting for a letter. Waiting for a word. Waiting for anything at all that might lead them to the traitor’s apothecary, worth a thousand gold crowns, living or dead.

A letter could be followed. A letter could be opened, and read, and carried to a man in a nursery with a ruined hand, who would know exactly what it meant.

A piece of cord could not.

It was nothing. It was string. It was the kind of thing a peddler might have in the bottom of his pack for tying up his wares, and throw away, and no one would ever look at twice. It said nothing. It had no name on it. It had no words at all.

He had only ever needed one person to read it.

---

He looked up at the peddler.

“There’s a village,” he said. His voice was very quiet. “In the forest country. On the river, at the edge of the desert. A green valley between low hills.”

The peddler was watching him with his faded blue eyes. He did not say anything.

“There’s an apothecary there,” said Esdel. “An old house with ivy on the walls. An old woman, and her granddaughter.” He held out the cord. “Give this to the granddaughter. Into her hand. No one else. You’ll know her. She has green eyes.”

The peddler looked at the cord. Three small neat knots on a length of plain brown string.

“That’s all?” he said.

“That’s all.”

“No message?”

“No.” Esdel held it out. “If anyone asks — anyone at all — tell them the truth. It’s a piece of cord. For tying herbs. That’s all it is.” He paused. “I won’t ask you to lie for me. Only don’t say more than that. Don’t tell her who gave it to you. Don’t describe me. Don’t say where you were. Just give it to her, and go.”

The peddler was silent for a long time. The ford ran over its stones. Somewhere behind them in the market, a goat was complaining.

Then he held out his hand.

Esdel put the cord into it. And a coin — a silver one, the last he had — on top.

The peddler looked at the coin. Then he gave it back.

“I’ve a sister,” he said, “in the forest country. I told you. She’s a widow. Her boy went for a soldier, three years ago now.” He tucked the cord, very carefully, into the inside pocket of his patched coat, over his heart. “She never got a letter either. Not one. Not a word.” He buttoned the pocket. “I’d have given a good deal, some winters, for someone to bring her a piece of string.”

He looked at Esdel with his faded blue eyes, and his creased face did something very gentle.

“Into her hand,” he said. “No one else. Green eyes.” He nodded. “Say nothing.”

Esdel could not speak. He only nodded back.

Then he picked up the salt, and the flour, and the cloth, and the thread, and the small bag of apricots for Pei Lun, and stood up, and walked away through the little market beside the ford without looking back. Up the goat track, into the brown hills, toward the ruined tower where the others were waiting.

He was back in less than an hour. The General was standing at the edge of the ruin when he came up the slope, with her hand on her sword. She looked at his face. She did not ask him anything.

He was grateful for that. He did not think he could have told her.

---

The peddler came down out of the hills in the autumn.

It was a long road. Down through the brown borderlands to the plains, where the harvest was in and the stubble fields were burning, sending up long blue columns of smoke into the pale sky. Along the river road, day after day, past towns whose names he knew and towns whose names he did not. Into the forest country at last, as the leaves were turning — gold and red and brown along the river, drifting down onto the water, catching in the eddies.

He stopped at his sister’s house. He stayed three days. He did not tell her about the cord.

Then he went on alone, down the river, to the edge of the forest, where the trees thinned and the land opened out into a green valley between low hills, and a river ran between its willows, and on the slope above it, catching the late autumn light, there were long thin lines of bamboo, like threads of glass, carrying water uphill.

There were two men in grey sitting on the bench outside the inn. They watched him come up the lane with his mules. He nodded to them, pleasantly, the way he nodded to everyone. They did not nod back.

He found the apothecary at the top of the lane. An old house, with ivy on the walls, going red now with the autumn. The door was open. Inside, in the dim light, jars stood in long rows along the shelves, catching the sun like water caught in glass.

There was a girl at the worktable. She was grinding something in a mortar. She had dark hair, tied back with a cloth, and when she looked up at the sound of the mules in the lane, the light from the doorway caught her eyes.

They were green. Bright as spring leaves.

---

She did not understand at first.

He came in, and bought a small pot of salve for his mules’ hooves, and paid for it, and talked about the weather, and the harvest, and the road. And then, as he was turning to go, he stopped, and felt in the inside pocket of his coat, as though he had just remembered something, and took out a piece of cord.

“Thought you might have a use for this,” he said. “For tying herbs.”

He put it into her hand.

She looked down at it.

It was a piece of plain brown cord. A little frayed at the ends. With three small knots tied in it, a hand’s width apart.

The peddler watched her face.

He watched it not change at all, for a long moment — while she looked at the cord in her palm, puzzled, polite, the way anyone would look at a piece of string a stranger had given them. And then he watched her thumb move. Slowly. Along the cord. To the first knot.

And stop.

He saw her go very still. He saw the color leave her face, and then come back, all at once, flooding up from her throat. He saw her other hand come up, slowly, as though of its own accord, and close over the first one, so that the cord lay between her two palms, held the way a person holds something small and alive.

She did not look up at him. She did not ask him anything. She was looking at the knot.

*You tie them like you’re afraid the water will get away.*

The peddler had been told to say nothing. So he said nothing. He only touched his cap to her, very gently, and turned, and went out of the old house with ivy on its walls, and down the lane past the two men in grey on the bench outside the inn, who watched him go with bored, incurious eyes — a peddler with a string of mules, and nothing in his hands at all — and out along the river road, and away.

He did not look back. But he thought about her face, for a long time afterward. All that winter, by his sister’s fire.

---

Lyora stood at the worktable for a long time, with the cord held between her hands.

She did not cry. She had thought, if it ever came — a letter, a word, anything — she would cry. She did not. She only stood there in the quiet shop, in the autumn light, with the jars catching the sun around her, and held it.

Three knots. She knew them. She had known them the moment her thumb touched the first one, the way she had known them once on a drying rack against the south wall of the yard. His hands. His knot. The one that held the water.

He was alive.

He had held this. He had sat somewhere — she did not know where, she would never know where — and tied these three knots with his own hands, and given them to a stranger to carry a very long way. With no name on them. With no words. With nothing at all that anyone watching could read.

Nothing anyone could read but her.

She thought of the night in the summer, on the stairs, with the lamp burning down to a blue bead. The small cold voice. *He forgot everyone he ever loved once already. What makes you think you’re any different?*

He had not forgotten the knot.

Out of everything he had lost — his name, his home, his whole life before the desert — his hands had kept this. The knot he had tied a hundred times on a hot afternoon at the start of the summer, thinking of the height of her reach. He had carried it out of this valley and through a war and a storm and a river, and somewhere at the edge of the world he had sat down and tied it three times, for her.

The voice had been wrong. She had known it was wrong by morning. But she had not *known* it — not like this, not in her hands.

She knew what they meant. She did not need to be told. She had sat on the stairs once, in the rain, and said everything but one thing, and he had heard that one most clearly of all. And now he had sent her three knots on a piece of string, from somewhere she would never see, and said everything but one thing.

And she heard it.

Behind her, in the doorway to the kitchen, Arwen was standing with one hand on the frame. Lyora did not know how long she had been there. The old woman did not ask what the peddler had brought. She did not ask anything at all. She only looked at her granddaughter standing at the worktable with a piece of cord held between her hands like something alive, and her old green eyes filled, very slowly, and she did not wipe them.

Neither of them said a word.

---

That night, Lyora took the lamp from the shelf.

She filled it, and trimmed the wick, and lit it, and turned it low. She carried it to the foot of the stairs, and set it on the second step, where it had burned every night for more than a year.

Then she took the cord from her pocket — she had not let go of it all day; she had kept it in her hand while she worked, and while she ate, and while she sat with Arwen in the evening by the window — and she tied it around the base of the lamp. Carefully. Once around, and then again, so that it lay snug against the brass. So that the three small knots sat in a row at the front, in the gold light, where she would see them every night when she came down the stairs.

She tied it off with a knot of her own. Plain. Ordinary. The kind anyone might tie.

Then she sat down on the stair beside it — the second step, the way she had sat once, in the rain, with her head against his knee — and looked at the three small knots in the lamplight for a long time.

Outside, the autumn wind was moving in the ivy. The river was running between its willows. In the inn at the bottom of the lane, two men in grey were asleep in their beds, with nothing to report. And very far away — farther than she could imagine, farther than any road she had ever walked or ever would — in the wind and the dark at the edge of the Kingdom, a man with one name was keeping a promise by staying away.

She did not know where he was.

She did not know if he would ever come back.

But she knew he was alive. And she knew he remembered. And she knew — the way she had known on the stairs, in the rain, in the silence where the word should have been — that some things are not said because they do not need saying.

The lamp stayed lit.

The cord stayed tied.

And the old house at the edge of the desert, with its ivy and its jars and its silent third stair, held its small gold light out into the dark — as it had every night, and as it would every night after — toward the door, and the lane, and the river road, and the whole wide world beyond the forest.

Waiting.
