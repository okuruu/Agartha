# **Chapter 77**

“You can undo it,” said Xueyao.

It was not a question. She stood at the window of her sitting room, where the orchids her father had loved stood in their pots along the sill — the ones the Count had so thoughtfully moved there — and she did not turn around.

Esdel stood by the door. Changli sat at the table, very still.

“I can try,” he said.

“Try, then.”

“General—”

“The college will not help. The court will not help. My mother—” Her voice did not change. “My mother has asked me to stop. So I will not ask anyone else for anything, ever again. I am asking you.” She turned. “Can you make something that will undo what he gives her?”

He had thought about it. He had thought about little else, since the hearing — since the Count’s soft voice in his doorway, since the ash in the copper dish. It had been, in a way, a relief to think about it. A problem with edges. Something his hands could hold.

“Not undo,” he said. “Not completely. I don’t know what the second—” He caught himself. “I don’t know everything that’s in it. But the greyveil I know. Its effect wanes if it isn’t renewed. If she stopped taking it, her body would begin to clear it on its own. Slowly. Over weeks. There are things that can help that along — bitter roots that draw it out, things that steady the heart while it recovers.” He hesitated. “But if it stops all at once, after a year — it won’t be gentle. She’ll be sick. Fever, trembling, sleeplessness. Pain. The body grows used to it. It will fight to have it back.”

“How sick?”

“Very. For a while.” He met her eyes. “And he’ll notice. He sits with her every day. He watches her face as she drinks. He’ll see that it’s not working the way it should.”

Xueyao was silent.

“Then we don’t stop it all at once,” said Changli.

They both looked at her.

She had not moved from the table. She sat with her hands folded before her, her beautiful face composed and thoughtful, the way it had been on the night in the barracks when she reported the result of her test.

“The Count carries the bottle in his coat,” she said. “And every tenth day, the old bottle goes back to the west wing, and a new one comes up from Master Vale.” She tilted her head. “Master Vale sends it up by way of the steward’s tray. I have become very familiar with the steward’s tray.” A faint smile. “What if the bottle that came up from the west wing were not quite the bottle Master Vale had filled? What if it were the same color, and the same thickness, and the same smell — but with half the greyveil? And the next one, half of that?”

Esdel stared at her.

“It could be done,” he said slowly. “The resin I can match. The color. The weight on the tongue. Honey and a little burnt sugar and—” His mind was already moving, measuring. “The second thing, I can’t make. I don’t know what it is. But I could leave it in. Separate the greyveil out of Vale’s tonic, reduce it, and put the rest back as it was. So the taste doesn’t change. So he doesn’t—”

He stopped.

*So the binding stays,* said a voice in his head. *So she still turns to him like a flower. Only now she’ll have the will to wonder why.*

He did not say it.

“How long?” said Xueyao.

“To make the first bottle? Four days. To bring her down to nothing, without him seeing?” He thought. “Six weeks. Maybe more.”

“And then?”

“And then she’ll be herself again,” said Esdel. “As much as anyone can be, after a year of that.”

Xueyao looked at him for a long moment. Then at Changli. Then back at the window, and the orchids on the sill, and beyond them the garden, where in the long amber light of the afternoon a maid was already carrying the white tea things down the path to the pavilion.

“Do it,” she said.

---

He did it.

He could not use his own room — not now, not with the Count’s patient eyes on the east wing. Han Ruo found him a place. There was an old stillroom behind the stables, where Lord Hengzhi’s grandmother had once made plum wine; it had been locked for twenty years, and the key was on a ring the captain had carried for thirty. Esdel worked there at night, by one shuttered lamp, while Pei Lun sat outside the door on an upturned bucket and whittled, and whistled softly if anyone came near the stables.

No one did. The Count’s household did not come to the stables. It was beneath them.

It was slow, patient, delicate work, and he loved it, in a way he was ashamed of. To take Vale’s amber tonic and draw the greyveil out of it, layer by layer, with spirit and time and a cold stone to settle it. To taste the remainder on the tip of his tongue, and add honey, and burnt sugar, and a thread of bitter bark, until the ghost of what he had taken out was there again in the mouth — the weight, the warmth, the faint dark edge — but not in the blood. To hold the finished thing to the lamp, and see it glow the same beguiling gold.

And beneath it all, untouched, the gold oil he could not name. Shifting faintly in the light, as though something moved in it that was not the flame.

On the fifth night, he gave Changli a small bottle of dark glass. It was the same bottle — she had brought him the empty — and it held the same amber, to the same depth, and it smelled of the same slow sweetness when he drew the stopper.

She held it to the window. She turned it. She looked at him.

“Half?”

“Half.”

She slid it into her sleeve and went out.

---

It was a strange, quiet, terrible time.

The house did not change. The Count came to the pavilion every afternoon in the amber hour, and poured the drops, and stirred them, and Lady Anlan drank, and took his hand, and held it to her cheek. Esdel watched from the gallery with his basket of cuttings. He could not see any difference.

Then, in the second week, he did.

It was very small. Lady Anlan was sitting in the pavilion after the Count had gone, alone with the empty cup, the way she sat every afternoon — soft and drowsing, her eyes half-closed, watching the petals fall on the pond. And a petal fell on her hand.

She looked at it.

That was all. She looked at it for a long time — really looked, the way a person looks at a thing they are seeing for the first time. Then she lifted her hand and turned it, so the petal slid off onto the cushion. And she frowned. A small, puzzled crease between her brows, the kind a woman makes when she has walked into a room and cannot remember why.

She sat like that, frowning at her own hand, until the maid came to take her in.

In the third week, the trembling began.

It was in her hands first, a fine unsteadiness, so that the teacup rattled faintly against its saucer when she set it down. The Count noticed. Esdel saw him notice. He saw him take her hand in the pavilion, and hold it, and feel it shake — and saw, for one moment, those clear grey eyes go very still.

Then the Count smiled and kissed her fingers and said something that made her laugh.

That night Master Vale was summoned to the Lady’s chambers. He stayed an hour. When he came out, walking back along the gallery to the west wing in his small grey way, he passed the door where Esdel stood in the shadow — and did not look at him.

The Count did not change the dose. Esdel did not know why. Perhaps Vale had called it a spring chill, a disorder of the nerves, a thing to be expected. Perhaps the Count believed him.

Perhaps he was only waiting.

In the fourth week, she had a fever that lasted three days and nights, and the household spoke of nothing else, and she did not come to the pavilion at all. On the third night, Esdel stood in the dark of the gallery beneath her window and listened to her crying out in her sleep — not for the Count, but for someone else, a name he could not hear, over and over, in a voice that did not sound soft at all anymore.

It sounded like a woman who had woken in a strange room, and was very frightened, and very angry, and did not yet know which.
