# **Chapter 68**

On the fourth night, the rain came, and they sheltered in a ruined waystation at the crossing of two roads.

It had been an inn once. Now it was four walls and half a roof and a hearth big enough to roast an ox, black with the fires of a hundred travelers. The soldiers filled it with their wet cloaks and their steaming horses and their low, tired voices, and Han Ruo built a fire so large that Pei Lun declared it visible from the capital.

Esdel took the far corner, near the broken window, where the draft kept the smoke away from his herbs. He had spread a cloth on the floor and was sorting through the medicine chest by firelight — checking what the damp had spoiled, what would last — when a shadow fell across his work.

He looked up.

The General stood over him with two cups in her hands.

It was the first time she had come near him since they left North Ridge. Up close, out of her armor, in a plain dark coat with rain still beaded on the shoulders, she looked younger than he had thought. Her black hair was coming loose from its soldier’s knot. Her golden eyes held the firelight the way still water holds the moon — reflecting it perfectly, letting none of it in.

“Tea,” she said. “Changli made it. She says I’m to stop frightening the apothecary.”

He took the cup. “You don’t frighten me, General.”

“No,” she agreed, and sat down on the broken bench beneath the window, as though it were a throne. “I noticed that. It’s very rude of you.”

He did not know if she was joking. He suspected she did not quite know either.

For a while they only drank the tea and listened to the rain on what remained of the roof. Across the room, Pei Lun was attempting to teach two older soldiers a gambling game with pebbles, and losing.

Then Xueyao said, without looking at him, “I asked you a question once. On the night of the Titan.”

He had known it was coming. Still, his hands went still on the cup.

“Who are you?” she said. “I’m asking again. I’m taking you into my mother’s house, Apothecary. I would like to know what I am bringing through the door.”

He looked at the fire. It was a fair question. It was, he thought, the only question anyone had ever really asked him.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Her eyes narrowed very slightly. He went on before she could speak.

“That isn’t an evasion. It’s the truth.” He turned the cup in his hands. “A little more than a year ago, I woke in the desert. East of here — far east. I don’t know how I came there. I didn’t know my own name until it came out of my mouth, days later, to a stranger. *Esdel.* That was all there was. Everything before the sand is—” He searched for the word. “Blank. Not dark. Not hidden. Blank. Like a page no one has written on.”

The rain ticked through the broken roof.

“There was a creature,” he said, quieter. “In the desert. It took my arm.” He held up his right hand, and turned it in the firelight — whole, ordinary, scarred across the knuckles from a winter of work. “When I woke in a village at the desert’s edge, the arm was back. The healer who tended me swears it was never gone.” A breath. “I cut myself, once, to see. It healed overnight. At the front, seven arrows. They healed in an hour.”

He had not said that aloud before. Not to anyone. Not to Halden, not to Benn, not to—

He stopped the thought there.

“And the eyes,” Xueyao said.

“And the eyes.” He set the cup down. “I didn’t know what they were called until you told me. I only know that when I think I’m going to die, the world stops. And I can see—” He hesitated. “Everything. Every heartbeat. Every intention, before it moves. And afterward, it feels as though someone has pushed needles through my skull.”

She was quiet for a long time.

When he finally looked at her, she was not looking at him. She was looking at the fire, and her face had changed. The cool, elegant mask was still there, but beneath it something had shifted — something careful and old, like a soldier stepping around ground she knew was mined.

“*Karunākṣa*,” she said softly. “Do you know what the word means?”

He shook his head.

“It’s the old tongue. The dragon tongue.” She turned her cup slowly. “*Karuṇā* is mercy. Compassion. *Akṣa* is the eye. The eye of mercy.” A faint, humorless curve of her lips. “A pretty name for a terrible thing.”

“Why terrible?”

“Because of what it sees.” She finally looked at him. “The stories say the House of Kālākṣa could look at a living thing and see how long its heart had left. The span of it, the way you or I might see a candle and know how much wax remains. That they were healers, once, the finest in the world, and spent their mercy accordingly. The eye that knows where mercy is still of use.” Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. “Stories grow in the telling. Especially about the dead.”

She turned the cup again.

“What my dragon showed me was simpler. Men and women who could not be touched. Who saw the blade before the hand that held it knew it would move. One of them against ten, and the ten lying in the grass.” Her voice was very even. “Not a healer’s eye, Esdel. A soldier’s. Whatever mercy there was in it, they had to bring themselves.”

The fire cracked. Across the room, Pei Lun lost another round of pebbles, loudly.

“What happened to them?” Esdel asked.

Xueyao was silent for so long that he thought she would not answer.

“The dragons remember them,” she said at last. “Mine does. When I was fourteen, the first time it spoke to me, it showed me a picture of them in my mind. Men and women with red eyes, standing in a field.” Her fingers tightened, very slightly, on the cup. “It hated them. I have never felt anything hate the way it hated them. As though they had seen something about it that it could not forgive.”

“And so they were—”

“Gone. Every last one. Long before my grandmother’s grandmother.” She set the cup down. “I don’t know who did it. I know the dragon-blooded houses did not grieve.”

He sat with that. The rain. The fire. His own hands, which had held the dying and known, somehow, before the pulse failed.

*You’ve done this before,* a voice had whispered once, in a tent in a village far away. He had never known what it meant.

“I have told you more than I have told anyone,” Xueyao said. It was not warm. It was a statement of fact, the way she might report a casualty count. “I did it because I need you to understand something. My dragon is at North Ridge. It will stay there — I will not call it. But it can feel me, Apothecary. Across any distance. And it can feel what I feel.” She met his eyes. “If it ever sees you the way I have seen you — with those eyes open — I do not know what it will do.”

He held her gaze.

“Then I’ll try to keep them closed,” he said.

Something moved in her face. It might, in another woman, have been the beginning of a smile.

She rose. She picked up both cups. At the edge of his cloth, she paused.

“The letters,” she said, not looking back. “The ones you don’t send.”

He went very still.

“You should send them,” said the General of the Northern Front, “before you’re somewhere they can’t reach.”

And she walked back across the ruined inn to the fire, and her soldiers made room for her without looking up, the way they had made room for him.

He sat in the corner for a long time afterward, with the rain coming through the broken window and the thin sheaf of paper warm against his chest.

On the fifth day, in the late afternoon, the road crested a long hill, and the capital lay below them.

It filled the whole valley. White walls, and within them, more walls; roofs of blue and green tile climbing in terraces toward the palace on its hill, where gold caught the slanting light and threw it back at the sky. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys. The river ran through the heart of it, broad and brown and crowded with boats, toward a harbor he could not see, where masts stood like a winter forest.

He had never seen anything so large. Or — he did not know. Perhaps he had. Something in him looked at those walls and did not feel wonder. It felt something colder. Recognition, perhaps. Or dread.

Beside him, Pei Lun whistled low.

At the head of the column, the General had stopped her horse. She sat very straight in the saddle, looking down at the city where she was born — at one terrace in particular, in the eastern quarter, near the river, where a long wall enclosed gardens and pale roofs and something that might, at this distance, have been a peach tree in flower.

She did not move for a long time.

Then she lifted her hand, and they went down.
