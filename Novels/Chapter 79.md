# **Chapter 79**

The rumor reached the Lián house before the Count did.

It came in with the morning bread, as rumors in that city always did — with the baker’s boy, who had it from the fishmonger, who had it from a clerk of the Ministry of Rites who drank at the tavern by the bridge. By the time the Count’s carriage came clattering through the great gate, every servant in the kitchens knew it, and by the time he had climbed the steps and handed his cane to the steward, it had gone up the back stairs and along the galleries and into the east wing, where Pei Lun heard it in the corridor and came running to Esdel’s door with his face gone white.

*The King is going to make the Count guardian of the Lián heir.*

---

“Not yet,” said Xueyao.

She sat in her sitting room with the orchids on the sill behind her. Her mother was asleep in the next room — the General had carried her there herself, from the garden, when she began to sway on the stones, and laid her on her own bed, and dismissed the Count’s maid with a single look that the girl would remember for the rest of her life. Changli sat by the bed now. The door between the rooms was open a hand’s width.

“It’s only talk,” Xueyao said. “Not yet a decree.”

“The Count was at the palace all night,” said Han Ruo, standing by the door. His voice was a low growl. “In private audience with His Majesty. The grooms heard it from the palace grooms.”

“I know where he was.”

“Then—”

“Then it’s talk,” she said again, “until it isn’t.” She looked at Esdel, who stood by the window. “Tell them what it means.”

Esdel was not sure why she had asked him. Perhaps only because he was the one who had least reason to be afraid of the answer.

“The Lián house was stripped,” he said slowly. “The ships, the ports, the trading companies — all put under the Crown’s supervision. Which means the Grand Duke. But the estate itself is still here. This house. The land. Lady Anlan’s dowry, which the Count took when he married her.” He paused. “And the name. The name was never erased. There’s a Lián heir. A boy. Too young to hold anything. Which means someone has to hold it for him.”

“And whoever holds it,” said Xueyao, “holds everything. Until he comes of age.” Her voice was very flat. “Or until he doesn’t.”

The room was silent.

“There’s more,” she said.

They waited.

“The Heavenly Dragon,” said Lián Xueyao, “is bound to a bloodline. Not to me. To the blood. It has been bound to the Lián line since before the Kingdom had a name. When I die, it will pass to the next of the blood who can bear it.” She looked through the half-open door at her mother’s sleeping form. “I have no children. There is only one other.”

Han Ruo made a sound in his throat.

“He’s a baby,” said Pei Lun from the corridor, where he had been listening without permission. His voice cracked. “He’s just a *baby*.”

“Yes,” said Xueyao. “And the man who raises him will teach him what to love, and whom to obey, and when he is grown and I am gone, will stand beside him when the dragon speaks.” She turned, finally, to look out the window at the garden. “I don’t think the Count wants my mother’s dowry, Captain. I think he never did.”

---

The Count did not come to the east wing that day. He did not come to the pavilion at the amber hour, either — for the first time since Esdel had lived in that house. He sent word by the steward that the Lady was indisposed and must rest, and that her physician would attend her.

Master Vale came at dusk. Xueyao met him at the door of her own rooms.

“My mother is sleeping,” she said. “She will not be disturbed.”

Master Vale looked at her with his small grey eyes. He had a leather case in one hand. He did not argue. He only inclined his head and said, very mildly, “The Count will be informed, General,” and went away again down the gallery.

That night, Xueyao slept on the floor beside her mother’s bed. Changli slept in the chair. Han Ruo sat in the corridor with his sword across his knees.

Esdel, in his narrow room at the end of the wing, did not sleep at all.

---

Across the city, in a private chamber of the palace where the windows looked east over the river, the King sat alone at a small table with a map.

It was an old map. It showed the Kingdom not as it was now but as it had been three hundred years before — before the eastern ports, before the merchant houses, before the war. In the margins, in faded red ink, someone long dead had drawn the great noble lines and their sigils. A griffin. A serpent. A crane over the moon.

And in the far north, beyond the ridges, a coiled golden shape with the old characters beside it: *Heaven’s Beast. Bound to the blood of Lián.*

The King looked at it for a long time.

The door opened behind him. The Prime Minister came in, and bowed, and waited.

“He asked for the boy,” said the King, without turning around.

“Yes, Majesty. And?”

“And I told him I would consider it.” The King touched the coiled shape with one fingertip. “He asked with great eloquence. He spoke of the child’s welfare, and the preservation of an ancient name, and his own humble willingness to bear the burden. He wept a little. He does that very well.” A pause. “He did not mention the dragon once.”

The Prime Minister was silent.

“Which is how I know,” said the King, “that he thinks of nothing else.”

He sat back. The lamp threw his shadow long across the map.

“Rowan will not like it,” said the Prime Minister carefully.

“No. Rowan will not like it at all.” The King’s voice was quiet and tired and entirely without expression. “Rowan wanted the Maiden at the front, fighting his war, bound to his service. Aldric wants her in his house, and her brother in his nursery, and the beast in his leash. Each of them believes he is the one holding the other by the collar.” He looked down at the map. “Let them pull. The harder they pull, the tighter the collar grows. And a collar, in the end, is only a thing to hold a dog until someone else is ready to take the leash.”

The Prime Minister bowed very low.

“And the General, Majesty? The Maiden herself?”

The King was silent for so long that the Prime Minister thought he had not heard.

“She accused him in his garden,” said the King at last. “Before forty witnesses. She petitioned the college. She will not bend. She is her father’s daughter.” He folded the map, slowly, along its old creases. “It is a great pity. I liked her father.”

---

Grand Duke Rowan Hale came to the Lián house the next afternoon.

He did not come in the front. He came through the garden gate on the river side, alone, in a plain dark cloak, and walked up the path beneath the peach tree to the pavilion — where the Count sat alone at the low table with the white tea things, pouring a cup of tea that no one was there to drink.

Esdel saw it from the gallery. He was carrying a basin of cold water to the General’s rooms. He stopped.

The Duke did not sit. He stood at the edge of the pavilion with his hands clasped behind his back and his pale eyes on the Count, and spoke. Esdel could not hear the words. He could see the Count’s face — the pleasant, attentive smile — and the Duke’s, which was like carved ice.

The Duke spoke for some time. Once he gestured, briefly, toward the house — toward the east wing, and the pale roof of the nursery beyond it.

The Count laughed.

He set down the teapot, and leaned back on the cushions, and laughed — warm and easy and delighted, the way a man laughs at a friend’s good joke. Then he said something. Only a few words. He said them lightly, smiling up at the Duke over the rim of his teacup, and when he had said them, he drank.

The Duke stood very still.

Then he turned and walked back down the path beneath the peach tree, to the river gate, and out. He did not look back. His cloak stirred the fallen petals on the stones as he passed, and they rose, briefly, and settled again.

Esdel stood on the gallery with the basin of water going warm in his hands, and watched the Count in the pavilion pour himself another cup of tea.

He had seen men in the field tents who were very near the end, and did not know it. There was a look they had. A brightness. As though the fever had burned away every fear and left only a kind of radiant, fearless certainty.

The Count had that look now.

Esdel did not know what it meant. He only knew that he had never once, in all the field tents, seen it end well.
