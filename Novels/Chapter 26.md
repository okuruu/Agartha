# **Chapter 26**

Market day came on the hottest morning of the summer.

The square was loud with it — awnings snapping in the dry wind, hawkers calling over one another, goats complaining, children weaving between the stalls with sticky hands. On market days, traders came up from the river road, and the air filled with smells that did not belong to Edenridge: pepper, lamp oil, tanned leather, something sharp and floral that made the eyes water.

Esdel had come for nails. Reyn had sent him with a list and a warning — *don’t let old Pell sell you the rusted ones, he’ll try* — and he had just finished bargaining for the good ones when a woman’s voice called out behind him.

“You. Tall one. You look like you know things.”

He turned.

She stood beside a cart heaped with bolts of cloth — indigo, saffron, a red so deep it was nearly black. A dye-merchant, by the stains on her hands, blue to the wrist. Sun-browned, sharp-eyed, perhaps thirty, with the brisk manner of someone who had talked her way through a hundred market towns and meant to talk her way through a hundred more.

“My wheel pulls left,” she said, slapping it. “All the way from the river it pulls left. My mule’s about to leave me for a better woman. What’s wrong with it?”

Esdel crouched and looked. It took him a moment.

“Your load,” he said. “It’s heavier on this side. The axle’s bearing unevenly. The wheel isn’t the problem — the weight is.” He pointed at the tall stack of indigo. “Move those to the center. Over the axle.”

She stared at him. Then she laughed — a big, surprised, full-throated laugh that turned heads across the square.

“That’s *it*? I paid a wheelwright in Harrowmere two silver to tell me it was the spokes!”

“It isn’t the spokes,” Esdel said, perfectly serious.

That made her laugh harder. She reached out and gave his forearm a quick, friendly squeeze — the kind of touch traders traded a hundred times a day, meaning nothing but *thank you* — and said, “You’ve saved my mule’s dignity. Here—” and pressed a small twist of cloth into his palm, a scrap of the deep red. “For your trouble.”

Then she was already shouting at the boy minding her mule, and the moment was gone. It had lasted no longer than it takes a kettle to boil.

Esdel looked down at the scrap of red in his hand, puzzled, tucked it into his pocket, and went to find Reyn’s hinges.

He did not see Lyora standing at the herb-seller’s stall across the square, a bundle of dried hyssop forgotten in her hands.

---

She had seen all of it.

Not the words. The square was too loud for words. But she had seen the woman laugh — that loud, easy, unashamed laugh — and she had seen the hand on his arm. She had seen him look down at whatever the woman had given him, and put it in his pocket.

And something went through her so fast and so ugly that she did not recognize it at first.

It was small. It was hot. It sat under her breastbone like a swallowed coal.

“Lyora?” The herb-seller was holding out her palm. “Lyora, dear, that’s four coppers.”

“Oh—” She fumbled for her purse. “Sorry. Sorry, yes.”

She paid. She walked home. She did not look toward the dye-merchant’s cart — very deliberately — the whole length of the square.

It was nothing. She knew it was nothing. A stranger, a wheel, a thank-you. By evening the woman would be gone down the river road and no one in Edenridge would ever see her again. Esdel had never so much as lingered at a girl’s gate in all the time he had lived under their roof. The village girls sighed over him — she knew they did — and he had never once seemed to notice.

She knew all of that.

The coal did not care what she knew.

---

He came home that evening at a proper hour, for once, because Reyn had thrown him out of the workshop. *Go home and eat something. You look like a fencepost.*

Lyora was at the hearth. She did not turn around.

“You’re back early,” she said.

“Reyn’s orders.”

“Mm.” The kettle began to tick with heat. “You were busy today.”

He paused in the doorway, unsure. “The market. Reyn needed nails.”

“I saw.”

There was something in the way she said it. Something he could not read. He looked at Arwen, who sat at the table sorting seeds, and Arwen looked very intently at the seeds.

“The good ones,” Esdel offered. “Not the rusted ones.”

Lyora made a small sound that was not quite a laugh.

He sat. She set out the bowls. She did not put his cup down close to his hand tonight; she set it at the far edge of the table, precisely, where he had to reach. He reached. She poured.

The tea was black.

Not strong. *Black.* Bitter and scorched, with a smell like a hearth gone out wrong. Esdel took one sip and set the cup down very carefully.

Lyora looked at the kettle. She had filled it — she was sure she had filled it — and then she had stood there glaring at it, thinking about a laugh and a blue-stained hand, and she had not—

The kettle was dry. The leaves had cooked to the bottom.

“Oh,” she said, very quietly. “Oh, *no*.”

Arwen put a hand over her mouth and was seized by a fit of coughing that did not sound at all like coughing.

“It’s fine,” Esdel said.

“It is *not* fine, it’s *burnt*—”

“I like it strong.”

“It isn’t *strong*, Esdel, it’s—” She snatched the cup from him, cheeks flaming, and emptied it out the window with considerably more force than was needed. “I’ll make more.”

“Lyora—”

“I’ll make *more*.”

She made more. She did not look at him while she did it. Arwen’s shoulders shook silently over her seeds for a good long while.

---

After supper, Arwen took him out into the garden.

“Come,” she said. “The rosemary’s gone wild. Old hands are slow.”

Her hands were not slow. He followed her anyway, out into the long violet dusk, where the cicadas were winding down and the air smelled of warm earth and crushed leaves. She put the shears into his hands and pointed.

“There. And there. Not that one.”

He cut where she pointed. The rosemary gave up its scent in green bursts, sharp and clean.

“You cut back what grows wild,” Arwen said, watching him, “so the root can hold.” She bent, slowly, and picked up a trimmed sprig, turning it in her fingers. “But cut too much, child — cut away everything that reaches — and you don’t have a plant anymore. You only have a stick.”

He paused with the shears open.

She did not look at him. She was looking at the sprig in her hand, as if they were only talking about rosemary. Perhaps they were.

“Good night, Esdel,” she said, and went in.

---

He lay awake a long time that night, staring at the beams.

He went back over the evening the way he went back over a broken mechanism — piece by piece, looking for the flaw. The market. The nails. *I saw.* The cup set just out of reach. The tea burnt black, because she had been standing at the kettle *thinking about*—

He sat up slowly in the dark.

He took the scrap of red cloth from his pocket and looked at it in the thin moonlight. A dye sample. A trader’s thank-you. It meant nothing. He had not thought of it again until now.

But she had.

The understanding came over him gently, and then all at once, like warm water rising. *She was jealous.* Lyora — steady, sensible Lyora, who scolded him about his cloak and laughed at his logic — had stood at the kettle burning the tea because a stranger had laughed at something he said about a wheel.

It should have made him happy. For one bright, treacherous heartbeat, it did.

Then it hurt, much worse than before.

Because he had already decided. He had decided at the well, with the water swinging steady in the pails. He had decided with every knot he tied on another man’s gift. There was a good man with a farm and a mother and a father learning to walk again, and a future waiting for her that would not vanish into sand. And there was him — a single name and nothing behind it, a body that healed when it should not, and dreams of a cliff above the sea.

He lay back down. He set the scrap of red cloth on the sill, where the wind could take it if it wanted.

Below him, through the floorboards, he could hear that she was not sleeping either. The creak of the bed. A long breath out. Once, the soft sound of her rising, and standing somewhere in the dark, and lying down again.

They lay awake, one above the other, in the same hot, still night — each listening to the other not sleep.
