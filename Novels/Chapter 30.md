# **Chapter 30**

The morning air was gentle — that kind of softness that carried the scent of dew and hearth smoke through the village lanes. From the window of the apothecary’s second floor, Esdel could see Edenridge already stirring: merchants unfurling their stalls, women sweeping porches, and the familiar chatter rising like birdsong.

A week had passed since the rain on the ridge. Lyora’s ankle had mended at last, and each night the lamp still burned, turned low, at the foot of the stairs.

After finishing his chores — restocking herbs, sweeping the back room, and feeding the apothecary’s resident cat (who pretended to dislike him but never left his side) — he decided to spend the day wandering the village. It was a rare morning of calm, neither weighed down by memory nor burdened by duty.

Outside, the village was alive. Sunlight spilled between the oak leaves, dappling the dirt paths. Children ran by, ribbons fluttering in their hands like little streaks of joy. Somewhere, a cart rumbled past, wooden wheels clattering along the stones. The distant clang of Reyn’s hammer echoed faintly from his workshop by the river bend, steady and grounding.

Esdel stopped at a few stalls, greeted by familiar smiles. A vegetable vendor pressed an apple into his hand “for strength,” and the baker — a burly man with flour permanently dusting his hair — insisted he try a new batch of sweet buns.

“They didn’t come out right,” the baker confessed with a laugh. “But waste not!”

Esdel took a bite. His face twisted immediately. “That’s… unexpectedly oceanic.”

The baker blinked. “Oceanic?”

“Salty,” Esdel clarified, coughing and trying not to offend. “Very… salty.”

There was a pause — and then a roar of laughter. “I must’ve switched the jars again!”

Esdel grinned despite the taste. “A bold experiment. Perhaps you’ve discovered a *new flavor revolution* — the future of bread!”

The crowd around them burst into laughter, clapping him on the shoulder. Even old Richard, who never smiled, was chuckling by the cart. Esdel laughed too, really laughed, the kind that escaped without permission.

It hit him then — he was laughing freely now. Not the polite smile he wore when everything felt distant, but the kind that left warmth in its wake.

Later, he wandered toward the village square, where a few men were struggling to repair a cart wheel. Naturally, he offered help, though his advice quickly spiraled into absurdity.

“You see,” he said seriously, “the axle’s friction coefficient is throwing off your load balance.”

The men blinked. One finally said, “I don’t know what you just said, but it sounds smart! Do it!”

Laughter exploded again. Even Esdel bent over, hands on his knees, tears in his eyes from how hard he was laughing.

“Friction coefficient, huh?” one man wheezed. “Next time the wheel falls off, we’ll just shout that at it!”

The laughter carried, echoing between the old stone walls. And for once, Esdel didn’t feel like a stranger among them.

As the afternoon waned, the village settled into its golden rhythm. The smell of bread baking drifted through the air, mingled with the sweetness of crushed grass. Esdel wiped sweat from his brow and turned toward home — only to spot a familiar figure in the market crowd.

Arwen was returning from her errands, basket in hand. Her shawl fluttered lightly in the breeze, her calm presence unmistakable.

“Ah, there you are, young man,” she greeted, voice warm and teasing. “I half-expected you’d gotten lost in the bakery again.”

“Not lost,” he said, smiling. “Held hostage by culinary innovation.”

That earned him a laugh. Before long, she joined the group at the square, effortlessly folding herself into their conversations. The villagers teased her too, calling her “the village sage,” to which she replied with such quick wit that even Esdel doubled over laughing.

It was strange and wonderful — to see her not as the wise, composed apothecary, but as someone full of humor and life. The sound of their laughter rose above the market noise, a rare music of belonging.

When the sun began to dip lower, painting the rooftops in honey and rust, Arwen suggested they walk home together. The air was cool, cicadas humming softly from the fields. The path curved gently up the hill toward the apothecary, the view behind them glowing in the warm afterlight.

Halfway there, Arwen began telling stories — soft, fond recollections that carried the rhythm of years gone by.

“Did you know,” she began, “when Lyora was little, she tried to make her first potion and used sugar instead of salt?”

Esdel turned to her, wide-eyed. “No.”

“Oh yes,” Arwen chuckled. “The entire cauldron exploded into sticky syrup. The walls sparkled for days. The house smelled like caramel for a week.”

Esdel burst into laughter. “That actually sounds impressive!”

“She also once tried to heal a stray cat’s wound with flower petals,” Arwen continued, her eyes dancing with mischief. “Ended up gluing the poor thing’s fur together. She cried for two days straight.”

Esdel was laughing so hard now he could barely walk straight. “That… that’s tragic heroism at its finest.”

Arwen smiled knowingly. “She’s always been that way — soft-hearted, stubborn, and hopelessly earnest.”

Their laughter slowed as they reached the turn of the hill — just in time to see Lyora walking toward them, a basket of groceries in her arms.

She blinked, surprised. “What’s going on?”

Arwen and Esdel froze. One glance between them was enough. Without a word, they both turned forward, walking with exaggerated innocence.

“…Grandma?” Lyora’s brow furrowed. “Esdel? Why are you two—”

Then she caught it — the whispered phrase Arwen couldn’t resist letting slip: “...the cat glue incident…”

Lyora’s face turned crimson. “Grandma! Stop! Please!”

Esdel nearly choked on his laughter, trying to cover his mouth. Arwen, delighted, could hardly breathe from laughing. “Oh, dear, you should’ve seen her face! Sticky as honey! Even her hair—”

“Grandmaaaa!”

Esdel’s laughter finally broke free, helpless and contagious. Lyora chased after them, half-scolding, half-laughing despite herself. Her embarrassment only made the moment livelier, brighter — as if the quiet, heavy air of the past days had lifted completely.

By the time they reached the top of the hill, the world was steeped in amber. The roofs below shimmered under the setting sun, and the fields stretched out like a painted dream.

They slowed, their steps in rhythm. Arwen still chuckled softly. Lyora pouted, trying to appear annoyed, though a smile tugged at the corner of her lips. Esdel walked beside them, caught between mirth and something gentler — something he didn’t dare name.

When they reached the apothecary gate, he stopped for a moment, watching them — the old woman with her quiet laughter, the young one still flushed from embarrassment. The light wrapped around them both like a blessing.

He smiled quietly to himself.

For the first time, he felt something he couldn’t quite name — a gentle, aching appreciation for the peace he’d found here. It wasn’t the roaring joy of adventure or the triumph of battle; it was something far rarer — *belonging*.

As the sun dipped beyond the horizon, the three of them walked home in warm, easy silence, the sky burning with a beauty that almost felt unreal. The cicadas sang their twilight hymn, the scent of earth rising from the rain-damp path, and Esdel thought — for the briefest, fragile moment — that even without his past, he could live like this.
