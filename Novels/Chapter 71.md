# **Chapter 71**

There was an hour in the Lián house that belonged to no one but the Count.

Esdel learned it on the third day. The household moved around it the way water moves around a stone. In the late afternoon, when the light went long and amber across the gardens and the peach blossoms began to close, Lady Wei Anlan would be brought to the small pavilion beside the pond. A maid would set out the tea things on the low table — white porcelain, a pot of the pale tea she liked, one cup. Then the maid would withdraw, and the Count would come.

He came every day. Never late. Never hurried. He would sit beside her on the cushions and talk to her softly — about the weather, the garden, some small thing at court — and while he talked, he would take a small bottle of dark glass from inside his coat, and unstopper it, and let fall into her tea, one by one, a few drops of something the color of amber.

Then he would stir it, and hand it to her, and she would drink.

Esdel watched it from the gallery on the third day, and the fourth, and the fifth. He was careful. He carried a basket of cuttings, as though he were only passing through to the kitchen garden, and he never stopped for long.

He watched her face. That was the thing he could not stop watching. How it changed as she drank — not suddenly, but slowly, the way a room changes when a curtain is drawn. The small crease between her brows, which she carried through the day like a question she had forgotten the words to, would smooth itself out. Her shoulders would come down. Her eyes would soften and turn toward the Count, and stay there, and fill with a kind of warm, sleepy gratitude, like a child who has been told a story.

And every day, when she had finished, she would set down the cup and take his hand in both of hers and hold it against her cheek.

And every day, the Count would let her.

---

“He never lets anyone else pour it,” Changli said.

They were in Esdel’s narrow room at the end of the east wing, with the door shut. Changli stood by the window, looking out at the wall. She had come to him, as she said, *for a headache powder*, and had closed the door behind her with her foot.

“Not the maids, not the steward. Not even his own physician, who brews it.” She turned her head. “Master Corwin Vale. A small grey man who lives in the west wing and never comes to meals. He makes it — the Count collects it from him himself, one bottle at a time — and the Count keeps it on his person. I have never once seen it leave his coat.”

“Then how,” said Esdel, “am I meant to see what’s in it?”

Changli smiled.

It was the smile he had seen on the night of the test, in the barracks at North Ridge — the one that had captivated, she said, countless men. It did not captivate him now either. But he found he understood it better. It was not seduction. It was a blade she kept sharp because she had never been allowed any other.

From her sleeve, she took a small bottle of dark glass and set it on the windowsill between them.

Esdel stared at it.

“He changes his coat for dinner,” Changli said. “Every night. Master Vale sends up a new bottle on the tenth day of each month, and the old one goes back to the west wing to be refilled. The old one had a little left in it. It was on the steward’s tray on the way back, among a great many other small things.” She shrugged. “Trays are very crowded in this house.”

“If he counts them—”

“Then he’ll find one missing, and blame the steward, who is a Reng man and deserves it.” She folded her hands in her sleeves again. “You have perhaps four days before anyone looks. Is it enough?”

He picked up the bottle. It was cool and heavy in his palm. When he held it to the light, perhaps a finger’s depth of amber liquid moved inside it, slow and thick, catching the window in a warm, beguiling glow.

“I don’t know,” he said honestly. “But I’ll find out.”

---

He had no laboratory. He made one.

He cleared the narrow table beneath the window. From the medicine chests he took what glass he had — two small flasks, a mortar, a set of vials, a copper dish meant for melting salves. From the kitchens, with a word to the cook and a coin for her trouble, he got a small brazier, a jar of lye soap, a jug of vinegar, a pinch of salt, a lump of chalk. From the gardener’s shed he took a coil of fine wire and a sheet of beaten tin.

Pei Lun, passing his door, stopped and stared.

“Are you,” he said, “making soup?”

“Something like that.”

“In your *bedroom*?”

“Go away, Pei.”

Pei went away. But that evening, without being asked, he came back with two mice in a covered basket, caught in the grain store — lively, brown, furious — and set it down by the door without a word.

Esdel looked at the basket. Then at Pei.

“You looked like you’d need them,” Pei said. “When the old surgeon at the Eastern Post tested a new salve, he’d try it on a rat first. Everyone knew that.” He shifted from foot to foot. “Is it — is it for the Lady? The stuff the Count gives her?”

Esdel said nothing.

“Right,” said Pei. “Didn’t ask. Didn’t see.” And he went away again, a little more slowly this time.

---

He began at midnight.

It was the kind of work that did not feel like work, at first. It felt like remembering. His hands knew what to do before he had thought it — how much to take, how to divide it, which question to ask first. *Divide the sample. Never use it all. Keep a portion untouched.* He did not know where the rules came from. They were simply there, clean and certain, the way the angle of a slope had been there when he first knelt beside the river at Edenridge.

He let three drops fall onto the tin sheet and set it over the brazier on the lowest heat. He watched the liquid thin, and bubble, and slowly give up its water in a thread of steam. What remained was a film — dark gold, sticky, faintly bitter on the air.

*A resin, then. Oil-bound. Not all of it will dissolve in water.*

He tried it. Water: cloudy, some of it settling. Spirit from his tincture flasks: clearer. Vinegar: a faint fizz, a sharpening of the smell. Lye: the gold went dark, almost brown, with a sudden sour-green edge that made his eyes sting.

He wrote everything down. Numbers. Colors. Smells. His handwriting in the candlelight was small and neat and not quite like anyone else’s — the letters formed a little strangely, as though the hand that made them had learned to write somewhere else entirely.

Near dawn, he held a thread of the residue to the candle flame on the end of the wire. It did not flare. It smoldered, slow, with a heavy sweet smoke that he did not like, and beneath the sweetness, very faint, something else. Something that did not smell like any plant he knew.

He sat back.

The window was going grey. Somewhere across the house, a door opened, and he heard the soft even tread of the Count going along the gallery toward the gardens, as he did every morning, to walk beneath the peach tree before the household woke.

Esdel pinched out the candle.

He looked at his notes for a long time — the neat, strange letters marching across the page — and then at the small bottle on the windowsill, where a finger’s depth of amber still glowed faintly in the dawn.

Then he got up, and opened the basket, and looked at the mice.

They looked back at him, bright-eyed and trembling, with no idea at all of what was coming.

He was sorry for that. He was, he found, very sorry. He sat with the basket in his lap for a while in the grey light, and let himself be sorry, because it seemed to him that someone ought to be.

Then he began.
