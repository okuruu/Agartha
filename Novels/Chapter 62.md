# **Chapter 62**

The air in the royal study was dense—a cloying, heavy atmosphere that came not from peace, but from the deep, paralyzing exhaustion of decision. It was the stillness of a battlefield after the final charge, where the living were too spent to celebrate victory or mourn loss.

The King, his face etched with shadows cast by days spent in deliberation, sat before the monumental slab of long **oak**. The desk, a relic passed down through three generations of sovereigns, was now a landscape of administrative wreckage: a terrain buried under tightly-rolled scrolls, heavy wax seals bearing the marks of lesser houses, and unopened letters from the furthest, most troublesome reaches of the realm. A single, beleaguered candle flickered beside him, its weak, trembling flame barely fighting back the encroaching darkness. It was a meager, lonely watchman against the vast, empty draft that swept in from the high, arched windows.

In his hand, slick with nervous perspiration, rested the **decree**. It was a compact sheaf of the finest vellum, its edges crisp and unforgiving. Scrawled within, in the careful, solemn hand of the Royal Scribe, were two names, now reduced to targets: **Lián Hengzhi**, once a pillar of the Kingdom.

And **Garron Reng**, the thief of House Reng — the single life the Crown would now accept in place of ten.

The justification for this necessary violence was woven into the ornate script beneath the names, a silk cord of officialdom meant to choke back public outcry: “For the **absolute safety** of the realm, for the unshakeable **stability** of the throne, and for the purity of the Sovereign’s lineage.”

He stared at the parchment for a period that felt like a lifetime measured in heartbeats. Every decree bore an inherent, formidable weight—the gravity of royal command—but this one… this one felt different. It was the weight of **betrayal dressed as necessity**, a poisonous medicine administered to save the body politic by sacrificing one of its most vital organs.

When he finally reached for the brass, lion-headed royal seal, his hand did not descend instantly. It lingered midair, hovering like a hawk assessing its final strike. The wax, a brilliant, terrifying **blood-red**, lay half-melted in a small, ivory crucible, waiting for him like a wound yet to close. He saw, not the words on the vellum, but the man: Lord Hengzhi—his steady, measured composure in council, his clear, unnervingly honest eyes, his **unimpeachable loyalty** that had once filled these very halls with reason and structural brilliance. Hengzhi had been the Kingdom’s architect, and now the King was demolishing his foundation.

The King’s breath came out slow, measured, and ragged, clouding the cold air. “This,” he whispered to the empty room, his voice barely disturbing the candlelight, “this is simply what **peace demands** in the aftermath of treachery.”

The seal finally descended, driving down into the yielding, hot mass. It struck with a dull, final sound—a quiet, sharp crackle of hot wax meeting cold brass, instantly and irrevocably sealing a man’s fate, and perhaps, the King’s own soul. He pulled the seal away, leaving the perfect, cold impression of the Royal Sigil, now colored in the hue of sacrifice.

Outside, the first faint pink glow of the nascent dawn was already coloring the eastern sky. The court attendants, standing guard in the silent halls, waited with a tense anticipation that transcended professional duty. Their faces were trained to neutrality, mask-like and unreadable, but their eyes, darting toward the heavy oak door, were alive with the furious, electric current of murmurs—rumors that would soon become tomorrow’s established, devastating truth.

By dusk of that same day, the decree, having been copied and dispatched with ruthless efficiency, had already spread like a chilling contagion through the capital and along the major trade arteries.

The formal charge was clear: The **House of Lián** was to be stripped entirely of its vast holdings and titles. Their prosperous seaports, their influential trading companies, and their meticulously mapped silk routes were to be placed “under the temporary supervision of the Crown”—or so the carefully drafted parchment claimed, for the benefit of public record.

But the truth, raw and dangerous, whispered differently behind the thick velvet drapes of the elite. The Grand Duke **Rowan Hale**, known for his terrifying efficiency and his bottomless ambition, had been granted direct and absolute **oversight**—a privilege of immense power reserved only for the most trusted, or the most feared, agents of the throne. With that single, royal signature, the immense, generational wealth of Lián House began a silent, calculated shift. Piece by excruciating piece, the Duke's agents—silent men dressed in the livery of the Crown—began funneling the assets into the Duke’s dominion.

Titles of nobility were immediately reassigned to Hale’s favored vassals. Contracts, some dating back a century, were ruthlessly re-drafted or nullified. Merchants who had built their fortunes alongside Lián were suddenly coerced into “temporary partnerships” with Hale’s own nascent syndicate. The paper trail was immense and meticulously constructed to look legal, yet every official document—every bill of sale, every ledger entry, every coerced agreement—bore the same, unmistakable secondary mark: Hale’s personal **sigil**, a silver griffin, gleaming like cold iron on the fresh wax.

And in the profound silence of that intricate, devastating transaction, **Aldric Reng** watched and smiled.

He stood at the side of the Duke’s sprawling, sunlit study, a stark contrast to the King’s candle-lit dungeon. Servants moved with hushed speed, carrying bundles of documents that represented centuries of Lián labor: thick stacks of asset lists, delicate trade maps highlighting protected sea lanes, and heavy, bound ledgers filled with numbers that dwarfed the treasury of many small kingdoms. Aldric’s expression was a masterpiece of control, a perfect, unreadable mask of weary civility.

“Eight-tenths, as agreed,” Rowan Hale stated, not bothering to glance up from the final parchment he was signing, his quill scratching sharply across the vellum. His voice was low, unhurried, imbued with the kind of absolute composure that, paradoxically, always made lesser men deeply uneasy. The words sealed the division: eight parts of the Lián fortune to the Crown’s “supervision”—which was to say, to Rowan Hale—and the remaining two to Aldric Reng.

Aldric inclined his head, a smooth, practiced gesture that conveyed respect without subservience. “The rest is of no consequence, Your Grace. I have no need for **crumbs** when I can secure the harvest.”

The Duke’s quill stopped midstroke, leaving a slight, dark blot of ink on the margin. He finally looked up, his pale blue eyes—eyes that seemed to catalog every vulnerability they encountered—meeting Aldric’s own. “You’re either too proud, Reng, or far, far too patient.”

Aldric allowed himself a faint, almost regretful smile. “Perhaps both, Your Grace. Pride is merely the shield one wears in court; patience is the blade one sharpens in the dark.”

Their eyes locked. For a brief, charged instant, the pretense of noble decorum dissolved. There was only the raw, shared understanding of **predators** who, though they might hunt the same immense prey, did so for utterly different motivations and with diverging, dangerous methods.

Rowan Hale leaned back in his high-backed chair, steepling his long, manicured fingers beneath his chin. “You got what you wanted, Reng. **Nine of your immediate kin** will live and see the winter. The House remains, though crippled and shorn of its influence. The King thinks you a loyal son who made the hardest sacrifice. The people will soon think you a brave man who rescued your family from the brink. And I—” His voice softened to a chilling intimacy. “I think you **dangerous**.”

Aldric gave a quiet, appreciative laugh, a sound that held no warmth whatsoever. “Dangerous men tend to be exceedingly useful, Your Grace.”

The Duke’s own smile was a cold, brief contraction of the lips that did not reach the coldness in his eyes. 

“Until they aren’t.”

Later that night, the air outside had turned to rain, and Aldric Reng stood perfectly still by the leaded window of his newly quieted estate. The sound of the downpour against the glass was a rhythmic, hypnotic drumming. He gazed out over the rain-washed, glistening streets of the capital. The iron-lantern lamps, fueled by whale oil, flickered weakly against the storm, their reflections dancing in the growing puddles like fragments of molten, corrupted gold.

He thought of his explicit promise to the Duke—eight-tenths of the value to Hale’s coffers. He had fulfilled the terms to the letter; the ledgers would prove his compliance, his sacrifice, his loyalty.

But there were always other, subtler ways to win the true game.

Aldric’s reflection stared back at him from the black mirror of the glass, its expression slowly twisting into a tight, satisfied smirk. “You can have your share of the coin, O Grand Duke,” he murmured to the ghost image, his voice barely audible over the rain. “I’ll take the **rest**—not in silver or gold, but in **consequence**.”

He turned away from the window. A single candle burned beside him, the wax guttering low, its flame bending and swaying with the draft that ghosted through the ancient window frames. Outside, the night deepened—quiet, watchful, and deeply, profoundly **conspiratorial**.

And in that silence, illuminated only by the struggling flame, Aldric Reng, the survivor, began the meticulous, slow weaving of his next, far more elaborate design.
