# **Chapter 7**

Early summer hung heavy already. The fields to the east had begun to crack and thirst, though the river ran just beyond the hill.

That was when Reyn Challon, the village engineer, came to the apothecary one morning with his sleeves rolled up, boots caked in dust, and a wide grin beneath his copper beard.

“Arwen, I’m borrowing your helper. I’ve got a problem the village needs fixed, and I hear this man has a head for odd ideas.”

Esdel did not move from the worktable. He had a sieve in one hand and a heap of dried calendula in front of him, and the orange of the petals had stained his fingertips.

“Apprentice,” he said mildly.

“What?”

“You said helper. I’ve been promoted.” He tipped the clean petals into a jar. “You let me use the good mortar.”

“Hm,” said Arwen, which settled it.

“Fine. I’m borrowing your *apprentice.*”

“The apprentice has calendula to finish, and a tincture to strain at noon, and every shelf to dust, because someone put the honey back without its lid.” He did not look at Lyora, who had become very interested in the ceiling. “The shop comes first. Mornings are hers. Always.” His voice was easy, but it did not bend. “Afternoons, I’m yours until the light goes.”

Reyn laughed, a big helpless laugh that rattled the lowest jars, and dragged out a stool to wait.

---

The fields on the outskirts were dying. The ground there was higher than the main irrigation lines, and the village’s water-guiding charms could no longer push the river uphill. “The last enchanter moved away three winters ago,” Reyn said. “The river doesn’t listen anymore.”

Esdel looked at the slope, at the way the river curved through the reeds. His mind worked like clockwork, measuring, imagining flow.

“Magic isn’t the only way water moves.” He picked a hollow stalk from a discarded pile. “Bamboo. It grows in abundance and it’s hollow. Cut and join it properly, and the pressure does the rest. Water doesn’t need permission to move. It only needs a path.”

They worked afternoons only. Every dawn he was at the shop, grinding and straining; every noon Reyn’s shadow appeared in the doorway, patient as a creditor. Lyora began calling him *the creditor.* Reyn began coming earlier each day, until Arwen put him to crushing rose hips so he would stop leaning on things.

When at last they opened the flow, the village gathered to watch. The water surged forward, clean and steady, through channels of hollowed bamboo like veins of living glass, and the cracked earth drank and darkened. No spells. No sigils. Just wood, gravity, and thought.

“By the gods,” said Reyn. “It *works.*”

Esdel wiped his brow. To him it had been simple. He smiled faintly, and as he watched the water ripple in strange concentric circles, his reflection wavered: his face shifting into something unfamiliar, his eyes a shade too bright.

He blinked. It was gone.

That evening, as he passed the stream under the fading light, the bamboo creaked softly in the wind, carrying water that glowed faintly under the moon, and a voice whispered through the current:

*“You were never meant to stay.”*

He turned sharply. There was no one there.

---

A week later the Chief called a gathering in the square. The villagers came with fruit and rice cakes and laughter.

“This man made the river listen,” said Reyn. “All I did was follow.”

The Chief rested a hand on Esdel’s shoulder. “You’ve given us trust again in our own hands.”

Esdel was not used to being thanked. He looked out over the faces until he found the one he wanted — silver braid, arms folded, standing at the back beside Lyora as though none of this concerned her.

“If you want to thank someone, thank Arwen. She fed me, and mended me, and has let me break only two of her jars. Every morning she teaches me something. Every afternoon I go out and try to look clever with what’s left over. I’m her apprentice. The bamboo is only what the apprentice does when he’s let out.”

Arwen did not unfold her arms. But her chin lifted, very slightly, and beside her Lyora ducked her head to hide a smile that got away from her anyway.

“Humble *and* funny,” said the Chief. “Arwen, where did you find him?”

“The caravan found him,” said Arwen. “I only kept him.”

---

Three weeks on, the Festival of Flow lit the night, held once a decade, and this year the village danced for the fields that had come back green. Lanterns swayed above the square, and the river carried hundreds of lights downstream like fireflies on a silver road. Reyn, a little red in the face, lifted a clay cup and shouted, “To the man who solved a three-year drought with *bamboo!*” and the crowd roared.

Esdel did not stay long on the bench. Lyora had set up the apothecary’s table under the great tree, and he went to earn his supper. A fisherman came first, neck burned red; Esdel reached for the aloe before Lyora could point. “Thin,” he murmured. “A burn wants air as much as it wants salve.”

“Why the calendula?”

“Aloe cools. Calendula mends. You asked me that yesterday.”

“I wanted to see if the answer had changed.”

“Herbs don’t change their minds.”

“People do. I was checking on you.”

The fisherman laughed so hard he forgot to flinch. Lyora pressed her lips together and did not laugh, which was, Esdel was learning, nearly the same thing.

Later she found him by the water with two cups of tea, and they sat on the old stone embankment while the festival sounds went distant behind the bamboo.

“Do you know what the villagers say about you? That you were sent here by the river gods.”

“I’m not sure the gods had anything to do with it.”

“I don’t know. Maybe they just wanted you to rest.” She watched the river. “You’ve changed how this village lives.”

“I didn’t do it alone.”

She hesitated. Her voice was almost a whisper. “You’ve changed how *we* live too.”

The words hung between them like a breath caught midair. He looked away first, at a lantern bumping against the bank. When he glanced back, she was looking at her tea, and her thumb was pressing very hard against the rim of the cup.

“I’m glad you came,” she said. She said it so quietly it might have been to the water. “I’m so glad. And I’m so—”

She stopped.

She never finished. She put the cup down and rose and said, “They’ll want the table packed,” and walked up the bank. He saw her at the top, under a lantern, lift the heel of her hand to her face once, quickly, and then go on.

Something long buried beneath sand and blood and pain had begun to *move.* Something gentle.

Something dangerous.

---

In the days after, the dark marks on his arm faded to pale traces. He told himself that was enough.

On a grey morning the woodcutter’s daughter came in with her shawl damp and her voice pulled thin. Her father had been burning two days and would not eat. Arwen listened, asked three short questions, and without looking up from her mortar said, “Esdel will make it.”

“I will?”

“You’ve watched me make it a dozen times. Willow bark. Go on.”

He set the small brass scale on the counter and waited for the pans to settle, the way Arwen did, as if the scale had to be allowed to breathe. He weighed the bark. Took two pieces away. “He’s a big man?” “Bigger than you.” He put one piece back. His fingers were steadier than his heart.

Arwen opened one packet, tipped it into her palm, weighed it with her eyes and then with the scale. The pans swung. Settled. She folded it closed.

“Tell her how.”

“Simmer it, don’t boil it. Give it warm with something in his stomach, even bread. Three times today, no more.” He smiled. “If the fever breaks tonight he’ll wake cross and hungry. That’s the good sign.”

“Why not boil it?” he asked, the moment she was gone.

“There it is.” Arwen went back to her mortar. “Boil it hard and it goes harsh, and what you want slips off with the steam. Slow is gentler. Most things are.”

He wrote it down word for word. *Most things are.*

“You didn’t look at me once, while you measured.”

“I was afraid if I looked at you I’d see I’d got it wrong.”

“You hadn’t.” The pestle turned. “This time.”

It was, from Arwen, very nearly a song.

That afternoon, Lyora came to Reyn’s workshop with bread and stew, pretending it was for convenience. “The woodcutter’s girl came back,” she said. “His fever broke in the night. He’s cross, and hungry, and says the tea was disgusting.”

Something warm and unfamiliar moved through his chest. “That’s the good sign,” he said, very quietly.

When she left, Reyn waited until she was out of sight before he whistled low. “The way you look at her when she leaves — like you’re afraid the wind might take her.”

Esdel tightened a bolt that did not need tightening.

“You can build water systems out of bamboo,” said Reyn, “but you can’t fix your own heartbeat.”

Outside, the sky had turned a deeper grey. He found himself looking at the path where she had gone. Something inside his chest drew tight — not pain, not fear, only *weight,* the kind that comes when a man realizes he has begun to want something fragile.

Something the world could easily take back.
