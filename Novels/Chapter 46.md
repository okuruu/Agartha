# **Chapter 46**

The stench inside the medical tent was suffocating. Sweat, blood, rotting flesh, and the sour tang of medicine that had failed to save too many. The moans of soldiers—half delirious, half begging—hung thick in the humid air.

Esdel stood among the chaos, his hands trembling slightly as he tied a bandage around a soldier’s arm. The feverish man muttered nonsense, eyes glassy, sweat dripping from his temples. The basin of water beside Esdel was clouded with filth.

Across the tent, a sudden crash silenced everything.

Halden had slammed his fist on the table so hard that glass bottles rattled and shattered, spilling precious tinctures over medical reports. His commanding face twisted in fury.

“Hundreds of patients—suffering, writhing in fever—and it was *poison* flowing from the same stream they drank from every day?!”

Benn swallowed hard, but before he could open his mouth, Halden’s glare fell on him.

“And you, Benn! You were supposed to ensure the safety of the water supplies. What were you doing—writing poetry instead of testing for rot?”

Benn lowered his head, forcing himself to stay calm. “I… did test it, sir. But the contamination source was deeper, near the cave—”

“*Deeper!*” Halden roared, cutting him off. “Everything is deeper, everything is *later*! Do you realize how many lives were lost because it took us days to figure out what poisoned them?”

Esdel tried to step in. “Sir, Benn found the cause. Without him, we wouldn’t even know—”

“And yet,” Halden snapped, “knowing doesn’t bring back the ones who died!”

The tent fell silent again, broken only by the labored breathing of patients. Esdel didn’t flinch when Halden turned away, pacing in frustration, his shadow long and trembling under the flickering lantern light.

When Halden finally spoke again, his voice was quieter, hoarse with exhaustion.

“...Just do your damn work. Fix what you can. That’s an order.”

Esdel and Benn bowed their heads slightly. “Yes, sir.”

And so, they worked.

Days blurred.

Morning and night meant nothing anymore—only the rhythm of pain, healing, and hope. Esdel barely slept. Benn looked like a ghost, his dark hair plastered to his forehead with sweat. Halden refused rest entirely, barking orders even when his voice broke mid-sentence.

Bandages, boiling water, disinfectant, crushed herbs—it became their world. Esdel’s fingers were cracked and green from mixing medicine. He watched life return to pale faces, watched men who were nearly gone open their eyes again.

Every life saved felt like defiance against the war itself.

And little by little, the tent grew quieter. The groans turned to snores. The dying turned to sleeping.

By the week’s end, the last patient was discharged, limping out of the tent with a shaky salute. For the first time in days, the tent stood still.

---

The stars hung low and clear, their light shimmering across the camp. The sounds of soldiers laughing faintly echoed from afar—a rare, fragile peace.

Esdel sat outside the tent, finally breathing air that didn’t smell like medicine. His shirt was still stained with old blood, but he didn’t care.

Behind him, Halden appeared carrying a pot of steaming coffee and a small basket of skewered meat. Benn followed, half-asleep on his feet, holding three cups.

“You two look like corpses,” Halden muttered, sitting down by the campfire Esdel had made earlier. “Might as well feed you before you actually become one.”

Esdel managed a small smirk. “If I’m to die, at least let me taste coffee first.”

“You’ll get more than that,” Halden said, pouring for each of them. “This is my private stash. If you tell anyone, I’ll make you scrub bedpans for a month.”

“Noted,” Benn said dryly, taking a sip. He instantly gagged. “Sir—this is *terrible!*”

Halden squinted at him. “It’s called *strong.* It’s how real men stay awake.”

Esdel chuckled. “You mean ‘stay awake and regret it’.”

“Quiet, boy,” Halden grunted, spearing a piece of meat and handing it over. “You’re lucky I’m feeding you after that herb incident.”

Esdel blinked. “The—oh, come on, that was weeks ago!”

“You mixed *antiseptic* with *sleeping tonic,* Esdel.”

“It was an experiment!”

“It was *attempted murder!*” Halden barked, though his eyes gleamed with amusement.

Benn nearly choked laughing. “I still remember the smell! The whole tent reeked like old vinegar and dead flowers!”

Esdel groaned, covering his face. “I hate both of you.”

Halden snorted. “And yet, here you sit, eating my meat and drinking my coffee. That’s gratitude for you.”

The three of them laughed—real laughter this time. The kind that bubbles up from exhaustion and shared misery.

Then Benn leaned back, staring at the sky. “Do you ever think,” he said softly, “what would happen if all this just… stopped? The war, the wounded, everything.”

Halden grunted. “I’d sleep for three months straight.”

Esdel smiled faintly. “I’d go back home. My village’s quiet. You can hear the wind in the wheat fields.”

“Sounds boring,” Benn said.

“It is,” Esdel replied, “but it’s peaceful.”

Halden looked at them both, his expression unreadable for a moment. Then he exhaled, slow and deep.

“You know,” he murmured, “when I first met you two, I thought the gods were punishing me. Two clumsy idiots—one too earnest, one too sarcastic. And now…”

“Now?” Esdel asked, grinning.

“Now I know I was right.”

They burst out laughing again, loud enough that a nearby guard shushed them. Benn threw a pebble at him.

For a long while after, they said nothing. The fire crackled softly, the stars glittered like distant embers, and a cool breeze swept through the camp.

Esdel broke the silence first.

“Halden… when do you think this war will end?”

Halden stared into the flames, his hand tightening around the cup. The orange glow lit the deep lines of his face—each one carved by years of seeing too much death.

“When men stop finding new ways to destroy,” he said quietly. “When they learn that bleeding doesn’t buy peace.”

No one spoke after that. The fire hissed, the night breathed. The three of them sat in silence, the weight of his words settling deep in their hearts.

Above them, the stars shone unchanging—cold, distant witnesses to a world that never stopped breaking.
