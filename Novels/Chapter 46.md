# **Chapter 46**

The herald came to Edenridge on the hottest day of the summer, when the wheat in the high fields had just begun to turn.

He came up the river road at noon on a tall bay horse, with two soldiers in the Crown’s blue behind him and a leather case across his saddle, and the village watched him come from doorways, and over fences, and through the gaps in shutters. No herald had come to Edenridge in living memory. Old Richard said there had been one in his grandfather’s time, to announce the old King’s coronation, and that it had rained.

It did not rain now. The sun stood white over the square, and the cicadas sawed in the willows, and the herald took a scroll from his case and nailed it to the great tree with four iron nails.

---

Lyora was in the apothecary when she heard the shrine bell, rung quick and uneven, the way a child rings a bell to call people in from the fields. She put down the pestle.

Arwen was at the window with a basket of mending she had not touched. Her hands lay folded on it, trembling.

“Someone’s come,” said Arwen.

They went down together. Lyora gave her grandmother her arm on the lane, the way she did now, without either of them mentioning it. The whole village was going the same way. Reyn fell in beside them wiping his hands on a rag. Eldric came up from the southern fields with his father limping behind him.

The herald read in a clear, bored, carrying voice, the voice of a man who had read the same words in forty squares in forty days. A general. A traitor. A dragon, and a stolen child, and a gate forced in a storm. It was a story from very far away, and the village listened and did not understand most of it. Somewhere at the back someone whispered *what’s a Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon* and someone else whispered *shh*.

Lyora was not really listening. She was watching Arwen’s face go pale in the heat. She was thinking she should take her grandmother home, out of the sun. She was thinking about the feverfew that needed hanging.

“*—and the apothecary Esdel,*” read the herald, “*of no family, formerly of the Third Logistic Company and the medical division of the North Ridge post, late of the village of Edenridge.*”

---

The square went silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The kind of silence that has weight, that falls over a crowd all at once the way snow falls off a roof. The cicadas still sang in the willows. But under the great tree, where the wishing ribbons had hung the summer before, no one made a sound.

*A reward of five thousand gold crowns. One thousand for any of her accomplices. Living or dead.* The herald rolled up his copy and looked around with mild, sweating impatience, as though waiting for someone to ask him a question so he could leave.

No one looked at Lyora. That was the thing she would remember. The whole village stood in the white heat, and every one of them very carefully did not look at the apothecary’s granddaughter. They looked at the herald, and the scroll, and the dust.

She stood very still with her grandmother’s arm through hers. She did not faint. She did not cry out. She felt her face grow wet, only that, quietly, without any sound, as though it were a thing happening to someone else, and she did not lift her hand to it because that would have been to admit it. She felt Arwen’s arm begin to shake against hers.

*Alive,* she thought.

It was the only word she could hold. It came up through her out of nowhere, clear and steady, like a lamp being lit in a dark room. *Living or dead.* They did not know which. They were looking for him. Which meant he was not in a grave or a ditch or a burned field. Which meant he was somewhere.

*Alive.*

---

Reyn moved first.

He stepped out of the crowd, a big dusty man with a copper beard, and walked up to the great tree and read the scroll slowly, all the way down, the way he read a plan for a bridge. Then he turned around.

“That’s a lie,” he said.

The herald blinked at him.

“That man lived in this village one spring and one summer. He fixed our fences. He carried our water. He made the river climb that hill when every enchanter in the valley said it couldn’t be done.” He pointed up the slope, where the bamboo channels caught the sunlight like threads of glass. “He never raised his voice. He never took a coin he didn’t earn. Whatever that paper says he did, it’s a lie.”

“I only read them, friend,” said the herald. “I don’t write them.”

“Then take a message back to whoever does. Tell them Edenridge doesn’t know anything about any traitor.” Reyn’s eyes went round the silent faces and rested, a moment, on the apothecary’s granddaughter, pale as paper. “We only knew an apothecary,” he said. “And he was a good man.”

*Was.*

It went into Lyora like a cold needle, the little word, and she saw Reyn hear it too, a half-second late, and shut his mouth. Nobody corrected it. She did not look at him. She understood that he had not meant it, that it was the way people speak of a man they have already begun to mourn, and she thought that if one more person said *was* she would not be able to stand.

Then the silence changed. It stood up straight, like a field of wheat after the wind has passed. The herald shrugged and rode out of the square with his two soldiers behind him and did not look back.

The scroll stayed nailed to the great tree. No one took it down. No one touched it.

---

That evening, two men came up the river road on foot and took a room at the inn. They wore plain grey and said they were wool buyers from the eastern valleys. They paid in good silver. Reyn mentioned them at the kitchen door: two wool buyers, in high summer, which was a strange time to be buying wool.

In the morning they did not go to look at any sheep. They sat on the bench outside the inn, in the shade of the eaves, and watched the lane that ran up from the square toward the old house at the edge of the village with ivy on its walls.

---

Lyora walked home with her grandmother on her arm, slowly, up the lane in the heat. Neither of them spoke. She put Arwen in her chair in the good light. She made tea, and put the cup in her grandmother’s trembling hands and folded them around it, the way Arwen had once folded hers.

Then she went into the kitchen and began, because her hands wanted something, to knead the evening bread. The flour rose in the heat. When her face was wet again she wiped it with the back of her floury wrist and told the empty kitchen it was the steam. There was no steam. She kept kneading. Her hands went on, and on, long after the dough was done.

When it was dark, she got up. She took the lamp from the shelf, and filled it, and trimmed the wick, and struck the flint once, steadily. She carried it to the foot of the stairs and set it on the second step, where it belonged.

She stood looking at the small gold flame. At the third stair above it, silent now, that had not creaked in a year.

*Late of the village of Edenridge.*

He was alive. She sat down on the second step beside the lamp and held on to that with both hands, as she had at the kitchen table in the spring, when the soldier said *alive and working.*

And then, because the house was very quiet and the night was very long, the other thing came.

She could feel it coming the way you feel a fever coming: a coldness first, at the back of the neck. And then the herald’s bored voice again in her head. *The woman Lián Xueyao. Formerly styled Maiden of the Heavenly Dragon.* And after it, in a list with fifteen soldiers, his name.

Not hers. *Hers.*

She had heard the whisperers in the square, afterward. They said the Maiden rode a dragon made of gold. They said she had golden eyes. They said she was the most beautiful woman in the Kingdom, and the most terrible, and that she had fled into the night with a handful of her father’s soldiers, and with him. That he had drawn his sword for her at some river against a Count. That he had given up his name and his whole life and gone with her into the dark.

For her.

It was not jealousy. Lyora knew what jealousy was. She had been jealous once, of a woman with blue hands who had only asked about a wheel, and it had been hot and small and shameful, and it had burned the tea. This was not that. This was cold. This was much worse.

*A year. More than a year.* What was a spring and a summer in a valley, against a storm, and a gate, and a woman with golden eyes who needed him? He had carried her down off the ridge once, in the rain. How many people had he carried since? He had held her on the floor at the foot of these stairs until she could breathe again. How many had he held since, in how many tents?

*He’ll remember us,* she told herself. *Of course he will.*

And a small cold voice answered: *He doesn’t remember anything. That’s the whole of him. He forgot everyone he ever loved once already. What makes you think you’re any different?*

She put her hands over her ears, as if that would help, as if it had come from outside. She did not make a sound. The tears simply ran, and ran, down her chin and onto her knees, and she let them, because there was no one to turn her face from. The hours went by, and the lamp burned down, and she did not turn it up.

She had turned it up every night for a year. She did not now. She watched the small flame shrink toward the brass and thought: *who am I keeping it for? For a man who went with her. For a man who’s gone.*

She let it gutter. It went down to a blue bead on the wick. It flickered. It almost went out.

Almost.

---

It was the watchers that saved it.

She did not think of them until nearly dawn, until the window over the shop went grey and she heard, very faintly, a door at the bottom of the lane. Footsteps. The creak of the bench under the eaves as two men sat down to begin their first day of watching the apothecary’s door.

She lifted her head.

*Late of the village of Edenridge.*

They had not written *late of the capital*, or *of the Northern Front*, or *of the General’s household*. Out of a whole year at the war, out of every place he had ever stood, they had chosen the name of one small village at the edge of the desert. And then they had sent two men to sit on a bench and watch one door.

You do not set a snare where the rabbit will never come.

They thought he would come here. Of every road in the world, the one they thought he would take was the road to this door. They knew him better than she had, tonight.

And he would know it too, wherever he was. He would hear the herald in some other square, and hear the name of the village read out after his own, and understand, the way he understood everything, all at once, like the slope of a field, that there were men waiting at the bottom of her lane. And he would not come. Not because he had forgotten. Because he had not.

He would stay away. For her. He would keep his promise to come back by never coming.

That was what finally broke her. Not the decree. Not *living or dead*. That she had sat all night on the stairs and let the lamp die down, doubting him, while somewhere in the dark he was running *away* from this door with everything in him, so that nothing would ever follow him to it.

She bent over her knees and pressed her face into her apron and made no sound at all. Her shoulders shook, and shook, and there was nothing else.

Arwen did not call out. But after a long time Lyora heard the old woman get up, slowly, painfully, and cross the room. She felt her stop in the doorway. And then the slow bending of old joints, the hand on the wall, and Arwen lowered herself onto the stair beside her, the first step, because the second was taken by the lamp, and sat there in the grey with her trembling hands in her lap.

She did not say anything. After a while one of the hands crept across and found Lyora’s wet one, and held it. It shook. They both shook. Between them the little blue bead stood on its wick.

Neither of them spoke. It was the longest conversation they had ever had.

At last Lyora lifted her head and wiped her face, once, with the heel of her hand.

She reached out and turned the lamp up. Slowly. Carefully. The bead caught, and grew, and steadied, and turned gold, and she kept turning, past where she had ever turned it before, until the small flame stood up bright and tall on the second stair and threw its light all the way across the dark shop to the door, and under it, and out into the lane.

Where two men in grey on a bench outside the inn could see it, if they liked. Let them.

It stayed lit.

It never went that low again.
