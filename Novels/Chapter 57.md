# **Chapter 57**

Later that morning, the wind came softly through the Lián gardens, a hesitant caress that moved pale, silk curtains and stirred the silver-leaved willows bowing beside the black mirror of the koi pond. It carried a layered perfume of high summer: sun-warmed earth underfoot, the heady sweetness of ripening peaches, and the sweet, lingering smoke of incense curling from the sheltered veranda. It was a perfect, fragile peace.

Lady Wei Anlan sat beneath the wide canopy of the peach tree, whose branches hung heavy with the first blushing fruit of the season. Her hand rested protectively on the small, warm body of Lián Yueming, who was nestled in her lap, his cheeks flushed like the ripening peaches above them. Anlan's eyes were half-lidded with a drowsy, sun-warmed contentment. Every so often, she would lean her face close to the child’s smooth crown to hum a quiet melody—a tender, wordless thread that seemed to stitch the morning silence into a cohesive calm. The only sounds were the soft lapping of water against stone and the distant, rhythmic *snip, snip* of shears.

Across the meticulously kept garden, Lord Lián Hengzhi was immersed in his own silent world. He knelt over the precise ranks of potted orchids, the ones Xueyao, their eldest, had planted before she left. His hands, gnarled but steady, carefully removed dead sheaths and guided the fragile blooms onto thin bamboo supports.

The action was methodical, demanding all his attention—a necessary distraction. He did not have to look to know Anlan was there, a presence of light and warmth. She and the boy were the tether to this home, the shield against the distant chaos. Every time he clipped a spent leaf, Hengzhi thought of Xueyao. She had tended these *Cymbidiums* with a soldier’s exactitude, demanding perfection from every root and node, a stark contrast to the beautiful destruction she now oversaw on the northern plains.

He picked up a narrow, jade-hued bloom, its petals pristine. It felt absurd, the delicacy of this flower against the vast, brutal reality of the frontlines. He wondered, as he did every morning, if the wind that carried the scent of orchid here also carried the scent of rain and desperation to his daughter.

Hengzhi finished the row and finally rose, his knees cracking softly in protest. The moment he turned toward his wife, the quiet facade of his concentration dropped, and the weariness settled again beneath his eyes.

Anlan looked up, her expression shifting instantly from motherly repose to spousal recognition. She understood that look—the deep-seated anxiety that no amount of pruning or incense could soothe.

“The weather is fine for the new sprouts,” she said, her voice low, acknowledging his turn without needing to name the reason for his troubled gaze.

Hengzhi walked slowly toward her, wiping his hands on a soft cloth. “They are strong. The roots run deep,” he replied, a deliberate double meaning in his words. He was speaking not just of the orchids, but of the strength of their family, the necessity of deep roots when the winds of war threatened to tear everything away.

He sat beside them, settling into the tranquility of the peach tree’s shade. For a long moment, they did not speak, simply existing in the quiet harmony they had built. Yueming stirred, his small hand fisting the silk of Anlan’s sleeve. Hengzhi reached out and gently ran his thumb over the child's tiny ear, a gesture of profound, silent gratitude for the peace that still remained.

“She would scold me for trimming too much,” Hengzhi murmured, examining the petals that fell onto the soil. “Xueyao said orchids thrive best when left to find their own shape.”

Anlan’s laughter drifted across the garden — light, musical, and tinged with sadness. “She always argued with you about the smallest things,” she said. “And somehow, she was always right.”

He smiled faintly. “She is her mother’s daughter.”

A small whimper escaped from Yueming, breaking the moment. Anlan brushed her son’s forehead. The infant’s lashes fluttered open, catching the morning sun. Hengzhi rested a hand over hers.

They stayed there a while longer, listening to the bamboo rustle and the water’s faint lapping. Everything in the Lián estate moved with rhythm — servants passing in hushed efficiency, the cooks preparing herbal broth, the calligrapher grinding ink for the afternoon prayers.

It was a good day. Almost too good.

Then — the soft clatter of sandals against stone.

An attendant hurried through the corridor, bowing deeply as he approached. “My lord,” he said breathlessly, “a messenger has arrived from the western gate. He bears the seal of Lady Anlan’s family — the Wei of Nanyue.”

Anlan’s brow lifted slightly, though her tone remained serene. “From my family? That’s unexpected.”

The messenger entered soon after — a middle-aged man, courteous and careful. His robes were clean, but his eyes darted too quickly across the courtyard before he knelt. He held out a scroll, its silk tie marked by the Wei sigil — the crane over the moon.

“By order of Lady Wei’s kin,” the man said, “a new vessel from the Nanyue fleet has docked at the western harbor. They send their filial respects and a gift to honor your daughter’s service in the war. They ask that Lord Lián Hengzhi himself come to inspect the ship tomorrow at dawn, as the vessel carries delicate trade goods and family correspondence.”

Hengzhi accepted the scroll. “A vessel from Nanyue?”

“Yes, my lord.”

He untied the ribbon and read. The words were warm, familiar — written in the practiced brush of Wei kinship, full of polite endearments and gratitude. Yet, as he reread it, a small hesitation flickered behind his calm eyes.

“Strange,” he murmured, half to himself. “My brother-in-law rarely writes so suddenly. But perhaps it’s about the new maritime tax.”

Anlan tilted her head, sensing the subtle tension. “Shall I prepare something to send in return? A small token, perhaps?”

“Of course,” Hengzhi said. “You’ll make it finer than I ever could.”

That night, the manor glowed with lamplight. Servants wrapped parcels of dried tea and rare silks, while Anlan prepared a jade carving meant as a goodwill offering. Hengzhi oversaw the ledgers, instructing his attendants to verify each crate for the morning’s visit.

None noticed the faint, uneasy glance exchanged between two servants — one of them the very man Aldric had bribed weeks before.

As the moon rose, the household fell into quiet. Hengzhi stood on the balcony overlooking the western mountains, fingers laced behind his back.

“She’ll be home soon,” he whispered to the night. “And when she comes back… may all this peace still remain.”

But even as he said it, a gust of wind swept through the garden — cold and heavy, as if something vast and invisible had begun to stir beyond the horizon.
