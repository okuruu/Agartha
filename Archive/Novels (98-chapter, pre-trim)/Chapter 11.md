# **Chapter 11**

A week passed since the river had been tamed.

Morning mist still hung over the fields when Esdel and Reyn stood side by side, watching the water run clean through the bamboo lines they had built. The system hummed softly, alive yet unenchanted — its rhythm steady as breath.

It was early. The shop would want him within the hour, and he had come out only to look. His fingers still smelled faintly of the feverfew he had ground by lamplight before he left.

For the farmers, it was nothing short of a miracle.

Where there had once been dry, cracked soil, green shoots now rose in perfect rows. The elders gathered near the banks, murmuring prayers of thanks, though none could quite agree on *whom* to thank — the gods, the river, or the quiet man who had simply shown it a path.

That afternoon, the Village Chief, an elderly man with silver hair tied neatly behind his head, called for a small gathering in the square. The villagers came bearing fruits, rice cakes, and laughter.

Reyn was the first to speak, voice bright with pride.

“Never thought I’d see water climb a hill without a spell,” he said, grinning toward Esdel. “But this man here—he made the river listen. All I did was follow his lead.”

A ripple of cheers followed.

The Chief nodded deeply, resting his hand on Esdel’s shoulder.  
“You’ve given more than a solution, Esdel. You’ve given us trust again — in our own hands.”

Esdel said nothing for a moment. He wasn’t used to being thanked — not like this.

He bowed his head slightly. “I only did what anyone could have done.”

“Maybe,” the Chief chuckled, “but no one *did* — until you came.”

Laughter rippled again, light as wind through wheat.

When it quieted, Esdel looked out over the faces until he found the one he wanted. Silver braid. Arms folded. Standing at the back beside Lyora as though none of this concerned her in the least.

“If you want to thank someone,” he said, “thank Arwen.”

Heads turned.

“She fed me, and mended me, and has let me break only two of her jars.” A few people laughed. “Every morning she teaches me something. Every afternoon I go out and try to look clever with whatever’s left over.” He smiled. “I’m her apprentice. The bamboo is just what the apprentice does when he’s let out.”

The laughter was warmer this time.

Arwen did not unfold her arms. But her chin lifted, very slightly, and beside her Lyora ducked her head to hide a smile that got away from her anyway.

The Chief shook his head, amused. “Humble *and* funny. Arwen, where did you find him?”

“The caravan found him,” Arwen said. “I only kept him.”

---

From that day onward, more people in the village began to change around him.

The farmers waved when he passed — “Morning, Esdel!” — and the children ran to greet him, their hands muddy from play. Some offered him roasted chestnuts, others small trinkets carved from wood. At first, he tried to refuse, but they insisted until his pockets were full.

The merchants at the market began asking for his thoughts — on tools, measurements, small ways to make life easier. It was the kind of respect that required no ceremony, no worship — only trust built quietly, day after day.

And every time his name was spoken, Lyora would smile.

At the apothecary, she’d beam as villagers came to thank him, setting down their baskets of herbs or jars of honey as gifts.

Some came for remedies instead and asked, a little shyly, whether the apprentice could fetch it. He always could. He read each label twice, the way Arwen had taught him, before he put a jar in anyone’s hands, and told them how much and how often in a voice so unhurried that people who had come in worried tended to leave less so.

“You’ve become the village’s favorite topic,” she teased one afternoon, setting a cup of tea beside him. “Even Grandmother mentioned your water pipes when she spoke to the shrine-keeper. Said they might last longer than the old spells.”

Esdel chuckled softly, brushing dust from his hands. “If they do, it’s only because nature prefers order.”

Lyora tilted her head, eyes bright with that quiet curiosity that always caught him off guard. “Order,” she echoed. “You talk about things like they’re alive.”

“Sometimes they are,” he said with a faint smile. “You just have to listen long enough.”

She laughed, light and melodic — the kind of laugh that filled the room without effort. “Then maybe that’s your real gift. You make the world want to talk back.”

He blinked, unsure how to answer that. So he didn’t. Instead, he lifted his tea and let her words settle like warmth in his chest.

---

That night, the village glowed beneath a calm sky. Lanterns hung from doorframes, their light swaying with the wind. The bamboo channels gleamed faintly in the moonlight, threading through the village like veins of gold.

Esdel stood by the riverbank alone, watching his reflection ripple in the water’s slow current. The sound of laughter carried faintly from the distance — Lyora’s voice among them, soft and alive.

He hadn’t meant to stay. He hadn’t meant to belong.  
And yet, as he looked out over the fields — green and full, the village lights dancing like fireflies — something within him loosened.

For the first time since he could remember, the silence inside his chest didn’t feel hollow.

It felt… gentle.  
Like water beneath the bamboo.
