# **Chapter 10**

The early summer air in Edenridge already hung heavy with heat. The fields to the east had begun to dry — their soil cracked and thirsty despite the river flowing just beyond the hill.

That was when Reyn Challon, the village engineer, arrived at the apothecary one morning — sleeves rolled up, boots caked in dust, and a wide grin beneath his copper beard.

“Arwen, I’m borrowing your helper,” he announced cheerfully, nodding toward Esdel. “I’ve got a problem the village needs fixed, and from what I hear, this man’s got a head for odd ideas.”

Arwen merely looked up from her mortar and smiled faintly. “Odd ideas have built more than they’ve broken, Reyn. Take him.”

Esdel did not move from the worktable.

He had a sieve in one hand and a heap of dried calendula in front of him, half of it picked clean of stems, and the orange of the petals had stained his fingertips. The room smelled of it, bright and resinous, with the steam of a fever tincture ticking on the hearth behind him.

“Apprentice,” he said mildly.

Reyn blinked. “What?”

“You said helper.” Esdel tipped the clean petals into a jar. “I’ve been promoted.”

Arwen did not look up. “Have you.”

“You let me use the good mortar.”

“Hm,” said Arwen, which settled it.

Reyn looked from one to the other. “Fine. I’m borrowing your *apprentice*.”

“The apprentice has calendula to finish,” Esdel said, “and that tincture to strain at noon, and every shelf to dust, because someone put the honey back without its lid.” He did not look at Lyora, who had become very interested in the ceiling. “The shop comes first. Mornings are hers. Always.” He met Reyn’s eyes, and his voice was easy, but it did not bend. “Afternoons, I’m yours until the light goes.”

Reyn turned to Arwen as if appealing to a higher court.

Arwen’s pestle went on, unhurried. “You heard him.”

“Half a man,” Reyn said, throwing up his hands.

“The better half,” Esdel said. “The morning half mostly yawns.”

Reyn tried to hold his frown and lost. He laughed, a big, helpless laugh that shook the jars on the lowest shelf, and dragged a stool out from the wall to sit on while he waited.

At noon, when the tincture had been strained and stoppered and labeled in his small, strange hand, Esdel washed the orange from his fingers.

And so, Esdel followed.

---

The project was simple in purpose, impossible in practice — or so Reyn believed.

The fields on Edenridge’s outskirts were dying. The ground there was higher than the main irrigation lines, and the village’s small enchantment wells could no longer sustain the flow uphill. 

“We’ve always used water-guiding charms,” Reyn explained as they walked. “But the last enchanter moved away three winters ago. The river doesn’t listen anymore.”

Esdel listened silently, scanning the terrain — the slope of the land, the way the river curved lazily through reeds. His mind worked like clockwork — measuring distance, imagining flow.

“Magic,” he said after a long pause, “isn’t the only way water moves.”

Reyn blinked, caught off guard. “You have something else in mind?”

“Bamboo,” Esdel said simply, kneeling by the riverbank. He picked up a long, hollow stalk from a discarded pile nearby and ran his fingers along its smooth surface. “It grows in abundance, and it’s hollow. If we cut and join it properly, we can make a channel system to redirect flow. The pressure will take care of the rest.”

Reyn frowned, trying to picture it. “You’re saying… to *carry* the river through these? Without enchantments?”

Esdel nodded. “Water doesn’t need permission to move. It only needs a path.”

The engineer stared at him, incredulous at first — then thoughtful. “Well,” he said finally, scratching his beard. “I’ve never met a man who talks like a scholar and works like an assistant apothecary. Let’s see if your path holds.”

---

They worked for days under the sun.

Afternoons only. Every dawn Esdel was at the shop before the light, grinding and sorting and straining, his notebook open beside the mortar; every noon, Reyn’s shadow appeared in the doorway, patient as a creditor. He began calling it *collecting my half*. Lyora began calling Reyn *the creditor*. Reyn pretended to be wounded and came earlier each day, until Arwen put him to work crushing rose hips so he would stop leaning on things.

In the evenings Esdel came home sunburned and smelling of cut bamboo, and before he ate he would ask what had come through the door that day — a cough, a burn, a child with a splinter — and what Arwen had given for it, and why. Then he wrote it down.

Together, they split bamboo stalks, burned their interiors clean, and connected them with resin and cloth binding. Reyn’s apprentices watched curiously, whispering among themselves as Esdel calculated angles and adjusted inclines by hand — his movements methodical, his reasoning effortless.

At first, the engineer doubted. Then, he began to listen.

There was something hypnotic about Esdel’s focus — the way he knelt by the river, tracing its edge with his hand, speaking softly as if it might answer. He didn’t rush. He didn’t guess. He *understood.*

And when at last they opened the flow, the village gathered to watch.

The water surged forward — clean and steady — running through the bamboo channels like veins of living glass. It curved around the slope, reaching the farthest fields where the soil had long turned to dust. The earth drank eagerly, darkening with life once more.

No spells. No sigils. Just wood, gravity, and thought.

For a moment, no one spoke.

Then Reyn let out a stunned laugh, hands on his hips. “By the gods… it *works.* It actually works!”

The villagers cheered, rushing to the edges of the fields as the first shoots of green broke through the soil. Some called it a miracle. Others whispered it was luck.

Reyn only stared at the bamboo channels, his voice low, awed.  
“So, it can be done without magic…”

Esdel wiped the sweat from his brow, blinking against the sun. He didn’t understand their wonder. To him, it had been simple. Logical. The kind of solution that should have always existed.

He smiled faintly, though his eyes lingered on the water’s surface — watching as it rippled in strange, concentric circles.

For just a heartbeat, his reflection wavered — his face shifting into something unfamiliar, his eyes a shade too bright.

He blinked, and it was gone.

---

That evening, Reyn clapped him on the back as they walked home. “You’re something else, Esdel. I don’t know what place you came from, but it’s one I’d like to see.”

Esdel chuckled softly, though his mind was far away. “I’m not sure it’s a place worth seeing.”

Reyn arched a brow. “Maybe not. But whatever it taught you — keep it close. This village needs more men who think like you.”

The words lingered long after they parted ways.

As Esdel passed by the stream under the fading light, the air grew oddly still. The bamboo channels creaked softly in the wind, carrying water that glowed faintly under the moon.

And somewhere beneath that gentle sound — so soft it might’ve been imagined — a voice whispered through the current:

*“You were never meant to stay.”*

Esdel turned sharply, but there was no one there.

Only the sound of water…  
flowing endlessly through the bamboo veins he had built.
