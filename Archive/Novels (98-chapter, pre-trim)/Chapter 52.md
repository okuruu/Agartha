# **Chapter 52**

The rain had not yet begun, but the clouds pressed low upon the palace spires, swollen with thunder, the air so heavy it felt like the world itself was holding its breath.

Inside the grand hall, silence was a living thing. The marble floors gleamed like a frozen lake beneath the pale glow of the chandeliers. Courtiers stood shoulder to shoulder — nobles, envoys, ministers — their whispers having long since died into the hush of waiting. The red carpets that ran to the dais of the throne seemed to pulse faintly beneath the torchlight, as though blood itself had seeped through the seams.

At the center of it all knelt a man — gaunt, trembling, his velvet sleeves smeared with the dust of travel and the sweat of dread. His lips quivered as he spoke, though the words barely left his throat.

“I beg Your Majesty for mercy,” he said, voice cracking like a snapped bowstring. “It was not greed— only desperation— I meant to return it, every coin—”

The King remained still upon his throne.  
He was not a man of large movements. His strength was quieter — a stillness that drained the courage from others. He sat with his chin resting against his hand, the faint gleam of the crown dimmed beneath the candlelight.

When he finally rose, the sound echoed — boots shifting, breaths held. Even the guards at the doors seemed to draw straighter, as though the air itself bowed.

“You,” the King said, descending one step, his voice low but carrying, like iron dragged across stone. “Stole from the crown in a time of war. From the fund that feeds my soldiers, you took what was not yours. How much?”

The man swallowed hard. “T-Twenty thousand, Your Majesty. Only that. Out of a hundred thousand—”

“Only?”  
 The single word cut through him like frost.

The King took another step forward, shadows tracing the edge of his cloak. “Twenty thousand lives could be fed with what you call *only*. Twenty thousand hands could wield blades, hold shields, keep the enemy from our gates.”

He looked down at the trembling figure. “Instead, you fattened your comforts while others starved. The crown does not forgive treachery disguised as indulgence.”

The silence that followed was unbearable.  
Even the crackle of the torches seemed to retreat.

Then came the sentence.

“Ten of your bloodline shall answer for your theft. The price of one man’s greed is the shame of his House.”

A single breath passed — then the hall erupted.

Gasps, mutters, disbelief — a hundred voices breaking into the same stunned tremor. “Ten—?” “He can’t—!” “That’s murder!” “House Reng—!”

The guards tensed at the uproar, but the King did not move. His eyes wandered through the crowd until they fell upon one man who had not spoken, not even shifted.

Count Aldric Reng.

He stood a few paces from the dais — tall, silver-haired, draped in black and ash-gray. His face was carved in the likeness of calm, his hands clasped lightly behind his back. But beneath that calm ran an undercurrent of fury so profound it could not be named.

When the accused cousin broke down — sobbing, begging, clutching at the guards’ boots as they seized him — Aldric did not flinch. Not even when the man screamed his name.

“Aldric! Aldric, help me—!”

The Count’s gaze remained fixed ahead. He neither acknowledged the plea nor denied it. He simply waited for the noise to end.

When it did, he stepped forward. Each stride precise, deliberate — the kind that could silence a room without force.

He bowed low before the throne. “Your Majesty,” he said softly. His voice was like polished glass — smooth, measured, dangerous in its restraint. “House Reng accepts the decree. The will of the crown is law.”

He straightened, eyes lifting — steady, clear, unreadable.

“But,” he continued, “permit me to say — the greed of one fool does not define the blood of an entire House. We shall bear this punishment as you command. Yet know that the Reng line stands loyal still, and shall not falter.”

The hall held its breath.

The King regarded him in silence for a long time, studying the Count’s composed expression, the faint quiver of emotion buried deep within his stillness. Then, with a faint, almost imperceptible nod, he said, “Your poise honors your name, Count Reng. See that it remains so. A single crack, and the rest will crumble.”

Aldric bowed again. “As His Majesty commands.”

His words were perfect.  
His tone was flawless.  
And yet, as he rose, his jaw tightened — only for a heartbeat — before he smoothed it away.

The court murmured again. Some whispered admiration, others pity. But Aldric neither heard nor cared. His thoughts roared too loudly for that — fury, humiliation, cold and bright like molten silver.

The cousin was dragged away, his cries fading down the corridor.  
The King returned to his seat, dismissing the crowd with a faint motion of his hand.

When the nobles began to file out, Aldric turned sharply, cloak whispering behind him. His face was still a portrait of grace — but his eyes had gone dark, his silence sharper than any curse.

That night, the storm finally came.

The thunder rolled over the palace rooftops, lightning flashing against the tall stained windows of the throne room. The chamber was empty save for the King and the Prime Minister.

The flames in the hearth guttered under the draft, their glow throwing long shadows across the marble.

The Prime Minister approached quietly, his footsteps echoing on the polished floor. “Your Majesty,” he began, bowing deeply. “The court is… unsettled. To demand the deaths of ten from a noble house— it will stir more than whispers.”

The King did not turn. His hands were clasped behind his back, gaze fixed on the window, where the rain drew silver lines across the glass.

“It is meant to,” he said softly. “If fear spreads, so will obedience. Loyalty is a plant that grows best in the shade of dread.”

The minister hesitated. “Then this… punishment… is tied to the succession?”

At that, the King’s reflection smiled faintly in the glass. “The crown must pass cleanly, without cracks or stains. My son will not inherit weakness. The nobles must learn again the price of disobedience — even when it hides beneath silk.”

He turned then, his expression illuminated briefly by the flicker of the hearth. “Before one builds, one must let the walls crumble.”

The Prime Minister’s voice dropped lower. “Then may I ask, Your Majesty… what exactly do you plan to do?”

The King regarded him for a long moment, his eyes like the storm outside — calm at the surface, but restless beneath.

“I will do nothing,” he said at last. “Only touch the surface of still water. The ripples will do the rest.”

The Prime Minister’s breath caught. “Your Majesty—”

But the King only smiled — a thin, knowing curve of the lips — as the thunder cracked overhead.

The rain hammered the palace, drowning every other sound.

The hearth flared, then dimmed, leaving only the faint glow upon the King’s crown — glinting like a promise, or a warning — while the storm raged on outside, relentless and wild, washing the kingdom clean of its illusions.
