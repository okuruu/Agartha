# **Chapter 75**

She did not stop. That was the thing the capital did not understand about her. They thought the garden had been the end of it — a madwoman’s outburst, a scene, a scandal to be whispered over for a season and then forgotten. They did not know that Lián Xueyao had never in her life retreated from a field she had chosen, and did not intend to begin now.

She petitioned the royal college of physicians for a hearing.

It took eleven days. Eleven days of letters, and waiting, and letters. Eleven days of Lady Anlan drinking her tea in the pavilion every afternoon while the Count sat beside her, and never once looked toward the east wing. Then the summons came, sealed in black wax. The college would hear the General’s evidence in the matter of the Lady Wei Anlan’s tonic, at the tenth hour, in the Hall of Remedies.

“They’ll listen,” said Pei Lun, the morning of it, with the fierce certainty of the very young. “They’re physicians. It’s their *job* to listen.”

Han Ruo, beside him, said nothing at all. He only looked at Esdel over the boy’s head. And Esdel, who had seen that look before — on soldiers at the edge of an ambush, on Halden over a wound he knew would not close — said nothing either.

---

The Hall of Remedies was cold. That was the first thing Esdel noticed. A long, high room of pale stone, with tall narrow windows that let in a grey, even light, and shelves along every wall packed with books and jars and the preserved things of old anatomies floating in yellow spirit. It smelled of vinegar and dust. At the far end, on a raised dais behind a long table of black wood, sat seven men in the black caps of the royal college.

In the center sat Grand Physician Morrell. He was old, and very thin, with a face like folded parchment and hands that did not move at all when he spoke.

The Count was not there. He had sent word, through his steward, that he could not bear to hear his wife’s name spoken in such a place, and trusted entirely to the wisdom of the college. In his stead sat Master Corwin Vale — a small grey man with small grey eyes and ink on his fingers, who folded his hands in his lap and looked at the floor.

The General stood before the dais in the dark coat of her rank. Behind her, one pace to the left, stood Esdel. On the table before them, laid out on a white cloth, were his notes, his vials, the tin sheet black with residue, and a small covered basket.

“You may begin,” said Grand Physician Morrell.

---

Esdel had told it well. He would think so afterward, lying awake — that whatever else had happened, he had told it well. He had stood before the seven black caps and explained, plainly and in order, everything he had done. The division of the sample. The heat and the solvents. The lye and the green-sour edge. The mice, and the doses, and the slackness of their limbs. The root. *Greyveil.* He had shown them the vial of separated resin, and the page of numbers, and the brown mouse drowsing in its basket.

He did not speak of the second thing. The gold oil. The hand the mouse followed. He had no proof of it that these men would accept, and he knew it.

When he finished, the hall was very quiet.

Grand Physician Morrell looked at him for a long time. Then he looked down at the notes on the table. He picked up one page — the one with the numbers — and held it a little way from his face, the way old men do, and read it.

“Your hand is unusual,” he said.

Esdel said nothing.

“Where did you study, apothecary?”

“I—” He stopped. “I didn’t.”

A murmur along the dais.

“You didn’t,” Morrell repeated.

“I learned in a village apothecary. And at the front, in the field tents. From a surgeon called Halden, at the Eastern Post.”

“Halden.” One of the other black caps, a younger man, snorted softly. “The butcher of the Eastern Post. I’ve read his reports. He boils his knives in *vinegar*.”

“Mortality in his tent fell by half,” said Esdel, “when we did.”

The younger physician’s face went red. Morrell raised one hand, very slightly, and he subsided.

“And before the village,” said Morrell. “Before the front. Where are you from? What is your family? Who taught you to write numbers in that fashion?” He tapped the page with one dry finger. “I have been fifty years in this college. I have never seen a man divide a sample so, or reason from a mouse to a woman. Where did you learn it?”

Esdel looked at him.

“I don’t remember,” he said.

The silence that followed was of a different kind.

“You don’t remember,” said Grand Physician Morrell.

“I woke in the desert a year ago. With no memory. I knew my name. Nothing else.”

He heard it land. He felt it — the whole room shifting, the way a floor shifts under a man who has stepped onto rotten boards. He saw the black caps glance at one another along the dais. He saw Master Vale, in his chair to the side, lift his small grey eyes from the floor for the first time, and look at Esdel with a kind of mild, interested pity.

“I see,” said Morrell.

He set the page down on the table. He squared its edges, neatly, against the others.

“General,” he said, turning to Xueyao, “the college is grateful for your concern for your mother’s health. It is natural in a daughter. And it is natural, after a long service in the field, to be — watchful. To see threats. The college understands this.”

“The college,” said Xueyao, very quietly, “has not tested the tonic.”

“The college has no need to. The tonic is brewed to the college’s own receipt for nervous exhaustion, which contains, as it happens, a small and entirely safe measure of greyveil. We have used it for forty years.” Morrell folded his hands. “Your apothecary has found greyveil in it. We are not surprised. It is there. It is meant to be there.”

“In such amounts? Every day? For a year?”

“In amounts judged proper by her physician, who is a licensed master of this college.” Morrell inclined his head toward Vale, who bowed modestly. “And not by a man who does not know where he was born.”

Xueyao did not move. “Test it,” she said. “Test it here. You have the bottle. You have his notes. Do what he did, and see if you find what he found.”

“We will not.”

“*Why?*”

And for the first time, Grand Physician Morrell’s thin face showed something. It was not anger. It was something tireder and more final than anger — the look of an old man who has sat at many tables, and knows exactly which hands have been dealt, and which have not.

“Because, General,” he said, “you have brought before this college an accusation against a peer of the realm — a man of proven loyalty, who bled for the Crown on the docks of the western harbor — on the word of a nameless field apothecary with no training, no family, and no past, who reasons from mice. And because your mother, whom you claim to protect, has herself, before forty witnesses, begged you to stop.” He paused. “A general may command soldiers. She may not command truth.”

The words fell into the cold room and lay there.

“The college finds no cause,” said Grand Physician Morrell. “This hearing is closed.”

---

There was one more thing.

It happened as they were leaving — as the black caps were rising from the dais, and Xueyao had turned, and Esdel was gathering the notes from the table with hands that were perfectly steady and felt like someone else’s. A door opened at the side of the hall, and two men came in.

One was a tall man in plain dark green. The other was an older man in black who walked a pace behind him.

Every physician on the dais bowed. Prince Ardbert did not seem to see them. He was looking at Xueyao.

“Grand Physician,” he said. “I heard the college was sitting on this matter. I had hoped to—”

“Ardbert.”

The voice came from the doorway behind him. It was not loud. It did not need to be.

The King stood there.

He had come without heralds. He was dressed simply, in grey, and he looked older than Esdel had imagined — tired, and thin, the lines deep around his eyes. He did not come into the room. He stood in the doorway with his hands clasped behind his back, and looked at his son.

That was all. He did not speak another word. He only looked.

And the Crown Prince of the realm stood in the middle of the Hall of Remedies with his mouth half-open on a sentence, and Esdel watched the sentence die in him. Watched him look at his father, and then at Xueyao, and then at the floor. Watched his hands, at his sides, close slowly into fists and open again.

“Forgive me, Grand Physician,” said Prince Ardbert, very quietly. “I interrupted.”

He bowed to the dais. He did not look at Xueyao again. He turned and walked back to the door, and his father stood aside to let him pass, and then followed him out, and the door closed behind them both.

---

They walked back through the palace in silence. Down the long corridors of pale stone. Across the second terrace, where courtiers strolling in the spring sun saw them coming and turned, as one, to admire the view in the other direction. Down the broad white steps to the carriage.

At the foot of the steps, Xueyao stopped.

She stood looking out over the terraces of the city, falling away beneath them toward the river — ten thousand roofs of green and blue tile, shining in the light. Her face was perfectly calm.

“No one,” she said.

Esdel, a step behind her, waited.

“Not one,” said the General of the Northern Front. “Not the college. Not the judges. Not a single one of the men who ate at my father’s table every week for twenty years. Not—” She stopped. “No one stood beside me.”

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say. He only stood where he was, one pace behind and to the left, where he had stood all morning in the cold hall — and after a moment she turned her head, very slightly, and looked at him standing there.

She looked for a long moment. Something moved in her face, and was put away.

Then she got into the carriage.
