# **Chapter 72**

It took three days.

He divided the mice. One would have the tonic, in a drop of water, twice a day — a dose scaled down as carefully as he could reckon it. The other would have only water. He fed them both from his own hand. He kept them in separate baskets beneath the table, and watched.

On the first day, the dosed mouse slowed.

It was not dramatic. It did not stagger or fall. It simply stopped running. It sat in the corner of its basket with its eyes half-closed, breathing slow and even, and when he reached in to lift it, it did not bite or struggle. Its little limbs hung loose in his palm, warm and heavy, as though it had forgotten how to hold them up. After an hour, it came back — a little. It groomed its face. It ate.

Then the second dose, and the same again.

He wrote it down.

*A paralytic. Mild. The muscles loosen before the mind does. Duration: an hour, perhaps two, the effect lingering after. Tolerance: not yet visible.*

That part he recognized. He was almost sure of it. It was the dark-green sour edge that came up under the lye, the faint numbness it had left on the tip of his tongue when he had — against every sensible instinct — touched the smallest trace to it. There was a root that grew in shaded valleys, grey-leaved, with pale drooping flowers; field surgeons used it sometimes, in tiny amounts, to calm a man who was thrashing too hard to be stitched. *Greyveil*, Halden had called it, in the tent. *A kiss of it will quiet a man. A mouthful will quiet him forever.*

He had seen it once before that, too. In a shaded hollow in the forest above Edenridge, on a green morning, with a basket on his arm — and a hand on his sleeve, stopping him before he could reach for it. *We don’t use that,* Lyora had said. *Not ever.* She had not told him its name. He had written it in his notebook anyway, that night, with a small drawing of the leaf, and beneath it, in his own strange hand: *Ask Arwen why.*

He never had.

Given slowly, every day, over months, it would do more than quiet. It would soften the will. Dull the hands. And, a little at a time, weaken the heart that had to keep beating through it.

That was poison enough. That was murder, done gently.

It was not all of it.

---

It was on the second day that he noticed the other thing.

He fed the dosed mouse its evening drop, as he had the others, from the tip of his finger. He set it back in the basket. And when he withdrew his hand, the mouse followed it.

Slowly. Drowsily. But it came to the edge of the basket and pressed its nose against the wicker, toward his hand, and stayed there.

He moved his hand to the left. The mouse moved left.

He sat back and looked at it.

The other mouse — the one that had only water — was in the far corner of its basket, as far from him as it could get, glaring at him with bright suspicious eyes, the way any sensible mouse glares at the giant who has caught it.

He tried it again in the morning. He fed the dosed mouse its drop himself, from his own fingertip, as he had from the start. Then he gave the basket to Pei and went out — to the market, to the stillroom, anywhere — and left the boy to sit with it through the long hours while the drop did its work. Pei talked to it. He could not help himself; he talked to everything. He named it, and told it about his sisters, and let it sleep in the hollow of his palm.

At dusk Esdel came back. And when both men held out their hands to the basket, the mouse went — not to Esdel, who had fed it every drop for two days, but to Pei.

It pressed its small body against Pei’s knuckles and closed its eyes.

“That’s—” Pei said, and stopped. His face had gone strange. “That’s not right. Is it?”

“No,” said Esdel. “It isn’t.”

---

He spent the last night trying to find what it was.

He could not. That was the truth, and he made himself write it down in plain letters, because he did not believe in hiding the truth from a page. He tried every test he knew, and several he invented at the table as he went. He separated the resin into what would dissolve in spirit and what would not, and found the paralytic in one part and the other thing in the second — a thin, clear gold oil that did nothing he expected. It did not react to lye or vinegar or chalk. It did not burn, quite. When he warmed it, it gave off that faint, sweet smell that was not like any plant — and when he held a drop of it up to the candle, it caught the light in a way that made him uneasy, a slow, shifting glimmer, as though something moved in it that was not the flame.

He did not know it. He knew, with the same clean certainty that told him how to divide a sample, that he had never known it. Whatever it was, it had not grown in a valley.

He sat with his notes in the grey dawn, and looked at them, and understood what the mice had told him.

*The paralytic softens her. Makes her pliant. Dulls her grief, and her anger, and her memory, and her will to resist.*

*And the second thing — whatever it is — binds her to whoever is there while it works.*

Not to the tonic. Not even to the hand that poured it. To the face in front of her. To whoever sat beside her, every day, in the amber hour, and spoke to her softly, and was the only thing in the room while the gold went through her blood.

He thought of Lady Anlan in the pavilion, holding the Count’s hand against her cheek. The warm, grateful, sleepy look in her eyes.

He thought of the General, beneath the peach tree, weeping without a sound.

---

She came to his room herself, that morning, when Changli told her he had finished. She stood inside the door in her plain dark coat with her arms folded and looked at the table — the flasks, the tin sheet black with residue, the pages of small strange writing — and then at the two baskets beneath it.

“Tell me,” she said.

He told her.

He told her about the resin, and the lye, and the flame. He told her about the root. *Greyveil.* A paralytic, given slowly, every day, in doses small enough to pass for a sleeping draught and large enough, over months, to dull a woman’s will and weaken her heart. He told her it was poison. He told her that, given long enough, it would kill.

He did not tell her about the mouse that followed Pei’s hand.

He had meant to. He had rehearsed the words in the grey hour before she came. *There is something else. It binds her to him. Her feelings for him may not be—* But when she stood there in the doorway with her arms folded tight across her chest, holding herself together by the elbows, he looked at her and thought of her shoulders shaking beneath the peach tree. Of her mother turning away from her like a flower toward the light.

He thought: if I tell her that her mother’s love is a thing that was brewed in a bottle, what will she have left?

And beneath that, quieter, a voice he recognized. A voice in a moonlit garden a world away. *Then don’t remember what hurts. Just remember what’s here.*

He said, “That’s what I found.”

Xueyao was very still.

“Poison,” she said.

“Yes.”

“He is killing her.”

“Slowly. Yes.” He made himself meet her eyes. “I can’t tell you whether he means to finish it. Only that, if nothing changes, it will finish itself.”

For a long moment she said nothing at all. Her face did not move. But he watched something happen behind her eyes — something vast and cold settle into place, the way ice settles over a river in a single night.

“Thank you, Apothecary,” she said.

She turned to go. At the door, she paused, with her hand on the frame, and spoke without looking back.

“Is that all of it?”

His heart stopped, very briefly, and began again.

“That’s all I can prove,” he said.

It was true. It was, he told himself, true. He could not prove the other thing. He had two mice and a feeling. He had a gold oil he could not name.

Xueyao nodded once and went out.

Changli did not. She had been standing by the window all this time, silent. Now she turned her head and looked at him — a long, steady, unreadable look — and he knew that she had heard it. The half-second before he answered. The place where the truth had turned aside.

She did not say anything. She only looked at the basket beneath the table, where the dosed mouse was pressed against the wicker, drowsily, toward the door where Pei had gone out.

Then she followed her mistress.

Esdel sat alone at the table for a long while after. He looked at his notes. Then he took the last page — the one with the mice, and the gold oil, and the word *binds* — and folded it in half, and in half again, and put it inside his coat with the letters he did not send.
