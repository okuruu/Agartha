# **Chapter 51**

The banners of House Reng still hung from the great hall — deep red, embroidered with the silver crest of the serpent devouring its own tail. Once, it had been a mark of cunning and survival. Tonight, it looked more like a curse.

The court was restless. Servants whispered as they passed; torches guttered in their sconces as if unwilling to burn here.

Count **Aldric Reng** sat at the head of the long table, the sleeves of his coat rolled up, his fingers stained with ink and rage. He had spent the morning drafting letters to half the kingdom — appeals, denials, reassurances. All the elegant words of a man watching his world unravel.

The war had bled everyone dry. House Reng had contributed more soldiers than most — five battalions and a fleet of supply wagons — and the King had rewarded them well. A hundred thousand gold crowns, entrusted to fund armor, grain, and medicine.  
But rumor traveled faster than truth ever could.

Now, the rumor had a name.

The heavy doors creaked open. A soldier entered, saluted, then removed his helm with shaking hands. His face was pale, his voice thin.  
“My lord… I bring word from the capital.”

Aldric didn’t look up immediately. His quill scratched one last line before it snapped under his grip.

“Speak,” he said.

The soldier swallowed. “It’s about Lord Garron Reng — your cousin. The treasury’s auditors found records. He’s been diverting funds from the royal stipend… for personal use. Nearly twenty thousand gold, my lord.”

The room seemed to still around them.

Aldric’s pen rolled off the table and struck the stone floor. “You’re certain?”

“Yes, my lord. The King’s inspector himself sealed the report. They say the gold was laundered through merchants in Iskar and Redmere. Lavish purchases — horses, jewels, silks.”

The soldier hesitated, then added quietly, “The King is… enraged. He intends to make an example.”

Aldric stood slowly. His chair scraped the marble behind him, echoing like a blade drawn from its sheath.

For a long time, he said nothing. The firelight flickered over his sharp features — the fine silver hair at his temple, the controlled fury burning in his eyes.

“So,” he murmured, “the fool thought he could drink from the treasury while men choke on mud and iron.”

He turned toward the window, where the banners outside shuddered in the wind. “Tell me, soldier — do they know it was his hand alone?”

The soldier lowered his gaze. “The report names him, yes… but the court whispers speak of more. The King’s council suspects others in House Reng knew.”

Aldric’s jaw tightened. “Of course they do.”

He crossed the room with measured steps, boots striking the stone in rhythm with his pulse. The serpent on the banners swayed, its silver thread catching the light — a symbol of eternity, and of destruction.

“When one Reng sins,” he said bitterly, “the whole bloodline rots with him.”

He stopped before the soldier, voice lowering to a tremor. “And what of the punishment?”

“The King’s decree is not yet public,” the man answered. “But the word among the guards is that His Majesty means to execute ten members of your House. To make an example.”

For a heartbeat, silence. Then a sharp crack — the sound of Aldric’s goblet shattering against the floor.

The wine bled into the cracks like spilled blood.

“Ten,” Aldric whispered, the word trembling with disbelief. “Ten for one man’s greed.”

The soldier dared not speak.

Aldric turned, his composure unraveling in slow, deliberate threads. The polished veneer of the courtly politician slipped, revealing the steel beneath. His voice, when it came again, was low and venomous.

“Find him,” he said. “Garron. Find the coward before the King does.”

The soldier hesitated. “My lord, the capital’s guard—”

“I said *find him!*” Aldric’s voice rose — not a shout, but a cut. His hand trembled only once before he steadied it. “Before the King makes the rest of us pay for his indulgence.”

He turned away, gripping the edge of the table until his knuckles went white.

Outside, thunder rolled over the hills — slow and distant. The banners of House Reng twisted in the wind, their silver serpent biting its tail.

And Count Aldric Reng, silver-tongued and unbowed, stared at the storm as if he could command it to strike his own kin first.
