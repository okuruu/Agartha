# **Chapter 54**

The gilded doors of the throne hall opened with a low groan, echoing through the high pillars and marble floors. Afternoon light spilled in through the long stained windows, casting gold and crimson hues across the grand chamber. Incense curled gently in the air.

Prince **Ardbert** stepped inside, his boots silent against the polished floor. His dark hair gleamed faintly in the glow, and his blue eyes—steady, calm, unflinching—caught the reflection of the banners draped from the ceiling. He stopped several steps before the throne, lowered his head slightly, and bowed.

“**Your Majesty.**” His voice was clear, respectful, and measured.

The King leaned back upon his seat. The years had not dimmed his presence; his gaze alone could command silence, yet it softened as it fell upon his son. “You’ve returned,” he said slowly, as if tasting the words. “I heard of your success in the famine lands.”

Ardbert raised his head. “It was... difficult at first, Your Majesty.” His tone carried no boast, only honesty. “The village had lost more than crops—they had lost hope. Grain meant nothing to those who no longer believed in tomorrow.”

The King’s eyes narrowed slightly, intrigued. “And yet you revived them.”

“I tried,” Ardbert replied. “I began by speaking with them, walking among them. I shared their meals—what little they had. I invited the nearby doctors to help, but they were reluctant at first, fearing the plague would spread. I couldn’t order them. So, I worked with them instead.”

A faint smile touched the King’s lips. “With them?”

“Yes,” Ardbert said quietly. “If I ask others to serve, I must first serve myself.”

Behind him, **Khalid**, the prince’s aide and most trusted companion, bowed his head respectfully but dared to speak. “Your Majesty, the prince worked tirelessly for weeks. Even the soldiers begged him to rest, but he refused. I told him he need not go that far—he could have sent orders from the capital.”

Ardbert’s gaze flickered toward Khalid, half amused, half fond. “You did say that. And yet, if I am to lead, I must understand what hunger feels like, what fear sounds like in a mother’s cry.”

Silence filled the hall. The sound of the wind brushing against the palace spires was faint, but clear.

Then, the King laughed—low and proud. “Three months,” he murmured. “Three months, and the famine was gone. The farmers spoke of you with tears in their eyes. The people cheer your name in the markets, and the nobles, I hear, now mention you with less envy and more... caution.”

Ardbert bowed again, humbly. “Their praise belongs to those who worked beside me. I was merely their voice.”

The King studied him quietly. “You have your mother’s heart,” he said finally, almost to himself. Then louder, “Go, rest. You have earned it.”

Ardbert nodded. “Yes, Your Majesty.”

He turned to leave, but just before he reached the doorway, the King called softly, “Ardbert.”

The prince paused.

The King said nothing more—only smiled. His expression, quiet yet full of pride, spoke the rest.

Ardbert bowed one last time, his chest warm, then exited the hall.

---

Outside, the sunlight poured through the open corridors, illuminating the kingdom’s vast courtyard. The prince walked slowly, his mind calm, though still carrying the echoes of his father’s tone.

Khalid followed at a measured distance, hands clasped behind his back. The man’s face, lined from years of loyalty and wisdom, softened as he watched Ardbert walk ahead.

As they reached the outer balcony overlooking the capital, the view stretched before them like a painted tapestry—villages nestled among green fields, rivers glittering under the sun, the hum of life rising faintly from below.

Ardbert rested his hands on the stone railing, his eyes tracing the horizon. “It’s strange, Khalid,” he murmured, his voice gentle. “When I was young, I thought the kingdom was only what I saw from these walls. But when I saw those villages... those people, I realized how small I’ve been all this time.”

Khalid smiled faintly. “And yet, Your Highness, even the smallest seed can feed a nation if it grows with patience.”

The prince chuckled. “That sounds like something my tutor would say.”

“I stole it from him,” Khalid said with a grin, his tone breaking the solemn air just enough to draw a quiet laugh from the prince.

They stood side by side for a while, letting the wind play with their hair and the faint songs of distant bells drift up from the city.

Then Khalid spoke again, softer this time. “When I heard His Majesty praise you today, I felt something I haven’t in years, Your Highness. Pride. You’ve grown into a fine young man. The people see it... and so does he.”

Ardbert glanced at him, his expression full of warmth. “You’ve been with me since I was barely tall enough to reach the stables’ latch. I should be the one thanking you. You’ve never left my side.”

Khalid bowed his head, his voice carrying a quiet laugh. “And I never will, Your Highness.”

The prince looked back toward the horizon, the golden fields swaying in the wind. “Then let us keep walking forward,” he said. “There’s still much to learn.”

The sun was setting by then, casting long shadows across the palace gardens. For a brief moment, Ardbert stood perfectly still, bathed in light—neither prince nor ruler, but simply a man who believed that compassion could mend a broken kingdom.

And beside him, Khalid smiled—knowing that perhaps, for once, the future did not seem so heavy.
