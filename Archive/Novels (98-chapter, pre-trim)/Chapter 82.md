# **Chapter 82**

Summer came early to Edenridge that year, and it came in green.

The rains had been good. The river ran full and slow between its willows, and the bamboo channels hummed night and day, carrying the water up to the high fields, where the young wheat stood so thick and bright that the old farmers shook their heads and said they had not seen its like since they were boys. The peaches were setting on the trees behind the Chief’s house. The cicadas came back in a single night, as they always did, and the whole valley filled with their long, sawing, drowsy song.

It was a year since the market day. A year since the storm on the ridge.

Lyora did not count that one. She did not have to. It came up out of the ground by itself, the way the mint came up between the stones.

---

Eldric’s mother came to the apothecary on a hot morning in the first week of summer.

She did not come for medicine. Lyora knew it the moment she saw her in the doorway — a small, weathered woman with her good shawl on and her hands folded tight in front of her, and the particular stillness of someone who has practiced what she means to say many times on the walk down the lane.

Arwen knew it too. She looked up from her mortar, and then at Lyora, and then rose, with the slow dignity of her years, and said, “Come in, Hessa. Sit. I’ll make tea.”

So they sat, the three of them, at the kitchen table, in the green light that came through the ivy at the windows. Arwen made tea. Hessa did not drink it. She sat with her hands around the cup and looked at Lyora, and her tired face was very kind, and very sad, and very determined.

“I asked him to wait a year,” she said. “Last summer. Eldric. He came to me and asked me to send the go-between away, and I did, because he asked, and because his father was still on his crutch, and because—” She stopped. “Because I saw how it was. I’m not a fool.”

Lyora looked down at her own hands.

“It’s been a year,” said Hessa gently. “A whole year, girl. And no word. Not a letter. Not a—” She stopped again. “I’m not saying anything unkind. I know you were fond of him. We all were. He was a good lad. Mended my gate. But the war is a long way off, and it takes men and doesn’t give them back, and you—” Her voice softened. “You’re not getting any younger. Nor is he. My Eldric. He’s thirty next spring. He wants a family. He wants — he’s never said it, but I’m his mother, I know. He wants *you*. He always has.”

The cicadas sawed in the ivy. Arwen said nothing at all.

“He won’t ask you again,” said Hessa. “He’s too proud, and too good, and he thinks it would be unfair. So I’m asking. I’m asking you to think on it. That’s all. Just to think.” She reached across the table and patted Lyora’s hand, awkwardly, the way a woman does who is not used to patting anyone. “You’d be happy with him. You know you would. He’d never leave you. Not ever. Not for anything in the world.”

Then she drank her tea, all of it, in one long swallow — and thanked Arwen, and got up, and went out into the green morning with her good shawl around her shoulders, and did not look back.

Lyora sat at the table for a long time.

Arwen did not say anything. She took Hessa’s empty cup and washed it, and dried it, and put it on the shelf. Then she came back and sat down across from her granddaughter, and folded her hands on the table.

They were trembling.

Lyora saw it. She had been seeing it for weeks — since the spring, perhaps. A fine unsteadiness, so small that it did not show when Arwen was working, grinding and measuring and pouring with the same slow sureness as always. It showed only when she was still. When her hands lay folded on a table, as they did now, and could not hide.

Arwen saw her see it. She did not move her hands.

“You don’t have to answer her,” she said. “Not today. Not ever, if you don’t want to.”

“I know.”

“But you should answer yourself.” Arwen’s green eyes were steady and very old. “That’s the only one who matters. Not Hessa. Not Eldric. Not me.” A pause. “Not even him.”

Lyora looked at her grandmother’s trembling hands on the table between them.

“I know,” she said again.

---

Eldric found her that evening at the river.

She had gone to the old stone embankment below the shrine, where the lanterns floated on Devotional nights, and sat there with her knees drawn up, watching the water go past in the long gold light. She did not hear him come. He sat down a few paces off, on the warm stone, the way he had learned to — not too close — and for a while he said nothing either.

Then: “Ma came to see you.”

She nodded.

“I told her not to.” He picked up a pebble and turned it in his fingers. “I *told* her. She went anyway. I’m sorry, Lyora.”

“Don’t be. She loves you.”

“She loves me very loudly.” He threw the pebble. It skipped twice and sank. “What did she say?”

“That you want a family. That you’ll be thirty in the spring. That you’d never leave me.” She kept her eyes on the water. “Not for anything in the world.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“Well,” he said. “She’s not wrong.”

The river went by. A heron lifted out of the reeds on the far bank, slow and grey, and flapped away downstream into the gold.

“I’m waiting,” said Lyora.

She said it very simply. She did not look at him.

“I know you are,” said Eldric.

“It’s been a year. There haven’t been any letters. A soldier came through in the spring and said he was alive, at a post in the north, and that was a long time ago, and I don’t know—” Her voice caught, very slightly. “I don’t know anything. I don’t know if he’s alive. I don’t know if he remembers the way here. I don’t know if there’s anything to wait *for*.” She drew a breath. “But I’m waiting. I don’t know how not to. I’m sorry. I know it isn’t fair.”

Eldric looked at her for a long time. At her profile against the gold water. At her hands, clasped around her knees.

Then he looked back at the river.

“Then I’ll wait a little too,” he said.

She turned her head.

“Not for you,” he said quickly. “I mean — not *at* you. I won’t hang about your door, or send Ma, or—” He rubbed the back of his neck, the way he did when words were difficult. “I just mean. I’m not in a hurry. The fields don’t care if I’m married. Da doesn’t, really, whatever Ma says. And I’d rather—” He stopped. “I’d rather wait and see how the story ends. That’s all. Before I start another one.”

Lyora looked at him. At his broad sunburnt face, and his honest eyes, and the straw still caught in his hair from the day’s work.

“You’re a good man, Eldric,” she said quietly.

“So everyone keeps telling me.” He smiled, crooked. “It never seems to get me anywhere.”

She laughed. She did not mean to. It came out of her, surprised and wet and helpless, and he laughed too, and for a moment they sat side by side on the warm stone above the river and laughed like children, the way they had laughed once, long ago, picking herbs in the forest when they were small.

Then it faded, and they sat in the quiet, and watched the river go gold, and then rose, and then grey.

---

When she got home, it was dark. Arwen had already gone up to bed.

The lamp was not lit.

Lyora stopped at the foot of the stairs. She stood there for a moment, looking at the dark second step, the empty place where the lamp should have been. Her grandmother had always lit it, the nights Lyora was late. Every time. All year.

She went and found it on the kitchen shelf, where it lived in the daytime. It was full; the wick was trimmed. Beside it lay the flint.

Arwen had tried. Lyora could see it now — the little scratches on the flint, the scatter of tiny black specks on the shelf where sparks had fallen and not caught. Her grandmother had stood here in the dark, with her trembling hands, and struck and struck and struck, and not been able to hold the flint steady enough to light it.

And then she had put it down, very neatly, beside the lamp. And gone up to bed without a word, so that Lyora would not have to see her try.

Lyora stood in the dark kitchen with the flint in her hand for a long time.

Then she lit the lamp. She turned it low. She set it on the second step, where it belonged.

And she sat down beside it, on the stair, in its small gold light — the way she had sat once, a year ago, in the rain — and put her arms around her knees, and rested her head on them, and let herself, for the first time in a year, cry.

Above her, in the dark at the top of the stairs, Arwen’s door stood open a hand’s width. It had been open all along.

Neither of them said a word.
